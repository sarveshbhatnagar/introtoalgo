Numbers to Know

A compact reference of rounded latencies, sizes, time conversions, server capacities and availability nines, each with how it gets used in an estimate.

A candidate designs a checkout page with a 200 ms budget. To show a price it calls a tax service, then a stock service, then a fraud service, one after another, and all three live in another region. Each call is a round trip of about 100 ms, so the page takes over 300 ms before it does any work of its own. Nobody computed it until the interviewer asked.

You need about twenty numbers, each right to within a factor of 3, and the habit of using them. Every number below is rounded and approximate: real values depend on hardware, distance and load. State them as assumptions.

#Latency

OperationAboutUse it to
Read from main memory100 nsShow an in-process lookup is free next to any network call.
Random read from an SSD100 µs (0.1 ms)See that in an indexed read of a few ms, the network and the database's own work cost more than the disk.
Round trip within one data center0.5 msPrice a call to a cache or another service nearby.
Round trip between zones in one region1 msPrice synchronous replication to another zone.
Indexed database read, over the network1 to 10 msCount how many reads a request can afford.
Disk seek on a spinning drive10 msExplain why spinning disks suit sequential logs and backups, not random reads.
Round trip between regions or continents50 to 150 ms; use 100Keep cross-region calls off the request path.

The ratios matter more than the values. A same-region call is about 5,000 memory reads; a cross-region call is about 200 same-region calls. Step through one request and watch where the time goes.

#Sizes and throughput

  • Small values: an ASCII character 1 byte, an integer or timestamp 8 bytes, a UUID 16 bytes, a typical metadata row 100 bytes to 1 KB.
  • Media: a compressed photo a few hundred KB to a few MB. Video at 5 Mbit/s is 5 ÷ 8 × 60 ≈ 40 MB a minute.
  • Powers of two: 210 ≈ a thousand, 220 ≈ a million, 230 ≈ a billion. 232 ≈ 4.3 billion is where 32-bit IDs run out, so use 64-bit IDs for anything that could pass a few billion rows. The estimation lesson has the full table.
  • Throughput: memory about 10 GB/s, SSD about 1 GB/s, a 10 Gbit/s network link about 1.25 GB/s. Use these to price copies: rebuilding a replica, a backup, a reshard.
Estimate: Rebuilding a replica

A database node dies. How long does copying its 1 TB to a new node take, if the network is the limit?

You know
  • 1 TB of data
  • A 10 Gbit/s link, fully used
  • 1 byte = 8 bits

Show the working
  1. 10 Gbit/s ÷ 8 = 1.25 GB/s
  2. 1012 B ÷ 1.25 × 109 B/s = 800 s
  3. 800 s ÷ 60 ≈ 13 minutes

Estimate 13 minutes (anything from 5 minutes to 40 minutes is a good answer)

For those 13 minutes or more, that partition has one fewer copy. It is why you keep three, not two.

#Time and capacity

A day is 86,400 seconds, about 105. A month is about 2.6 million seconds, a year about 31.5 million. So 1 million events a day is about 12 a second; call it 10 and say you rounded down.

Single-server capacities vary by ten times or more with hardware and query shape, so treat these as orders of magnitude to state and check with the interviewer:

  • A stateless API server: about 1,000 requests a second for typical work.
  • A relational database primary: a few thousand writes a second; reads by index into the tens of thousands.
  • An in-memory cache node: about 100,000 simple reads a second.
  • Open connections on one server (WebSockets): tens of thousands, up to about 100,000 mostly idle ones.
  • One large machine: hundreds of GB of memory and a few TB of SSD. If your data set fits, say so: one machine is the simplest design.

#Availability

TargetDown per 30-day monthDown per year
99%7.2 hours3.7 days
99.9%43 minutes8.8 hours
99.99%4.3 minutes53 minutes
99.999%26 seconds5.3 minutes

Use it two ways. Parts that must all work multiply: three services at 99.9% each give 0.9993 ≈ 99.7%, about 2 hours down a month. Redundant copies, where any one is enough, multiply their downtime instead: two copies at 99% give 1 − 0.012 = 99.99%, assuming they fail independently, which shared power or a shared bad deploy can break. More in reliability and failure.

Estimate: A downtime budget

The target is 99.95% availability. How much downtime does that allow in a 30-day month?

You know
  • 30 days × 24 hours × 60 minutes = 43,200 minutes
  • Allowed downtime is 100% − 99.95% = 0.05%

Show the working
  1. 43,200 × 0.0005 = 21.6 minutes

Estimate 22 minutes (anything from 10 minutes to 45 minutes is a good answer)

A single failover that takes 5 minutes uses almost a quarter of the month's budget, so failover must be automatic.

#In the interview

Bring a number in whenever a decision rests on speed, size or uptime, and finish with "so":

  • "A cross-region call is about 100 ms, so it can't be on the checkout path."
  • "50 million users at 1 KB each is 50 GB, so the sessions fit in one cache node's memory, with a replica."
  • "99.99% is about 4 minutes a month, so no manual step can be in failover."

Follow-ups you should expect:

  • "Why can't cross-region be faster?" Light in fibre covers about 200 km per ms. New York to London is about 5,600 km, so a round trip takes at least 2 × 5,600 ÷ 200 = 56 ms before any routing.
  • "Where does 1,000 requests a second per server come from?" "It's an assumption for typical work. I'd load test, and the design doesn't change if it's 3,000."

#Common mistakes

Adding calls that could run in parallel

Three independent 100 ms calls made at once take about 100 ms, not 300. Before you add latencies, say which calls depend on each other.

Quoting numbers as exact facts

"An SSD read is 87 µs" invites a debate you cannot win. Say "about 0.1 ms" and that it varies by hardware. The order of magnitude carries the decision.

Quick check

A page makes 4 sequential calls to a service in another region, and each needs one round trip. Roughly how long does the page spend waiting?

  1. Not quite. That uses the same-data-center figure of 0.5 ms.
  2. Right. 4 × 100 ms. Sequential calls add up.
  3. Not quite. That would be true only if the calls ran in parallel.

A request needs three services in a chain, each 99.9% available. About how available is the request?

  1. Not quite. Each one can fail on its own, so the chain is less available than any part.
  2. Right. 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 ≈ 0.997, about 2 hours down a month.
  3. Not quite. That is how redundant copies combine, not parts in a chain.

Hot product data is read on every request by 50 app servers and changes a few times a day. Where should it live?

  1. Not quite. That spends 1 to 10 ms on every request for data that barely changes.
  2. Right. One copy for all servers, fast, and easy to invalidate a few times a day. A copy in each server's memory, refreshed every minute, would also work for data this stable.
  3. Not quite. About 100 ms per read, and it fails when the link does.
