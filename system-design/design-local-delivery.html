Design Local Delivery

Design one-hour delivery from small local warehouses: mapping customers to centres by travel time, fast availability reads, and orders that never oversell.

It is 9 pm and you are out of milk. You open an app, see only what can reach you within the hour, add milk and snacks, and pay. A driver brings them from a small warehouse a few kilometres away, one of hundreds the company runs across the country. A service such as Gopuff, delivering from its own local warehouses, is the kind of product this question means.

Interviewers like it because it mixes three skills. "Near me" means travel time, not distance. The catalogue page must read stock fast across several warehouses. And the order must take the last carton of milk exactly once, across a whole basket of items. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design. Try each "Your turn" box before reading on.

#Clarify the problem

  • Functional: given a delivery address, show products that can be delivered within the hour, with availability; place an order for a basket of items; reject or adjust an order if an item just sold out.
  • Non-functional: catalogue pages in under 200 ms; availability at most a few seconds stale; never oversell (a confirmed order always has its stock reserved); a failure in one region does not stop orders in the others.
  • Out of scope: driver dispatch and tracking (see Design Ride Sharing), picking and packing, restocking, pricing.

#Estimate the scale

  • Catalogue pages: say 20 page loads per daily user: 5 million × 20 = 100 million a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 1,200 a second. Evenings are about 4 times busier: about 4,600 a second at peak.
  • Orders: 1 million a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 12 a second, about 46 at peak. Spread over 500 centres, the average centre takes one order every 10 seconds even at peak.
  • Inventory: 500 centres × 5,000 products = 2.5 million rows. At about 100 bytes each that is 250 MB: it fits in the memory of any server.

Your turn: Define the API

Write the catalogue and order endpoints. Who chooses the centre, the app or the server? What does the order return when one item has just sold out?

Compare with a model answer

The app sends the address; the server chooses the centre, both when browsing and when ordering. A sold-out item returns 409 with what is missing and the alternatives, so the customer can fix the basket in one tap.

GET /orders/{id}

The decision worth arguing is that the server picks the centre. If the app sent a centre id, an old app would order from a centre that has since closed, or one that is now 45 minutes away in traffic. Sending the address lets the server apply today's travel times and today's centre status. The order carries an idempotency key, so a retry after a timeout cannot create a second order.

Your turn: Model the data

Sketch the records for centres, stock and orders. How would you partition stock so that an order for 15 items is one local transaction?

Compare with a model answer

Stock rows keyed by (centre, product) with on-hand and reserved counts, in a relational database partitioned by region. An order ships from one centre, a centre belongs to one region, so every order's rows sit on one partition.

#Model the data

Records
centres
  centre_id PK, region_id, lat, lng, status    -- open | paused | closed
 
cell_centres                         -- precomputed, key-value
  cell        CHAR(6)                -- geohash of a ~0.75 km² area
  time_bucket SMALLINT               -- morning | midday | evening rush | night
  centres     JSON                   -- [["C1", 12], ["C2", 24]]: minutes away
  PRIMARY KEY (cell, time_bucket)
 
inventory                            -- partitioned by region_id
  region_id, centre_id, product_id
  on_hand     INT                    -- physically on the shelves
  reserved    INT                    -- promised to confirmed orders
  PRIMARY KEY (centre_id, product_id)
  CHECK (reserved <= on_hand)
 
orders                               -- same regional partition as inventory
  order_id PK, idempotency_key UNIQUE, region_id, customer_id, centre_id,
  status,     -- reserved | paid | picking | out | delivered | cancelled
  items JSON, total_cents, created_at

Available stock is on_hand - reserved. Keeping two counts means an order reserves at checkout and the shelf count only drops when a picker scans the item, so the warehouse's numbers always match what is physically there.

An order changes several rows that must all succeed together, so inventory goes in a relational database with transactions. Partition it by region, a metro area with a few dozen centres, because an order touches one centre and so one region: no transaction ever crosses partitions. Orders live on the same partition, so the order row commits with its reservations. Partitioning by product would spread every basket over many partitions and need a distributed transaction for each order. Product names, photos and prices change rarely and go through a cache and a CDN.

Your turn: High-level design

Sketch the read path for a catalogue page and the write path for an order. Where does each get stock from?

Compare with a model answer

An availability service answers catalogue pages from an in-memory copy of stock, fed by the inventory database's change stream. An order service runs the reservation transaction on the region's inventory partition. Both use the precomputed cell-to-centres table.

#High-level design

The read side serves 460,000 lookups a second from a copy; the write side takes 46 orders a second against the truth. Neither slows the other.

Step through Ana browsing and ordering the last carton of oat milk at her nearest centre, while Ben tries to buy the same carton.

#Deep dives

Interviewers push on three things: which centres can reach a customer, reading stock fast across them, and reserving a basket without overselling.

#Mapping a customer to centres by travel time

A centre 3 km away across a river with one bridge may be 30 minutes out; one 6 km away along a highway may be 10. The promise is about time, so "near" must be travel time. Say picking and handover take about 15 minutes; a 30-minute driving limit then leaves a margin inside the hour.

The arithmetic shows the table is small. A precision-6 geohash cell is about 1.2 km × 0.6 km ≈ 0.75 km² near the equator (smaller further from it; see Design Proximity Search). Say each centre serves about 50 km², roughly a 4 km radius in city traffic (a scenario assumption). Then 500 centres cover at most 25,000 km², about 25,000 ÷ 0.75 ≈ 33,000 cells. At 100 bytes per cell that is 3.3 MB. With 3 centres per cell and 4 time buckets (morning, midday, evening rush, night), a nightly rebuild is 33,000 × 3 × 4 ≈ 400,000 route calculations, a modest batch job.

Live conditions go on top as flags read at request time, not as rebuilds: a centre marked paused because its pickers are overwhelmed, or closed for the night, is skipped. That gives the team a lever during a rush without recomputing anything.

#A fast availability read across centres

A catalogue page needs, for 50 products, "can I get this within the hour?". That is the best of the customer's 2 or 3 centres for each product. The truth is in the inventory database, but 460,000 lookups a second should not land on the database that takes orders.

My pick.

Each availability server loads a snapshot of all stock at start-up, noting the stream position it corresponds to, then applies deltas from that position on. That is the same idea as a database replica, built from the change stream. Two more rules make the copy useful. For products whose shelf counts often drift with breakage and mis-scans, show them as available only above a small safety buffer, so you rarely promise a unit that is not there. And never show a count ("only 2 left") unless you accept it will sometimes be wrong for a second.

#Ordering without overselling

A basket of 15 items must reserve all 15 or none. If 14 succeed and 1 fails, the customer is charged for a bag with a hole in it, or the 14 are locked away from other customers by a half-finished order.

Reserve a basket at one centre, all or nothing
BEGIN;
UPDATE inventory AS i
   SET reserved = i.reserved + b.qty
  FROM (VALUES ('p_17', 1), ('p_42', 2), ...) AS b(product_id, qty)  -- 15 rows
 WHERE i.centre_id = 'C1' AND i.product_id = b.product_id
   AND i.on_hand - i.reserved >= b.qty;
-- rows changed = 15: INSERT the order, COMMIT.
-- fewer than 15:    ROLLBACK, and report which products fell short (409).
COMMIT;

Why it is safe: each row the update changes stays locked until commit. A second order for the same product waits, then, at read committed in databases such as PostgreSQL, re-checks its WHERE clause against the new values, finds too little stock, and skips the row; the count comes up short and it rolls back. At stricter isolation it fails with a serialization error, which you treat the same way (see transactions and isolation). Two baskets sharing products can lock them in different orders and deadlock; the database aborts one, and the order service retries it once. The CHECK (reserved <= on_hand) constraint is a last line of defence against a bug elsewhere.

Deciding from the catalogue copy

The availability copy said oat milk was in stock for both Ana and Ben. If the order service trusted it and simply decremented, C1 would promise one carton twice. The copy decides what to show; only the conditional update decides what to sell.

The rest of the flow follows ticket booking: reserve first, then authorize the card, then confirm. A reservation whose payment never completes expires after a few minutes: a sweeper moves the order from reserved to cancelled only if it is still reserved, and releases its stock in the same transaction. Before cancelling, it asks the payment processor by idempotency key, in case the card was authorized just before a crash. When a picker finds a shelf empty despite the count, the order offers a substitute or a refund for that item and the count is corrected; no transaction can fix a miscounted shelf.

#Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x, 46,000 page loads and 460 orders a second at peak, the availability servers scale by adding copies, since each holds everything. The order side is 460 ÷ 500 ≈ 1 order a second per centre, still easy for a region's partition. The first strain is a hot product: a promotion on one item sends many orders to the same few rows at one centre, and they queue on its row lock. Cap the quantity per order, and if needed split the item's stock into several rows that orders pick at random, as in the general-admission answer of ticket booking.

#What a strong answer sounds like

LevelWhat the answer does
JuniorFinds centres by straight-line distance, reads stock from the main database on every page, and decrements quantities one item at a time.
MidUses travel time, caches availability, and reserves the basket in one transaction with a conditional update.
SeniorPrecomputes travel time per map cell, sees that all stock fits in memory and copies it into the read servers from a change stream, partitions inventory by region so every order is a local transaction, and separates the copy that shows from the truth that sells.

#Follow-up questions

#No single centre has the whole basket. What then?

Offer the choices in the 409: move the whole order to the farther centre with a later time, drop the missing items, or split into two deliveries for an extra fee. Splitting means two reservations on two centres in the same region, each its own transaction, with the second released if the first fails.

#How do you show a delivery time on the page?

From the cell table's minutes for the chosen centre, plus the current picking backlog at that centre, which the order service publishes every few seconds.

#A customer is right at the edge of a centre's area. Do they see it?

Only if their cell's travel time is under the limit. Cells are about 0.75 km², so the edge is fuzzy by a few hundred metres; add a small margin to the limit rather than computing routes per address.

#How do you handle restocking deliveries?

A delivery to a centre is a transaction that adds to on_hand; the change stream carries it to the copies, and products reappear on pages within seconds.

#Could you reserve stock when an item goes into the cart?

It would stop disappointments at checkout, but abandoned carts would hide stock for minutes from customers ready to pay. Reserve at checkout, and warn in the cart when the copy shows an item has run out.
