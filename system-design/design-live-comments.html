Design Live Comments

Show comments on a live video to millions of viewers within seconds: SSE fan-out, pub/sub per video, sampling hot streams, history and ordering.

A cup final is streaming live to 5 million people. A goal goes in, and thousands type "GOAL" in the same second. Each viewer should see a lively stream of comments within a second or two, and someone joining at half time should see what was just said.

Writes are small. The hard part is reads: one comment may have to reach millions of connections on a thousand servers, fast. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design; try each "Your turn" box before you read on.

#Clarify the problem

  • Functional: post a comment; see new comments live; see recent ones on joining; scroll back.
  • Non-functional: shown within 2 seconds; every comment stored durably; up to 5 million viewers on one video; no duplicates on screen; a failing panel never affects the video.
  • Out of scope: replies, likes, recordings, moderation tooling beyond a basic filter.

#Estimate the scale

  • Comments at peak: 50 million viewers ÷ 600 seconds ≈ 83,000 comments a second.
  • On the biggest video: 5 million ÷ 600 ≈ 8,300 a second.
  • Daily average: 2 billion ÷ 86,400 ≈ 23,000 a second; peak is three to four times that.
  • Storage: 2 billion × 200 bytes = 400 GB a day, about 146 TB a year before replication.
Estimate: Naive fan-out on the biggest video

If every comment on the biggest video went to every viewer, how many deliveries per second is that?

You know
  • 5 million viewers on the video
  • Each viewer comments once every 10 minutes

Show the working
  1. Comments: 5,000,000 ÷ 600 s ≈ 8,300 a second
  2. Deliveries: 8,300 × 5,000,000 ≈ 4 × 1010, about 40 billion a second

Estimate 40 billion/s (anything from 10 billion/s to 100 billion/s is a good answer)

Nobody can read 8,300 comments a second. Cap what is shown and the problem shrinks by orders of magnitude.

Show at most 10 comments a second per video, and send each viewer one batch every 500 ms. The biggest video then costs 5 million × 2 = 10 million small messages a second over 1,000 servers: 10,000 each, no longer tied to how fast people type.

Estimate: Realtime servers needed

How many realtime servers hold the open connections at peak?

You know
  • 50 million viewers at peak, one connection each
  • Assume 50,000 connections per server (load test it)

Show the working
  1. 50,000,000 ÷ 50,000 = 1,000 servers
  2. Add spare capacity for a dying server's viewers

Estimate 1000 (anything from 300 to 3000 is a good answer)

Your turn: Define the API

Write the calls to post, receive new comments, and load older ones. Which transport carries new comments?

Compare with a model answer

Posting is plain HTTP. New comments flow down a server-sent events stream that resumes from the last event id. Older comments are a paged read with a cursor.

  200 { "comments": [...], "next_before": "7101..." }

The decision worth arguing is the transport. Viewers almost only listen, so use server-sent events rather than WebSockets: plain HTTP, proxy-friendly, and browsers reconnect on their own with the last event id. Rare posts use an ordinary POST with normal authentication and rate limiting. A retry with the same request_id returns the first comment instead of a duplicate. Realtime communication compares the options.

History pages with a cursor (before a comment id), not an offset: at 8,300 new comments a second, "page 2" changes every time you ask.

Your turn: Model the data

What is a comment's key, and how do you read "the 50 before this one" on a video getting thousands a second?

Compare with a model answer

Partition by video plus a time bucket, sorted by a time-ordered comment id. History is one range scan, newest first.

#Model the data

Records
comments                       -- wide-column store, about 200 B a row
  video_id     BIGINT
  bucket       INT             -- time bucket, sized per video
  comment_id   BIGINT          -- time-ordered 64-bit id
  user_id      BIGINT
  text         VARCHAR(200)
  status       TINYINT         -- visible | removed
  PRIMARY KEY ((video_id, bucket), comment_id DESC)
 
recent:{video_id}              -- in-memory list, last 200 shown
  [ { seq, comment_id, user, text }, ... ]

Writes append, reads are "newest N before an id", nothing joins. A wide-column store such as Cassandra fits: built for heavy writes, and the time-ordered id gives newest-first order for free.

The bucket bounds partitions. Quiet streams use one-hour buckets. The biggest video would put 8,300 × 3,600 ≈ 30 million rows, 6 GB, in an hour, so busy videos use one-minute buckets (500,000 rows, 100 MB). Store the bucket size on the video so readers can walk back.

Your turn: High-level design

Sketch the path from a POST to storage and on to 5 million connections. How does a comment find its viewers' servers?

Compare with a model answer

Store it and append it to a stream partitioned by video. A fan-out worker per partition samples busy videos and publishes to a pub/sub channel per video, which every realtime server with a viewer of it subscribes to.

#High-level design

Writes and fan-out are split: the comment service makes a comment durable; the fan-out path decides what is shown and where. Realtime servers only hold connections.

#Deep dives

#Fan-out: which server holds which viewer

Viewers land on any of 1,000 realtime servers. A comment on video 77 must reach exactly the servers holding viewers of 77.

The pub/sub layer is the registry: each channel lives on one pub/sub node (by hashing the video id), which knows its subscribers. A dead server's subscriptions vanish with its connection. The biggest video is subscribed by all 1,000 servers, fine at 10 comments a second: 10 × 1,000 = 10,000 messages a second from one node. Unsampled it would be 8,300 × 1,000 ≈ 8.3 million.

Each realtime server then batches 500 ms of comments into one SSE event per viewer, well inside the 2-second goal.

#Very hot videos: sample, do not drop

Sample at the fan-out worker: the stream is partitioned by video id, so exactly one worker sees all of a video's comments and can count its rate.

Handling a video with 8,300 comments a second

How the worker samples: below 10 a second, everything passes. Above it, keep the creator's and moderators' comments, drop exact repeats ("GOAL" ×3,000 is noise), and fill the rest with a random pick from each 100 ms window. Posters always see their own comment locally. The cost: on the biggest stream, almost everything is stored but never broadcast.

Joins spike too: 1 million in a minute is 1,000,000 ÷ 60 ≈ 17,000 reads a second of one key. Each realtime server reads the recent list at most once a second and serves all its joiners from that copy, so the cache sees at most 1,000 reads a second.

#Ordering, duplicates and history

Comment ids come from servers whose clocks differ by milliseconds, so they are only roughly ordered. Live order comes from the video's one worker, which numbers comments 4,121, 4,122, and so on. With a pub/sub that keeps one publisher's messages on a channel in order (most do over a single connection), every viewer sees the same sequence. History, sorted by comment id, can differ by a few milliseconds; nobody notices.

  • Gaps: pub/sub stores nothing, so a restarting server misses messages. Reconnects fill the gap from the recent list via Last-Event-ID; past the 200 kept (20 seconds at 10 a second), the client skips ahead.
  • Lost broadcast: a comment service dying between insert and stream append leaves a comment stored but never shown, which "a sample is fine" allows.
  • Duplicates: a restarted worker replays from its last committed position, so a comment can arrive twice. Clients drop ids already shown; "never twice" comes from that check.

Older history is rare, so it reads the store directly: before an id, newest first, walking back through buckets.

Polling for new comments

5 million viewers polling every 2 seconds is 2.5 million requests a second for one video, nearly all returning the same few comments. Push over long-lived connections, and batch.

#Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x (500 million viewers, 50 million on one stream):

  • Realtime tier: 10,000 servers. Outbound bandwidth strains first: delivery cost grows with viewers, not comments.
  • Pub/sub: the hottest channel sends 10 × 10,000 = 100,000 messages a second. Add a few dozen relays between the node and the servers.
  • Storage: 4 TB a day; add nodes. The hottest video's 83,000 writes a second hit one partition, so add a shard number 0 to 15 to its key and merge the 16 on reads.

Sending comments from the comment service to viewers directly

The comment service does not know where viewers are. Asking every realtime server "any viewers of 77?" per comment is the firehose in disguise. Let subscriptions record where viewers are.

#What a strong answer sounds like

LevelWhat the answer does
JuniorStores comments; clients poll or hold a WebSocket. With help, sees sender and viewers sit on different servers.
MidPicks SSE or WebSockets with a reason, uses a pub/sub channel per video, pages history with a cursor.
SeniorComputes the naive fan-out, agrees a display cap, samples at a per-video worker, says where ordering and no-duplicate guarantees come from, and isolates the panel from the video.

#Follow-up questions

#How would comments replay with the recording?

Store each comment's offset into the video. During playback the client fetches the next minute's comments by offset and shows each at its moment: a cacheable read, not a realtime problem.

#A moderator removes a comment that was already shown. What happens?

Mark it removed and publish a small "remove 7193..." event on the video's channel. Clients delete it, and the recent list drops it.

#How would you add live heart reactions?

Nobody needs each reaction. Count them per video per second at the fan-out worker and broadcast only the counts.

#How would you run this across regions?

Give each region its own realtime servers and pub/sub. A video's worker publishes once per region, so a comment crosses an ocean once, not once per viewer.

#How do you stop spam and bots?

Rate-limit per account and IP, require verified accounts on big streams, and filter text before publishing. Sampling also mutes floods: 1,000 copies of one link are one repeat.
