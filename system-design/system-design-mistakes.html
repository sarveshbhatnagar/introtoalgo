System Design Mistakes

The habits that most often fail system design interviews, what the interviewer sees when each one happens, and how to avoid it.

Most candidates who fail a system design interview fail because of how they work, not because they lack knowledge. The interviewer scores what they can observe: whether you scope the problem, whether the design matches the scale, whether you explain your decisions, and whether you handle failures and pushback well. This lesson lists the habits that cost candidates the most, grouped by the point in the interview where they happen, and says what to do instead.

Each of these habits leaves the interviewer without evidence. If you never ask about scale, they cannot tell whether you would have chosen the design for the right reason. If you draw in silence, they cannot tell what you considered and rejected. A candidate who knows the material can still receive a weak score for this reason.

#Mistakes before you start drawing

#Skipping requirements

The most common mistake is putting the first box on the board right after hearing the prompt. The interviewer sees that you did not scope the problem, and you have no facts to justify any later decision. Spend the first few minutes asking about the features that are in scope, the expected scale and what the system must prioritise, such as low latency or never losing data, and write the answers where the interviewer can see them. The questions to ask are in Requirements and Scope.

#Designing for a scale you did not establish

Some candidates add sharding, a stream processor and several caches to a system that serves a few hundred users. Every extra component is something you must justify, and for a small system there is no justification. Let the requirements decide: say that the load fits on one database with a replica, and name the number at which you would start sharding. Knowing when not to scale a system is a strong signal, because it shows that you choose components for a reason.

#Giving no numbers

A statement such as "this is a lot of traffic, so we need caching" gives the interviewer nothing to check. Spend about three minutes on an estimate of peak requests per second, storage and one more figure that matters for this problem, and end each calculation with a decision, for example "this is 3,000 reads per second, which is too much for one database, so a cache goes in front of it". Estimation is covered in Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation, and the reference figures are in Numbers to Know.

#Reciting a memorised design

Interviewers can tell when an answer comes from a video rather than from the question in front of them. A typical case is a request for a chat app for hospital staff that receives a consumer chat design with global fan-out and no mention of message retention or access control. Reuse patterns, not whole answers, and say which requirement of this prompt makes it different from the one you studied.

#Mistakes while you design

#Drawing in silence

The interviewer scores what they hear, and a design that appears without explanation shows little of your reasoning. Narrate each decision, including the options you rejected, for example: "I considered a queue here, but the user needs the result immediately, so this is a direct call."

#Listing technology names instead of reasons

Saying "Kafka in front, Redis beside it, Cassandra behind, all on Kubernetes" gives the interviewer four claims to test and no reasoning. Name the category and the reason, for example "a durable log, because three consumers read the same events", and add a component only when a requirement needs it. If you do name a product, be ready to explain how it behaves. Talking Through Trade-offs shows how to phrase these decisions.

#Going deep too early

Spending fifteen minutes on the perfect ID scheme while the read path does not yet reach a database leaves no time for the parts the interviewer cares about. Get a simple design working from the client to the data store and back by roughly the middle of the interview, then go deep where the interviewer points or where the problem is hardest.

#Not driving

Finishing each part and asking "what should I do next?" hands the interview to the interviewer. From mid level upwards, interviewers score whether you lead. State a plan at the start, and at each pause propose the next step: "The hard part here is the hot-key problem, so I would go there next, unless you would rather see the storage design."

#Ignoring failure

A design that only works while every box is up is incomplete. Once the main design is drawn, go through the components one by one and say what happens when each one fails, including how much load the survivors receive. #Mistakes when the interviewer pushes back

#Defending a choice after a hint

A question such as "is a database write on every click a problem?" is rarely neutral. Interviewers use questions to point at a flaw they have noticed. If you answer "no, databases are fast", you have defended a weak spot and shown that you do not listen. Assume the question points at a real problem, say what breaks and at what load, and then change the design.

#Promising guarantees you cannot keep

Claims such as "the queue gives us exactly-once delivery" are a common trap. When an acknowledgement is lost, the sender cannot know whether the message arrived, so it must either resend, which can deliver the message twice, or give up, which can lose it. Real systems provide at-least-once delivery and use an idempotent consumer, so that a repeated message has no additional effect. The result is exactly-once processing, not exactly-once delivery. Name the real guarantee and the mechanism, as described in Idempotency and Retries.

#Skipping over the hard part

In a seat-booking design, saying "the database handles the concurrency" skips the one place where the interviewer expected depth. At the part that makes the problem hard, slow down. Name the race, for example two users buying the last seat at the same moment, show the rows involved, and say which lock or conditional update prevents it.

#Treating a flaw you notice as fatal

Sometimes you see a flaw in your own design at minute 30. You have three options. You can keep going and hope the interviewer did not notice, which is wrong for a real flaw, because if they did notice they now think you did not. You can start over, which costs ten minutes and the time you needed for depth, so it is only reasonable early in the interview and only if the flaw breaks a core requirement. Or you can name the flaw, patch it and continue, which takes a minute and shows the self-review that interviewers score. The third option is almost always right.

#Which steps to do every time and which to do only when asked

An interviewer expects four things in every interview: clarifying requirements, a rough estimate of the load, a high-level design that works end to end, and a detailed look at the hardest part, followed by a discussion of failures. Detailed API design and detailed schema design are worth doing only when the interviewer asks for them or when the problem depends on them, and a short sketch of each is enough otherwise. If you are short of time, shorten the optional steps and keep the four required ones. A Framework for Any Design gives the full order.

#In the interview

You cannot practise all these habits at once, so pick the three that you tend to fall into and rehearse the sentence that prevents each one, for example "Before I draw, a few questions about features and scale", "Next I would go into the hardest part, which here is double bookings" and "Let me go through each component and say what happens when it fails".

Questions to expect:

  • "How long should you spend on requirements?" Spend about five minutes in a 45-minute interview. Ask about features, scale and priorities, state your assumptions aloud and confirm them. Going on for longer takes time from the design, and stopping earlier leaves you with nothing to justify your decisions.
  • "What happens if this grows a hundred times?" Work out the new load with arithmetic before answering. If it is still small, say that the design holds and name what you would monitor. If not, say which component breaks first and how you would scale it.
  • "What are you least sure about in this design?" Name your weakest part, say why you are unsure and say how you would test it. Answering that you are sure about everything suggests that you did not look for flaws.
  • "Why did you choose this component?" Give the requirement that needs it and the cost it brings. If you cannot connect the component to a requirement, remove it from the design instead of defending it.
  • "What happens if the primary database fails?" Say what clients see and how long it lasts. Say whether the replica is promoted and how much recent data could be lost because replication is asynchronous, and mention synchronous replication to one copy if the requirement is no data loss.
  • "How do you show depth without running out of time?" Finish a simple end-to-end design first. Then pick the hardest part of this particular problem, announce that you are going there, and ask whether the interviewer wants a different area.
Quick check

A candidate gets a design for a service used by one office of 400 people. They propose sharding and a multi-region failover plan. What is the main problem with this answer?

  1. Not quite. Sharding is appropriate when the load needs it. The problem is that this load does not.
  2. Right. Components should follow from the estimated scale. A single database with a replica fits this load, and the candidate could name the point at which sharding would be needed.
  3. Not quite. It can be explained. It is the wrong choice here because the scale and availability requirements do not call for it.

A candidate says their queue gives exactly-once delivery. What is the accurate version?

  1. Not quite. Networks lose acknowledgements, so the sender must either resend or risk losing the message.
  2. Right. Duplicates can always arrive, and idempotent processing makes the effect happen only once.
  3. Not quite. That avoids duplicates by allowing messages to be lost, which is a different trade-off.

You notice at minute 30 that your design loses data when the primary database fails. What is usually the best response?

  1. Not quite. If the interviewer saw it, they now think you did not.
  2. Not quite. This uses most of the remaining time and loses the depth you were about to show.
  3. Right. It costs about a minute and shows the self-review that interviewers look for.
