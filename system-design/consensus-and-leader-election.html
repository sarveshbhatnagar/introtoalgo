Consensus and Leader Election

Why machines struggle to agree, what majority consensus gives you, how leases and fencing tokens stop split brain, and how much to say in an interview.

A billing scheduler runs on two machines so one can take over if the other dies. Each sends a heartbeat every second and takes over after 5 missed beats. One night the network between them drops for 10 seconds. Both decide the other is dead and both run the nightly billing job. 40,000 customers are charged twice.

Consensus lets machines agree on one answer (who leads, what was written) even when some are slow, dead or cut off.

#Why agreeing is hard

A silent machine might be dead, overloaded, paused by garbage collection, or cut off; no timeout tells these apart. Clocks drift, and messages arrive late, twice, or never.

With two machines, any rule fails. If each waits for the other's vote, one failure stops everything; if each acts alone, a partition gives two leaders (split brain).

#The idea: majorities

Use an odd number of nodes, usually 3 or 5, and require a majority for every decision. Any two majorities share a node, so two conflicting decisions can never both win. A cut-off minority cannot act.

Raft-style consensus, simplified:

  • One leader per term. Terms are numbered 1, 2, 3… Leading a term needs a majority of votes; each node votes once per term.
  • A replicated log. The leader appends each change and copies it to followers. An entry is committed once a majority stores it; only then is it applied and acknowledged.
  • Elections on silence. After a randomised timeout (a few hundred ms) of silence, a follower becomes a candidate for the next term. Nodes vote only for a candidate whose log is at least as complete as theirs, so a new leader has every committed entry.

The cost: every write waits for a majority. With 2f + 1 nodes you survive f failures: 3 survive 1, 5 survive 2. 4 still survive only 1 (a majority of 4 is 3), so even sizes buy nothing.

A subtlety: until it hears of term 4, the old leader may serve stale reads. Systems promising fresh reads confirm leadership with a majority first, or use a time-based lease.

#Leases and fencing tokens

Most systems do not run consensus themselves. They ask a consensus-backed coordination service (such as ZooKeeper or etcd) for a lease: "you lead for 10 seconds; renew before then." If the holder dies, the lease expires and someone else gets it.

A lease alone is not enough: clocks drift, and a process paused for 15 seconds wakes believing its lease is valid and writes. The fix is a fencing token, a number that grows with every grant. Storage rejects any write with a smaller number than one it has seen.

#Where it shows up

Database leader election, shard ownership, cluster membership, configuration, locks and single-worker schedulers. Keep it for small, critical metadata like this; a majority round trip per user request costs too much.

Estimate: Commit latency with five nodes

In a five-node cluster, how long does the leader wait before a write is committed?

You know
  • Round trips from the leader to the other four nodes: 30 ms, 80 ms, 90 ms, 120 ms
  • A write commits once a majority of the five have stored it

Show the working
  1. Majority of 5 = 3: the leader plus 2 followers
  2. The 2 fastest followers answer in 30 ms and 80 ms
  3. Commit after the slower: 80 ms

Estimate 80 ms (anything from 40 ms to 240 ms is a good answer)

The majority's slowest member sets latency, not the slowest node. Placing two nodes near the leader cuts it.

#In the interview

Mention consensus when something needs exactly one owner (a leader, a lock, a shard map) or you promise no lost writes on failover. Keep it to a sentence or two unless pushed; interviewers rarely want Raft on the whiteboard.

Follow-ups:

  • "What happens on the minority side of a partition?" It cannot elect a leader or commit, so it refuses writes: consistency over availability.

#Common mistakes

Heartbeats between two nodes

Two nodes deciding alone both lead during a partition. Use an odd-sized majority or a coordination service.

A lock without a fencing token

A lock cannot stop a process that paused and lost the lock without knowing. Only the resource checking a token can.

Quick check

How many node failures can a 5-node majority cluster survive and still commit writes?

  1. Not quite. That is a 3-node cluster. A majority of 5 is 3.
  2. Right. 5 − 2 = 3 remain, still a majority.
  3. Not quite. One node alone is not a majority.

A storage service has seen fencing token 52. A write arrives with token 51. What should happen?

  1. Not quite. Token 51 belongs to a replaced lease.
  2. Right. A newer leader exists; this is the paused old one.
  3. Not quite. Time checks fail with paused processes and drifting clocks.
