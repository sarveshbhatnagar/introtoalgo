Design a Digital Wallet

Hold balances and move money between users safely: a double-entry ledger, idempotent transfers, no overdrafts, and transfers across database shards.

Alice owes Bob $20 for dinner. She opens a wallet app such as Venmo and taps Send; her balance drops $20 and Bob's rises $20. Behind that tap: two balances that must change together, a double tap that must not send $40, and two phones spending the same $30 at once.

It reuses the idempotency keys and ledger rules of Design a Payment System. The difference: both sides of every transfer are accounts you hold, possibly on different database shards, so you move money between your own databases without creating or losing a cent. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design.

#Clarify the problem

  • Functional: top up from a card; send money to another user; view balance and paged history.
  • Non-functional: the rules above, even under concurrent requests; no acknowledged transfer is lost; an append-only ledger kept for 7 years; a send answers within about 500 ms; the recipient is credited within seconds.
  • Out of scope: withdrawals, currencies, fraud, spending limits, card processing (the payment system's job).

#Estimate the scale

  • Top-ups: 5 million ÷ 86,400 ≈ 60 a second, small next to transfers.
  • Balance reads: 300 million app opens a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 3,500 single-row reads a second.
  • Database writes: one relational primary safely takes about 5,000 short write transactions a second. Today's peak fits on one; 10x does not.
Estimate: Peak transfer rate

About how many transfers a second must the wallet handle at a 5x peak?

You know
  • 50 million transfers a day
  • A day is 86,400 seconds
  • Peaks run 5 times the average

Show the working
  1. 50 × 106 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 580 a second
  2. 580 × 5 ≈ 2,900, call it 3,000 a second

Estimate 3 thousand/s (anything from 1 thousand/s to 10 thousand/s is a good answer)

One primary can take that, so the first answer is a single database.

Estimate: Ledger storage per year

Each transfer writes 4 ledger entries of about 200 bytes (two on the sender's side, two on the receiver's). How much ledger data accumulates per year?

You know
  • 50 million transfers a day
  • 4 entries per transfer
  • About 200 bytes per entry
  • Entries are never deleted

Show the working
  1. 50 × 106 × 4 × 200 B = 40 GB a day
  2. 40 GB × 365 ≈ 14.6 TB a year
  3. Seven years: about 100 TB

Estimate 15 TB (anything from 5 TB to 50 TB is a good answer)

Recent months stay in the main database; closed periods move to cheaper queryable storage.

Your turn: Define the API

Write endpoints for top-up, send, balance and history. Who creates the id that makes a retried send harmless, and when?

Compare with a model answer

The app, when the user taps Send, before any network call. The server stores it as the transfer's primary key, so a retry cannot become a second transfer.

#Define the API Optional

Optional in the interview. Sketch the API only if the interviewer asks, if an API choice shapes the design, or if time permits. Otherwise say "I'll skip the detailed API unless you want it" and go to the data model.

  409 { "error": "insufficient_funds", "balance_cents": 1000 }

  404 no such recipient
 
GET /v1/wallet
  200 { "balance_cents": 1000, "as_of_entry": 99812 }
 
GET /v1/wallet/history?before=99812&limit=50
  200 { "entries": [ { "transfer_id": "t-81", "amount_cents": -2000,
                       "balance_after": 1000, "at": "..." }, ... ], "next": "99761" }

The decision worth arguing is when "sent" is true: once the money has left Alice's balance, not once it reaches Bob's. From then the transfer cannot be lost; it completes or is reversed to Alice automatically. Waiting for Bob's side adds a second database to the latency path for no safety gain. Money is always integer cents, never floating point.

Your turn: Model the data

Sketch the records. Is the balance a column or computed? Where do one transfer's ledger entries live if Alice and Bob are on different shards?

Compare with a model answer

The entries are the truth; the balance column caches their sum, updated in the same transaction. Entries live with their account, so a transfer has entries on two shards, and each half must balance on its own.

#Model the data

Per shard; every table is partitioned by wallet
wallets
  wallet_id      VARCHAR(32) PRIMARY KEY
  balance_cents  BIGINT        -- cache of SUM(ledger_entries), same transaction
  status         VARCHAR(8)    -- active | frozen
 
ledger_entries                 -- append only
  entry_id       BIGINT PRIMARY KEY
  account_id     VARCHAR(32)   -- "alice", or "transit:shard-A"
  transfer_id    VARCHAR(36)
  amount_cents   BIGINT        -- change to that account: -2000, +2000
  balance_after  BIGINT        -- cheap history and audits; NULL for transit
  INDEX (account_id, entry_id DESC)
 
transfers                      -- on the sender's shard
  transfer_id    VARCHAR(36) PRIMARY KEY
  from_wallet, to_wallet, amount_cents, request_hash
  status         VARCHAR(10)   -- sent | completed | reversed
 
applied_credits                -- on the receiver's shard
  transfer_id    VARCHAR(36) PRIMARY KEY
  outcome        VARCHAR(8)    -- credited | refused
 
outbox                         -- on the sender's shard
  transfer_id, payload, created_at

Every operation is a short all-or-nothing transaction over a few rows: a conditional debit, two ledger entries, a transfer row. Use a relational database with ACID transactions and synchronous replication, so an acknowledged transfer survives losing the primary. Past one primary, shard by wallet_id, so a wallet's balance, entries and history stay on one shard.

Your turn: High-level design

Sketch a send between wallets on different shards. What commits first, what carries the transfer to the second shard, and what if that step can never succeed?

Compare with a model answer

Debit Alice and write an outbox message in one transaction; a worker credits Bob once; a permanent refusal reverses the debit. Each step is a local, balanced transaction.

#High-level design

This is the sharded design for 10x; today, both legs are one transaction in one database. A top-up is simpler: once the payment system confirms, the wallet service credits Alice from a card-funding account, keyed by top-up id so it credits once.

#Deep dives

#Moving money across shards

With 16 shards, Bob is on Alice's shard 1 time in 16, so about 94% of transfers cross shards. Sharding by wallet makes this the normal case.

One transfer, two accounts

Pick one database while it fits, then a saga. Two details make the saga safe. First, debit first, credit second: the debit is the only step that can fail for a business reason (not enough money). After it, the credit is nearly certain, and a rare refusal (a frozen wallet) has a simple reversal. The opposite order could credit Bob and then fail to debit Alice, creating money from nothing.

Second, each leg balances on its own through a transit account per shard: Alice → transit:A, then transit:B → Bob. In flight, transit:A is +2000 and transit:B is −2000, so the books still sum to zero, and the transit accounts sum to money in flight: a precise reconciliation check.

Two traps. Every transfer touches its shard's transit account, so a cached balance row for it would be the hottest lock in the system: transit accounts get entries only. And a refusal is final: shard B stores it in applied_credits, and the reversal runs only if the transfer is still sent. Otherwise a redelivery after Dan is unfrozen would pay him after Alice got her money back.

#Preventing overdraft under concurrency

Alice has $30 and two devices each send $20 within a millisecond. If the service reads the balance, checks it in code, then writes, both see 3000 and both go through: −$10. Check and write must be one atomic step.

The debit leg on Alice's shard
BEGIN;
INSERT INTO transfers (transfer_id, from_wallet, to_wallet, amount_cents, status)
     VALUES ('t-81', 'alice', 'bob', 2000, 'sent');      -- duplicate id: stop here
UPDATE wallets SET balance_cents = balance_cents - 2000
 WHERE wallet_id = 'alice' AND balance_cents >= 2000;    -- 0 rows: ROLLBACK, 409
INSERT INTO ledger_entries ... ('alice', -2000), ('transit:shard-A', +2000);
INSERT INTO outbox (transfer_id, payload) VALUES ('t-81', '...');
COMMIT;

The conditional UPDATE locks Alice's row, so the second debit waits. In PostgreSQL at read committed, it then rechecks its WHERE against 1000 and changes nothing; at repeatable read or serializable it fails with a serialization error, and the retry sees 1000. Either way, no overdraft. Name the isolation level you rely on (see Transactions and isolation).

Checking the balance in a cache

Deciding a debit from a cached or replica balance reopens the race: two requests both see $30, and a replica may be seconds behind. Only the conditional write on the primary enforces the rule; caches are for display.

#Ledger, audit and reconciliation

The payment system's ledger rules hold: entries sum to zero, are never edited, and mistakes get reversing entries. Here the ledger is the balance, and balance_after lets anyone check any point in history without summing years of rows. Run these checks continuously and page on failure:

  • Per wallet: cached balance = latest balance_after = sum of entries. Sample all day; check every wallet nightly.
  • Per transfer: one still sent after a minute is stuck; a sweeper re-drives it from the outbox.
  • Across shards: all accounts sum to zero; transit accounts sum to money in flight.
  • Against the outside world: all wallets total the company's pooled bank account, and top-ups match the payment system's settlement records.

#Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x (30,000 transfers a second at peak) each cross-shard transfer is two local transactions: 60,000 a second. At about 5,000 per primary that is 12 primaries; use 16 for headroom. Balance reads, 35,000 a second, are one-row lookups. History can come from replicas a second behind, with the app showing its just-sent transfer as pending.

#Follow-up questions

#A merchant wallet receives 1,000 credits a second. What breaks?

Its balance row lock. Credits need no balance check, so append entries and fold them into the cached balance every second, or split the account into 10 sub-accounts summed on read.

#How would you add withdrawals to a bank?

Debit into a "payouts pending" account, then call the bank with an idempotency key. An unknown outcome stays pending until the bank's records settle it.

#How would you support several currencies?

One wallet per currency. A conversion goes through the company's account in each currency at a recorded rate, so each currency's ledger sums to zero.

#How do you prove the ledger was never edited?

Insert-only permission on the ledger table, a hash of the previous entry in each entry so any edit breaks the chain, and daily totals published where the database cannot change them.

#Why not compute the balance from entries on every read?

An active wallet has thousands of entries, and 3,500 reads a second would sum them all. The cached column is exact because it changes in the same transaction, and the nightly check proves it.
