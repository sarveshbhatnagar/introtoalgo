Design an Email Service

Receive, store, search and send mail for 100 million users: the SMTP receive path, mailboxes partitioned by user, attachments, bounces and device sync.

Someone in another company presses Send. Their mail server looks up your domain, connects, and hands over a message. A few seconds later it appears on your phone and your laptop, filed in the inbox, with spam already moved aside, and you can find it next year by typing one word. A web mail service with labels and search is the kind of product this question means.

Interviewers like it because a familiar screen hides several classic problems: a receive path that must never lose an accepted message, storage growing by petabytes, a search index per person, retries against servers you do not control, and devices that must agree. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design. Try each "Your turn" box before you read on.

#Clarify the problem

  • Functional: receive mail over SMTP; filter spam and viruses; list mail by label; open messages and attachments; send, with retries and bounces; label, mark read, archive, delete; search your own mail; sync devices.
  • Non-functional: no accepted message is ever lost (durability above everything); new mail visible within 5 seconds; inbox list in under 200 ms; search in under 500 ms; receiving stays up even when reading is degraded.
  • Out of scope: calendar, contacts, chat, ads, end-to-end encryption, IMAP (discussed as a follow-up).

#Estimate the scale

Assume an average email is 30 KB stored: 10 KB of headers and body, and one in 20 emails carries attachments averaging 400 KB, which adds 400 ÷ 20 = 20 KB on average. Peak is twice the average, since mail is spread over the world's time zones.

  • Received: 100 million × 40 = 4 billion emails a day. 4,000,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 46,000 a second, about 90,000 at peak.
  • Sent: 100 million × 5 = 500 million a day, about 5,800 a second.
  • Reads: if each user opens a list or a message 20 times a day, that is 2 billion reads a day, about 23,000 a second.
  • Inbound bandwidth: 4 billion × 30 KB = 120 TB a day, and 120 TB ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 1.4 GB/s.
  • Per user: 40 × 365 × 30 KB ≈ 440 MB a year.

Your turn: Define the API

Write the app-facing endpoints: list a label, open a message, send, change labels, search, and fetch what changed since the device last looked. What does "send" return, given that delivery to another provider can take days?

Compare with a model answer

Reads are GETs by label with a cursor; label changes are small operations. Send returns 202 Accepted: queued, not delivered. Sync is one endpoint, "changes since N".

The API decision worth arguing is sync. Re-downloading the inbox on every wake fetches 50 rows when maybe one changed, and misses label changes on older mail. Instead, a device asks a per-user change log for "everything after 81,237". It catches every kind of change, and a device offline for a week just gets more.

Label changes are operations ("add Receipts, remove INBOX"), not a new label set, so two devices editing different labels both win.

Your turn: Model the data

Sketch the records. Where do bodies and attachments live? What is the partition key, and how do you list "Inbox, newest first" quickly when a message can have many labels?

Compare with a model answer

Small metadata rows in a database partitioned by user_id; bodies and attachments in object storage. A separate per-label index row (user, label, time, message) makes label listings a single range scan, and a per-user change log feeds sync.

  user_id, label_id, received_at, message_id
  PRIMARY KEY (user_id, label_id, received_at DESC, message_id)
 
labels    (user_id, label_id, name, total, unread)
changes   (user_id, seq, message_id, kind)   PRIMARY KEY (user_id, seq)

A label is a tag, and a message can carry many. A folder is just a label with the rule "one per message", and Inbox, Sent, Spam and Trash are system labels. Archiving removes INBOX; nothing moves.

Almost every query starts with "for this user", so partition everything by user_id. A sharded relational database fits: on one shard, a delivery writes the message, label rows, counters and change-log entry in one transaction, so counts and the sync log never disagree with the mailbox. Bodies and attachments never change once written, which suits object storage.

Your turn: High-level design

Sketch the boxes for receiving a message from the internet, filing it, and showing it on a phone. When exactly do you tell the sending server "accepted"?

Compare with a model answer

SMTP servers write the raw message to object storage and a reference to a durable queue, and only then reply "accepted". Workers filter and deliver into the user's shard. Apps read through a mail API; a change stream feeds search.

#High-level design

A durable queue splits the receive path: the edge only stores the message safely, and everything slow happens after. The send path is in the deep dives.

#Deep dives

#The receive path: when to say 250

SMTP has a clear contract. 4xx means "try again later", and the sender will. 5xx means "permanently refused", and the sender's server tells its user. 250 after the body means you now own the message. So store it durably before replying, and decide where each check runs.

Where to stop bad mail
OptionGood atBad atPick it when
Reject during SMTPThe sender learns at once and you store nothing.Runs while the connection is open, so checks must be quick.Cheap, sure checks: unknown recipient, bad sender reputation, a domain policy that says reject.
Accept, then file as spam Usual defaultWorkers have time for content classifiers and virus scans. A false positive sits in Spam, where the user can rescue it.You store the spam, and the sender is never told.Content-based decisions. The usual answer for most filtering.
Accept, then bounceTells the sender, in theory.Spam forges its sender, so your bounce goes to an innocent address. That is backscatter, and it gets your servers blocklisted.Never for spam. Bounces are only for mail you cannot deliver, sent from the send path.

The pipeline is at-least-once on purpose: a sender may retry after a lost 250, and the queue may hand a message to two workers. Instead of chasing exactly-once, make the mailbox write idempotent with a dedupe key per recipient (see idempotency and retries). A message to three of your users is stored once and queued as three deliveries.

Back-pressure comes free: if the queue nears its limit, SMTP in answers 451, a temporary failure, and senders retry later. Mail is delayed, never dropped.

Replying 250 before the message is safe

Answering 250 as soon as the bytes arrive, then writing, feels faster. If the server dies in between, the sender has deleted its copy and yours is gone. Write and replicate first, then reply.

#Mailbox storage, partitioned by user

Storing mailbox metadata
OptionGood atBad atPick it when
Sharded relational, by user Usual defaultOne user lives on one shard, so a delivery updates message, labels, counts and change log in one transaction.You run the sharding: a directory from user to shard, and moving heavy users between shards.Per-user consistency matters, as here. The usual answer.
Wide-column store, partition by userSpreads 46,000 writes a second with little effort and grows by adding nodes.Writes are atomic only within one partition, so label index rows, counters and the change log can drift apart and need repair jobs.Write scale matters more than exact counts and a perfect sync log.

Map users to shards with a directory, not user_id modulo the shard count, so you can move one user without rehashing everyone (see sharding and partitioning). Metadata grows 2 TB a day, so new shards keep arriving.

Attachments are parsed out at delivery and stored by the SHA-256 hash of their bytes, so a 5 MB file sent to 200 of your users takes 5 MB, not 1,000 MB. Access always goes through a message the user owns, never by hash. Since many messages can share one object, a background sweep deletes objects nothing has referenced for a few days; the grace period protects a delivery that is reusing the object right now.

#Search, one user at a time

Every search is "my mail only". That decides how to split the index.

Index headers, body text and attachment names; a slower job adds text pulled out of documents. from:, has:attachment and label: become filters. Results are checked against the mailbox before showing, so a message deleted a second ago never appears. Search systems covers the inverted index.

#The send path: retries and bounces

Before returning 202, one transaction on the user's shard writes the message to Sent and an outbox row; a relay moves outbox rows to the queue, so a crash cannot leave mail in Sent that never leaves. The draft id is the idempotency key, so a double tap sends once. Recipients on your own service skip SMTP and go straight to the delivery workers.

  • Delivery: group recipients by domain, look up its MX record, connect over TLS, and sign with DKIM so the receiver can verify the message came from you.
  • Temporary failure (4xx, timeout, no answer): retry with growing gaps, say 5 minutes, then 15, then hourly. The SMTP standard suggests senders keep trying for at least four to five days before giving up.
  • Permanent failure (5xx, such as "no such user"), or retries used up: put a bounce naming the address and reason into the sender's inbox. Only this path makes bounces.
  • Reputation: receivers judge your sending IP addresses, so rate-limit accounts and send bulk and personal mail from separate address pools.

#Sync across devices

Each user has a counter. Every mailbox change, in its own transaction, takes the next number and writes a row to changes. Taking the number locks the counter row until commit, so changes commit in number order and a reader never skips one that commits late. A device remembers the last number it saw. IMAP has the same idea, a per-mailbox modification sequence.

  • Telling devices: an open connection for web, a mobile push for phones (see realtime communication). The push only says "something changed" and the device pulls, so a lost push only delays sync.
  • Offline edits: the device queues operations such as "mark m_9f2 read" and replays them on reconnect. Operations on different labels do not conflict; if two devices set the same flag differently, the last write wins.
  • Far behind: keep 30 days of change log. A device that asks for something older gets 410 and resyncs the recent window, such as the newest 500 messages per label.

Because a user lives on one shard, the counter is a plain column, not a distributed sequence. Design File Sync and Design a Chat App use similar cursors.

#Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x, about 460,000 messages a second and 1.2 PB a day, storage cost is the first wall. Compress bodies, store attachments once, tier old mail and enforce quotas. SMTP in and the workers are stateless and scale out behind several MX records; mailbox and search shards grow by adding more.

#Follow-up questions

#How does "undo send" work?

Put the message on the outbound queue with a delay, say 10 seconds. Undo deletes it from the queue before it is released. Once SMTP out has handed it to another provider, it cannot be recalled.

#A mailing list sends one message to 1 million of your users. What happens?

Store the body once and queue 1 million deliveries: about 22 seconds of the system's average 46,000 a second. Give bulk senders a lower-priority queue so personal mail is not stuck behind it.

#How do you stop your own users from sending spam?

Per-account rate limits, stricter for new accounts, and a spam score on outgoing mail. A hijacked account shows up as a sudden burst to strangers; suspend sending and ask the owner to verify.

#Could you add end-to-end encryption?

Yes, but the server can no longer read bodies, so server-side search and content filtering stop working. Search moves to the device and filtering relies on sender reputation.

#How would you support IMAP clients?

Add an IMAP front end that maps labels to folders and the change log to IMAP's modification sequences. The catch: a message with two labels appears in two folders, and deleting it from one should only remove that label.
