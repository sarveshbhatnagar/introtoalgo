A Framework for Any Design

Seven steps with a time budget that turn any system design prompt into a structured answer, worked end to end on a small pastebin service.

16 minLesson 2 of 6Completed

Two candidates know the same material. One hears "design a pastebin" and jumps between caching, sharding and the API. The other clarifies, estimates, then designs, and has time left for depth. The second one passes, on order alone.

A fixed order means you never skip a step, the interviewer can follow you, and no thought goes on what to do next.

#The seven steps

Every case study on this site follows these steps, with these headings. Each step produces something the next one uses: the estimates decide the data store, the access patterns decide the keys, the hard parts decide the deep dives. Minutes are for a 45-minute slot with about 40 minutes of design, as in how the interview works.

StepMinutesWhat you produce
1. Clarify the problem5Functional and non-functional requirements, and what is out of scope.
2. Estimate the scale4Requests a second (average and peak), storage, bandwidth.
3. Define the API3The main endpoints or messages, with error cases.
4. Model the data3The records, how they are looked up, and the kind of store.
5. High-level design10A diagram where the main read and write paths both work.
6. Deep dives10Two or three hard parts, each with options and a choice.
7. Bottlenecks and failures5What breaks at 10 times the load, and what happens when each part dies.

That adds up to 5 + 4 + 3 + 3 + 10 + 10 + 5 = 40 minutes. In a 60-minute slot, give most of the extra time to steps 6 and 7.

#Worked example: a pastebin

A pastebin stores a block of text and gives it a link. Here is the whole framework, at spoken length.

#1. Clarify the problem

  • Functional: create a paste (text up to 1 MB, optional expiry) and get a link; read a paste by its link.
  • Non-functional: a created paste is never lost; reads are fast (under 100 ms) and highly available; links are hard to guess.
  • Out of scope: accounts, editing, search, syntax highlighting.

Requirements and scope covers which questions to ask.

#2. Estimate the scale

Round a day to 100,000 seconds (really 86,400, so rates come out about 14% low). The estimation lesson explains the shortcuts.

  • Writes: 5 million ÷ 100,000 = 50 a second. Assume peaks of 3 times: 150.
  • Reads: 10 × 50 = 500 a second, 1,500 at peak.
  • Bandwidth out: 500 × 10 KB = 5 MB/s on average, 15 MB/s at peak. Small.
Estimate: Paste storage for one year

How much paste content is stored after one year?

You know
  • 5 million new pastes a day
  • 10 KB per paste on average
  • Ignore expiry and replication

Show the working
  1. Per day: 5 × 106 × 104 B = 5 × 1010 B = 50 GB
  2. Per year: 50 GB × 365 ≈ 18,000 GB = 18 TB

Estimate 18 TB (anything from 6 TB to 60 TB is a good answer)

18 TB a year, growing forever, is the number that shapes this design.

#3. Define the API

  413 Content Too Large   over 1 MB
 
GET /pastes/{id}
  200 OK   { "content": "...", "created_at": "...", "expires_at": "..." }
  404 Not Found   no such paste, or it has expired

#4. Model the data

The metadata is a small row per paste (about 200 bytes: id, size, timestamps, a pointer to the content), looked up only by id. The content is up to 1 MB of text that never changes. So: metadata in a key-value or relational store keyed by id, about 5 million × 200 B = 1 GB a day; content in blob storage under the key pastes/{id}.

The id is 8 random characters from 62 letters and digits: 628 ≈ 2.2 × 1014 possibilities. After five years (9 billion pastes) a new id clashes about 1 time in 24,000, so insert-if-absent and retry. That ratio cuts both ways: one random guess in 24,000 also finds some paste, so a scraper can harvest them. If that matters, use 10 characters (about 1 in 90 million) and rate-limit 404s.

#6. Deep dives

Pick the parts where the numbers are hardest. Here: where content lives, and id generation (see unique IDs).

Where paste content lives
OptionGood atBad atPick it when
In the database rowOne write, one read, one system to run.18 TB a year of large values bloats the database and slows backups.Small total data, or values of a few KB at most.
Blob storage, pointer in the row Usual defaultCheap per byte, grows without limit, built for large immutable objects.Two writes per create and two reads per miss; you must handle the gap between them.Large, immutable content that grows forever. This case.

What would change the choice: if the interviewer capped pastes at 4 KB and kept them for 30 days, the total would be about 5 million × 4 KB × 30 = 600 GB, and a single database would be simpler.

#7. Bottlenecks and failures

At 10 times the load (15,000 reads a second at peak), add paste servers, since they are stateless, and turn the cache into a cluster. Metadata then grows about 3.65 TB a year (10 GB a day), so partition it by id; random ids spread evenly. Then walk the failures: a dead cache node sends reads to the stores, the metadata database fails over to a replica, and a blob storage outage stops creates.

#In the interview

Say the plan once, then announce each transition:

  • "Before I draw anything, I want to pin down features and scale."
  • "Writes are 50 a second and storage grows 18 TB a year. That second number matters most, so I'll keep content out of the database."
  • "The design works end to end now. The two parts I'd like to go deeper on are content storage and id generation. Which interests you more?"

Follow-ups you should expect:

  • "Can we skip the estimates?" Yes, if the interviewer says so. Ask for the one number that matters ("roughly how much data?") and move on.
  • "Skip to the hard part." Go. The framework is a default order, not a script to defend.

#Common mistakes

Following the steps out loud but not using them

Saying "now estimates" and then ignoring the numbers is ritual. Each step must change something later: here the 18 TB estimate is what moved content into blob storage.

Quick check

In the pastebin, the interviewer changes the limit: pastes are at most 4 KB and expire after 30 days. With 5 million a day, about how much content is stored at any time?

  1. Not quite. That is one day of content (5 million × 4 KB). Pastes live 30 days.
  2. Right. 5 × 106 × 4 KB × 30 = 600 GB. Small enough that keeping content in the database row becomes reasonable.
  3. Not quite. That is a full year at 4 KB. Expiry caps it at 30 days.

Why does the create path write the blob before the metadata row?

  1. Not quite. The order is about what a reader can see, not speed.
  2. Right. The reverse order has a window where the row is visible and the read fails. An orphan blob is harmless and cleaned up later.
  3. Not quite. They are in different systems, so there is no shared transaction. The order is how you make the gap safe.
