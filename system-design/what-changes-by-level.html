What Changes by Level

What interviewers expect from junior, mid-level, senior and staff candidates in a system design interview, shown on one question, and how to prepare.

A system design interview is scored against the level you are applying for. The question is the same for a new graduate and for a staff engineer, but the interviewer expects different things: how much of the session you lead, how deep you go, and how much you notice beyond the boxes you drew. Knowing the bar for your level tells you where to spend the session.

Without that knowledge, candidates prepare for the wrong bar. A senior candidate who waits to be asked about failures, or a junior candidate who spends the session on rollout plans, can draw a correct design and still be scored below the level they applied for.

#What stays the same at every level

The scorecard is the same for everyone: exploring the problem, the design itself, trade-offs, depth, and communication. What changes is how much of it you do without being asked. A junior candidate is expected to reach a correct design with some guidance, while a senior candidate is expected to decide what the interview should be about. Time is spent differently as well: a junior candidate spends most of it reaching a working design, and a senior candidate spends the second half on two or three hard areas. Companies draw the lines between levels in slightly different places, so treat the descriptions below as typical, not exact.

#What is expected at junior level

A junior candidate is expected to produce a simple design that works from end to end and to use hints well. You should ask a few sensible questions about features and scale, name the basic building blocks (clients, a service, a database), and explain what each one does. The interviewer will usually steer you toward the next topic, and taking that hint and building on it is part of what is scored.

The usual weakness is not a wrong design but a design with no reasons behind it. If you add a cache or a queue, say what problem it solves. When asked what happens if the database fails, the expected answer is a replica and a failover, with a sentence on what the user sees in between. Do not try to cover every advanced topic: a clear, correct and complete simple design scores better than a long list of technologies you cannot explain.

#What is expected at mid-level

A mid-level candidate is expected to drive the structure of the session. You ask about features and scale without prompting, work out the read-to-write ratio, do a short estimate, and justify each component by pointing to a number or a requirement. When the interviewer points at one area, such as the cache, you go into it properly: what is cached, how it is invalidated, and how stale the data may be.

The usual weakness is stopping at the first workable answer. A mid-level candidate describes each component well but waits for the interviewer to ask about failures and bottlenecks. When asked, you should be able to walk through each box and say what happens if it goes down.

#What is expected at senior level

A senior candidate is expected to run the whole session. After the basic design you find the hard part of the problem yourself, go deep on two or three areas, compare options with their costs, and raise failure cases before you are asked. You also say what each choice gives up, for example that a counter which is updated asynchronously will lag behind by a second.

The usual weakness is passivity. A senior candidate who finishes the diagram and asks "is there anything you would like me to cover?" has handed the interviewer the job that the level requires the candidate to do. Choose the topic, explain why it is the hard part, and fix it.

#What is expected at staff level

A staff candidate is expected to do everything a senior candidate does and also to question the problem. That means asking which requirements are really needed, saying what should not be built, estimating what the system costs to run, and describing how it will change over time and which other teams will depend on it. A staff answer also covers how the system is rolled out, how a failure would be detected, and who would be paged.

The usual weakness is reaching for these topics too early. Ownership and migration plans at minute 10, before the main request path reaches a database, look like avoiding the work. Show a working design first, and then widen the view.

#One question answered at each level

The example question is "Design the like button for a social app, with a count under every post." Assume the app receives about 500 likes a second and that a post is viewed about 100 times for every like, which makes about 50,000 count reads a second. #Nuances interviewers probe

#The level you are interviewed for may not be the level you expect

Interviewers calibrate during the session. A strong mid-level performance in a senior interview often leads to an offer at mid-level, and a strong senior performance in a staff interview often leads to a senior offer. Ask which level the interview is for, and treat the answer as the target for what you volunteer.

#Showing seniority with jargon

Naming more technologies does not raise your level, because every name gives the interviewer something to probe. If you cannot explain why a component is there, remove it from the design.

#Closing gaps in your own design

Senior and staff candidates look for gaps in what they just drew. In the like counter, the like service can crash between inserting the like and publishing the event, which leaves a like that is stored but never counted. The fix is a periodic job that recounts popular posts from the like rows, and saying so unprompted is the kind of detail that separates the levels.

#Asking what the interviewer wants

If you do not know where to go deep, ask: "Should I focus on the overall design or go deep in one area?" This is expected at every level. What changes is that a senior candidate follows the answer with a proposal, such as "I would pick the hot post, because it is where this design breaks first."

#How to prepare for your level

Redrawing case studies from memory builds the vocabulary of components and patterns quickly, which is useful for juniors in the first weeks, but it trains recall and not judgement, so move on from it. Timed solo mocks practise pacing and structure, and they suit mid-level candidates and anyone who runs out of time, but nobody pushes back, so you never practise handling hints and curveballs. Partner mocks with someone who scores you give real interruptions and feedback on how you sound, and they are the most useful method for senior candidates and for everyone in the last two weeks. For staff, reading real design documents shows how cost, migration and ownership are argued, and it works best alongside partner mocks.

Whatever your level, practise one level up in one area, for example by raising a failure case before you are asked.

#In the interview

Ask which level the interview targets if you do not know it, and ask what they want you to focus on. Then make the moves that define your level out loud, so the interviewer can score them. For example, a mid-level move is "Reads are 100 times more frequent than writes, so I will cache counts and accept a few seconds of staleness", and a staff move is "Do we need exact counts? If the app shows 12K, an approximate count is much cheaper."

Questions to expect:

  • "How do you decide how much to do without being asked?" Do the moves of your level every time, and the moves of the next level once. For a senior candidate that means choosing the hard part yourself, going deep on it, and then raising failure before the interviewer does.
  • Mid-level: "Why did you choose that database?" State the access pattern, name the property you need, and say what you give up. The trade-offs lesson has a structure for this answer.
  • Senior: "What breaks at ten times the traffic?" Name the first bottleneck and the number at which it breaks, and then give the fix. In the like counter, the first thing that breaks is the count row of a viral post, not total throughput.
  • Senior: "What happens when this component fails?" Say what is lost, what only lags, and how the system recovers. For example, a dead aggregator freezes counts but loses no likes.
  • Staff: "How would you roll this out?" Put the new path behind a flag and write both the old and the new counts for a while. Compare them, and then switch the reads to the new count. Say how you would roll back if the numbers differ.
  • Staff: "Which requirement would you push back on?" Pick one that is expensive and ask whether it is needed, such as exact counts that the screen rounds anyway. State what the cheaper version would save and what users would notice.
  • "You have said a lot. What is the one thing you would worry about?" Choose the single most likely failure, explain why, and say how you would detect it, for instance by alerting on queue backlog.
