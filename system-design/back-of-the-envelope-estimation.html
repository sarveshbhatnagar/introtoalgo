Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation

A short set of anchors to memorize, so daily users become requests per second, storage, bandwidth and servers with no long division.

A candidate designing a photo-sharing app is told "10 million daily users, 10 requests each". They spend four minutes dividing 100 million by 86,400 to get 1,157.4 requests a second. A candidate who knows one anchor says "10 million a day is about 100 a second, times 10 is 1,000" in five seconds and moves on to the design.

Memorize the anchors on this page and scale them by 10s. Every figure is rounded: you want the right order of magnitude, then a sentence that starts "so...".

#Per day to per second

A day is 86,400 seconds; call it 100,000. That gives the anchor everything else hangs on.

The one to remember

1 million a day is about 10 a second. Every 10 times more a day is 10 times more a second.

Events a dayAbout, a second
1 million10
10 million100
100 million1,000
1 billion10,000

The exact figures are about 15% higher (1 million a day is 11.6 a second). Say once that you rounded down. For monthly numbers: 1 billion a month is about 400 a second.

#Daily users to requests per second

Multiply the anchor by how many requests each user makes a day. Find your row and column and read the average rate.

Daily users1 request each10 each100 each
1 million10/s100/s1,000/s
10 million100/s1,000/s10,000/s
100 million1,000/s10,000/s100,000/s
1 billion10,000/s100,000/s1 million/s
  • The one to keep: 10 million daily users at 10 requests each is about 1,000 a second. Scale from there: 100 requests each is 10,000 a second; so is 100 million users at 10 each.
  • Peak is about 3 times average for a consumer app. It is an assumption; say so.
  • Reads to writes is about 10 to 1 unless told otherwise. For the write rate, move one column to the left.

#Count times size

Storage, cache memory and bandwidth are all a count multiplied by a size. Three products cover most of it.

Count× 1 KB (a row, a message)× 1 MB (a photo)
1 thousand1 MB1 GB
1 million1 GB1 TB
1 billion1 TB1 PB
  • Storage per year: multiply a day's worth by about 400 (really 365). 1 million rows a day at 1 KB is 1 GB a day, about 400 GB a year. 1 million photos a day at 1 MB is 1 TB a day, about 400 TB a year.
  • Copies: durable storage keeps 3 copies, so multiply by 3. 400 TB a year becomes about 1 PB.
  • Cache memory: the same table. 10 million hot items at 1 KB is 10 GB, which fits on one cache node.
  • Bandwidth: 1,000 responses a second at 1 MB is 1 GB/s. Links are sold in bits, so multiply by 8: 8 Gbit/s.

#Servers

These capacities vary with hardware and workload, so state them as assumptions. Numbers to know has the longer list, with latencies.

One machineHandles about
Stateless API server1,000 requests a second
Relational database primarya few thousand writes a second
In-memory cache node100,000 simple reads a second

Every 1,000 average requests a second needs about 4 API servers: 3,000 at peak, on servers kept at 70% load, is 3,000 ÷ 700 ≈ 4.3. So 10 million daily users at 10 requests each is a fleet of about 5 servers.

#Small, medium and large

Three sizes cover most interview questions. Each assumes 10 requests per user a day, reads to writes at 10 to 1 (so about 1 write per user a day), and a peak of 3 times average.

ScaleDaily usersRequestsWritesSo
Small100 thousand10/s1/sOne app server and one database carry it. Run two of each so a failure is not an outage.
Medium10 million1,000/s100/sAbout 5 API servers, a cache and read replicas. One database primary still takes the writes.
Large1 billion100,000/s10,000/sAbout 430 API servers. The writes are past one primary, so the data is sharded, across several regions.

#Assumptions to propose

When the interviewer gives no numbers, propose round ones: "Can I assume each user posts once a day, about 1 KB, with reads to writes at 100 to 1?" These defaults keep every step a power of ten. The results are for 1 million daily users; multiply by 10 for 10 million and by 1,000 for 1 billion.

AppWrites per user a daySize of eachReads to writesWritesReadsNew data a day
Messaging10 messages1 KB10 to 1100/s1,000/s10 GB
Text feed1 post1 KB100 to 110/s1,000/s1 GB
Photo sharing1 photo1 MB10 to 110/s100/s1 TB

The three apps stress different things. Every app here reads more than it writes: even in a chat, each message is delivered, synced to other devices and read again when people scroll back. Messaging has the most writes, ten times the other two. A text feed is read-heavy with little data, so the work is caching. Photo sharing is about bytes: 100 reads a second at 1 MB is 100 MB/s going out, so photos need blob storage and a CDN.

#Read the answers off

Answer these from the anchors, without long division.

Estimate: Photo storage for one year

How much disk do one year of uploaded photos need, counting all copies?

You know
  • 1 million photos uploaded a day
  • 1 MB per stored photo
  • 3 copies of every photo for durability

Show the working
  1. Anchor: 1 million × 1 MB = 1 TB a day
  2. Per year: 1 TB × 400 ≈ 400 TB
  3. Three copies: 400 TB × 3 = 1,200 TB, about 1 PB

Estimate 1 PB (anything from 300 TB to 3 PB is a good answer)

A petabyte a year rules out keeping photos in a database. They go to blob storage; the database keeps a small row per photo.

Estimate: Photo bandwidth

How much photo data per second goes out to viewers on average?

You know
  • 10 million daily users
  • Each views 10 photos a day
  • 1 MB per photo

Show the working
  1. Anchor: 10 million users at 10 each is 1,000 views a second
  2. Anchor: 1,000 a second × 1 MB = 1 GB/s

Estimate 1 GB/s (anything from 300 MB/s to 3 GB/s is a good answer)

1 GB/s is 8 Gbit/s on average and about 24 Gbit/s at a 3x peak. That is a job for a CDN, not for your API servers.

#In the interview

Estimate after clarifying and before the API, as in the framework. Compute only what can change the design, usually peak rate, storage and bandwidth, and end each number with the decision it drives.

If the interviewer asks how sure you are: "Within a factor of 2 or 3, which is enough to choose the architecture." Be more careful only when a number lands close to a real limit, such as 900 requests a second against a server that handles 1,000.

Forgetting peak and copies

Average rates size nothing: systems fall over at peak. Storage without replication is a third of the disk you will buy. Apply the 3 times for peak and the 3 times for copies, out loud.

Quick check

A chat app has 10 million daily users, each sending 100 messages a day. About how many messages a second is that on average?

  1. Not quite. That treats 1 million a day as 1 a second. The anchor is 10 a second.
  2. Right. 10 million a day is 100 a second; × 100 = 10,000 a second.
  3. Not quite. That treats 1 million a day as 100 a second. The anchor is 10 a second.

Users upload 10 million photos a day at 1 MB each. About how much is that a year, for one copy?

  1. Not quite. That is less than one day: 1 million × 1 MB is already 1 TB.
  2. Right. 10 million × 1 MB = 10 TB a day; × 400 = 4,000 TB = 4 PB.
  3. Not quite. That is the year for 1 million photos a day at 1 MB. This is 10 times more data.

Images go out at 1 GB/s on average. What network capacity is that in the units links are sold in?

  1. Not quite. Links are quoted in bits; 1 byte is 8 bits.
  2. Right. 1 GB/s × 8 bits per byte = 8 Gbit/s, before any peak factor.
  3. Not quite. That divides by 8 instead of multiplying.
