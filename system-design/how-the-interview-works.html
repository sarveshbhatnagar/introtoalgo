How the Interview Works

What happens in a 45 to 60 minute system design interview, what the interviewer scores, how to split the time, and what an interruption means.

A candidate hears "Design a photo-sharing app", says "OK", and starts drawing. Forty minutes later the board holds fourteen boxes, a queue, three caches and a sharded database. The verdict is "no hire": the candidate never asked how many users there were, could not say why the queue existed, and talked over two hints.

Nothing on the board was wrong. But the interview is not a drawing test: it is a design meeting, scored on how you think out loud under time pressure.

#The format

Most interviews last 45 or 60 minutes; a 45-minute slot leaves about 40 minutes of design. The prompt is vague on purpose ("design a chat app"), and the interviewer plays a teammate who knows the answers to your questions.

There is no single correct design; two different systems can both pass. The interviewer collects evidence on five things.

What is scoredStrong signalWeak signal
Problem explorationAsks about features, scale and priorities, then states the scope.Draws at once, or asks twenty questions and never commits.
DesignSimple, works end to end, each box has a reason.Components added by reflex, or a path that does not connect.
Trade-offsNames two options, picks one, says its cost and what would change the choice."We add a queue because it scales." No cost, no alternative.
DepthGoes several levels down on the hard part: data layout, failure, numbers.Stays at box level, or goes deep on something easy.
CommunicationThinks out loud, checks in, builds on hints.Long silences, or defends a choice after it is shown to break.

Seniority changes the depth expected, not the list (see what changes by level).

#How to spend the time

The usual failure is spending 20 minutes on requirements and estimates and never reaching depth. Plan the time before you walk in.

The middle blocks are the steps of a framework for any design. In a 60-minute slot, give most of the extra 15 minutes to deep dives and failure.

Tip

Check the clock when you finish clarifying (aim for minute 11) and when the high-level design is drawn (minute 27). If you are late, say so and cut: "I'll skip the detailed API and come back to it if there's time."

#When the interviewer interrupts

An interruption is not a sign you are failing. It is the interviewer steering toward a signal they still need. Work out which kind it is.

  • A steer. "Let's assume the database handles it. How do clients get new messages?" They believe you can do this part. Follow.
  • A probe. "What happens if this cache dies?" A test of depth. Answer directly, with numbers, then return.
  • A hint. "Is a database write on every click a problem?" There is a flaw. Do not defend it: say what breaks and fix it.
  • A curveball. "Now it has to work in three regions." Restate it and change only the parts it affects.
  • A time check. "We have ten minutes left." Go to the most interesting remaining topic.

#In the interview

Before drawing, say your plan so the interviewer can correct you early.

  • Opening: "I'll ask about features and scale, estimate, sketch the API and a high-level design, then go deep on the hardest part."
  • Checking in: "Does this level of detail work, or should I go deeper on storage?"
  • Committing: "A relational database, because bookings need transactions. If writes grow 50 times, I'd revisit."
  • Admitting a gap: "I haven't run that in production, but here is how I'd reason about it."

Expect these near the end:

  • "What would you build first?" The smallest version of the main path: one region, one database, no sharding. Say which metric would trigger the next piece.
  • "How would you know it is working?" Two or three numbers to watch (99th-percentile latency, error rate, queue depth), each with an alert. See observability.
  • "What would you change with more time?" Name your design's weakest part and its fix.

#Common mistakes

Designing in silence

The interviewer can only score what they hear. Narrate decisions as you make them, especially the ones you reject.

Going deep before the design connects

Fifteen minutes on a perfect sharding scheme, before the read path even reaches a database, leaves no time for the rest. Connect a simple design end to end first, then go deep where the interviewer points.
