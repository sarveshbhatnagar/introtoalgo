Design Ticket Booking

Sell seats for a popular event without selling one twice: seat holds that expire, locking choices, a virtual waiting room for the spike, and payment.

A band announces one stadium show, on sale Friday at 10:00. At 9:59 two million fans are on the page, and there are 50,000 seats. Within minutes, each seat must go to exactly one person, everyone who pays must get a seat, and the site must stay up.

The normal day is easy. The hard parts: two people clicking one seat in the same second, a huge spike at one moment, and a payment that takes minutes while the seat stays reserved. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design.

#Clarify the problem

  • Functional: browse events and a live seat map; hold seats for a few minutes; pay and get tickets; release unpaid seats.
  • Non-functional: no seat sold twice (strong consistency on inventory); a paid order always ends with tickets or a refund; stay up when demand is 80 times supply; holds last 10 minutes.
  • Out of scope: resale, dynamic pricing, event creation, ticket scanning at the door.

#Estimate the scale

  • Normal day: 5 million orders a month ÷ 2.6 million seconds ≈ 2 a second. Browsing at 100 times that is 200 reads a second. One database handles it.
  • On-sale demand: 50,000 seats at about 2 per order is 25,000 orders: 80 fans per order. Most leave with nothing and must be told fast.
  • Without control: two million fans refreshing every 5 seconds is 2,000,000 ÷ 5 = 400,000 requests a second, all at one event's 50,000 rows.
  • Storage: 100,000 events a year × 2,000 seats × 100 B = 20 GB of seats; 60 million orders × 1 KB = 60 GB. Size is not the problem; contention is.
Estimate: How fast to let fans in

At most 10,000 fans may be in the booking flow at once, and each spends about 5 minutes there. How many new fans per second can you admit?

You know
  • At most 10,000 fans in the flow
  • About 5 minutes (300 seconds) per fan

Show the working
  1. Each slot frees up every 300 seconds
  2. 10,000 slots ÷ 300 s ≈ 33 fans a second, about 2,000 a minute

Estimate 33/s (anything from 10/s to 100/s is a good answer)

At 33 a second, 25,000 orders sell out in about 25,000 ÷ 33 ≈ 750 seconds, roughly 12 minutes, if everyone admitted buys.

Estimate: Polling load from the waiting room

Two million fans wait, each browser checking its place every 10 seconds. How many requests per second?

You know
  • 2 million fans waiting
  • One check per fan every 10 seconds

Show the working
  1. 2,000,000 ÷ 10 s = 200,000 requests a second

Estimate 200 thousand/s (anything from 60 thousand/s to 600 thousand/s is a good answer)

Too much for any database, so the waiting room must answer without one.

POST /orders/{id}/pay    Idempotency-Key: 9001
  200 { "status": "paid", "tickets": [...] }
  402 Payment Required   card declined; the hold stays until it expires
  410 Gone               the hold expired before payment started
 
DELETE /orders/{id}                         release a hold early

The decision worth arguing is the waiting-room status. Each fan's ticket carries their number, so the server publishes one number per event, "admitted up to 40,500". Every fan gets the same tiny response, so a CDN caching it for a second absorbs nearly all 200,000 requests a second.

Make pay safe to retry with an idempotency key (the order id), so a double-click on "Pay" never charges twice.

Inventory and orders

orders
  order_id     BIGINT PRIMARY KEY

Claiming several seats is all-or-nothing across rows: a relational database with transactions, partitioned by event_id when one machine is not enough. A key-value store does single-row conditional writes, not an atomic claim of two seats.

Hold the seat in the row, not in a database lock: database locks last milliseconds, and a fan takes minutes to pay.

#Deep dives

#Preventing double booking

Two requests for C-13 arrive within a millisecond. Each alone would read "available" and write "held". One must fail.

Ways to stop two buyers taking one seat
OptionGood atBad atPick it when
Pessimistic lockSELECT … FOR UPDATE locks the rows; then check and write. Easy to reason about.Other buyers queue behind the lock. Holding it while the fan pays freezes seats for minutes.The check needs several reads, and the transaction is short.
Optimistic version checkNo read locks. Write with WHERE version = 7; if someone wrote first, retry.Under heavy contention most attempts fail and retry, wasting work.Conflicts are rare, as on a normal day.
Conditional update Usual defaultOne statement claims the seats only if they are free. One round trip; the row lock lasts one statement.The rule must fit a WHERE clause; the caller must check rows changed.Claiming a known set of seats. The usual answer here.
Holds in an in-memory storeVery fast; "set if absent, expire in 10 minutes" gives self-expiring holds.A second source of truth. A failover that loses a write lets two fans hold one seat, so the sale must still be checked in the database.The database cannot take the hold rate, even behind a waiting room.
Claim two seats, all or nothing
BEGIN;
UPDATE seats
   SET status = 'held', order_id = 9001,
       held_until = now() + interval '10 minutes'
 WHERE event_id = 77 AND seat_id IN ('C-12', 'C-13')
   AND (status = 'available'
        OR (status = 'held' AND held_until < now()));
-- rows changed = 2: COMMIT.  fewer than 2: ROLLBACK, report 409.
COMMIT;

Why this is safe: the second transaction to update a row waits for the first one's row lock. At the default read-committed level in databases such as PostgreSQL, it then re-checks its WHERE clause, sees held, and changes nothing. At repeatable read or serializable it fails with a serialization error; treat that like a 409. Databases differ (see transactions and isolation), so name the level you rely on. Overlapping claims in opposite order can deadlock; the database aborts one, which reports the seats as taken.

I would pick the conditional update: one round trip, and no lock held while anyone thinks.

Checking, then writing, in two steps

Reading "is C-13 free?" then writing "hold C-13" in a separate statement leaves a gap: two requests both see free, both write held. Check and write must be one atomic step.

#Surviving the on-sale spike

More servers do not help: the 50,000 seats live on one partition, and more booking servers only send it more conflicting writes. Instead, limit how many fans reach booking with a virtual waiting room.

  • Join: each fan gets a number in a signed ticket. Numbers handed out before 10:00 are shuffled at 10:00, so racing the clock gains nothing; after that, first come, first served.
  • Wait: fans poll only the cached "admitted up to N", so 200,000 polls a second cost almost nothing.
  • Admit: a controller keeps at most 10,000 fans in the flow, raising N as orders complete or passes time out. The database sees about 33 new fans a second, whatever the crowd.
  • Enter: an admitted fan gets a signed 15-minute pass tied to their account. The gateway checks it without shared state.
  • Stop: when no seat is available or held, announce sold out. At 33 a second, two million would take 2,000,000 ÷ 33 ≈ 60,000 seconds, about 17 hours, to drain.

The seat map cache is rebuilt about once a second. The map is advice and the conditional update is the truth, so a stale map costs a fan one 409, never a double sale.

#Paying and releasing holds

Bugs live between "held" and "sold".

  • The hold must outlive the payment. If a hold lapses at 10:31:04 and the charge succeeds at 10:31:05, another fan may hold the seat. So on Pay, move the order to paying and extend held_until past the processor's longest time. paying holds are never swept.
  • Confirm with a check. The sale is UPDATE seats SET status = 'sold' WHERE order_id = 9001 AND status = 'held', and it must change both rows. If not, refund automatically.
  • An unknown result is not a failure. After a timeout the charge may have gone through. Keep the order in paying, ask the processor by idempotency key, then sell or release (see Design a Payment System).

#Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x, twenty million fans for one show, 2 million polls a second for one cached number is a CDN problem. The booking side does not change: the admission cap sets its load. For many on-sales at once, partition inventory by event_id so each big show gets its own partition.

Asynchronous replication on the inventory database

With asynchronous replication, an acknowledged hold is lost if a replica that never received it takes over. The seat looks free and sells again. For inventory, pay the write latency of a synchronous replica.

LevelWhat the answer does
JuniorA booking service and a database; with prompting, a transaction around "check, then book".
MidExpiring holds, a conditional update or lock with reasons, a cached seat map.
SeniorSees the spike lands on one partition and cannot be scaled away, adds a waiting room with a cheap status endpoint, protects the hold-to-payment gap, and names each cost.

#Follow-up questions

#How would you handle general admission?

Keep a count per section and decrement it with a conditional update: SET remaining = remaining - 2 WHERE remaining >= 2. Every claim waits for that one row's lock. Split the count across, say, 10 rows and have each request try a random row with tickets left, so ten claims commit at once.

#How do you stop bots from buying everything?

One place in line per verified account, a challenge on joining, and a ticket cap per order and per account.

#A group wants six seats together. What changes?

Offer "best available": the booking service finds six adjacent free seats and claims them with the same all-or-nothing update. If one was just taken, search again.

#Could you push seat changes to the map instead of polling?

Yes, over server-sent events. It cuts 409s but adds 10,000 open connections and a fan-out per write. A map one second stale is harmless, so add push only if 409s are frequent.

#Why not hold the seats for 30 minutes to be kind?

Each minute of hold hides an abandoned seat from fans in line. Ten minutes, extended once payment starts, balances both.
