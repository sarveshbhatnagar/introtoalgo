Mock Interview Practice

Run a timed 45-minute mock alone or with a partner, then work eight new prompts with clarifying questions, likely deep dives and a self-review.

Reading ten case studies feels like progress. Then the clock starts, someone is watching, and you hear yourself say "um, so, a database" with no plan. Reading trains recognition; interviews test saying a design out loud, on time, while someone interrupts.

The fix is timed, spoken practice on prompts you have not seen: how to run a session, then eight prompts no case study covers.

#Eight prompts

Run the whole mock before opening a box's notes: questions to ask, likely deep dives, and three checks for your review.

Your turn: 1. A game leaderboard

A mobile game with 20 million daily players wants a global leaderboard and a "your rank" screen.

Compare with a model answer

Ask: global only, or friends too? Daily, weekly or all-time? How often do scores change? Must rank 9,000,000 be exact, or is "top 45%" fine?

Deep dives: a structure that answers rank in O(log n), such as a sorted set; sizing: 20 million entries at about 100 bytes is 2 GB, so one in-memory node and a replica hold it, and sharding is not the hard part; rejecting impossible scores.

Check yourself: I estimated peak score updates. I argued exact versus approximate rank. I rebuilt the board from durable scores when its node died.

Your turn: 2. A parking garage system

A chain of 500 garages wants online reservations and entry gates that keep working when the network is down.

Compare with a model answer

Ask first: "The class design or the distributed system?" It is asked both ways. Then: specific spots or a count per type (compact, EV)? How long is a reservation held for a late driver?

Deep dives: no double booking, with a conditional decrement on (garage, type, time slot); the gate's offline mode, with today's reservations cached locally and synced later; paying once even when the gate retries.

Check yourself: I said the scale is small. I designed the offline gate and its sync. I made payment idempotent.

Your turn: 3. A metrics dashboard

10,000 servers each send 200 metric values every 10 seconds. Engineers want charts for the last 30 days and alerts.

Compare with a model answer

Ask: how fresh must charts be? How long is full resolution kept? How many label combinations? Can data arrive late?

Deep dives: ingest rate, 10,000 × 200 ÷ 10 = 200,000 points a second, buffered through a stream; time-series layout partitioned by metric and time, compressed; rollups to 1-minute and 1-hour points so a 30-day chart reads 720 hourly points per series, not 259,200 raw ones.

Check yourself: I computed the ingest rate. I planned rollups and retention. I said what happens to alerts if ingestion falls behind.

Your turn: 4. An online code judge

Users submit solutions to programming problems: 10,000 submissions an hour, 10 times that during contests.

Compare with a model answer

Ask: which languages? Time and memory limits per test? Is there a contest scoreboard? (The code is untrusted; say so yourself.)

Deep dives: sandboxing with no network, CPU and memory limits, a fresh environment per run; workers sized for contests: 10,000 an hour is about 3 a second, 30 at peak, and at 10 seconds per run that is 30 × 10 = 300 runs at once; returning results by polling or push.

Check yourself: I treated submitted code as hostile. I sized workers from rate × run time. I said what users see when the queue is long.

Your turn: 5. A hotel booking site

Travellers search and book rooms across 50,000 hotels for date ranges.

Compare with a model answer

Ask: specific rooms or a room type? Pay now or at the hotel? Is any overbooking allowed? Do hotels also sell through other sites?

Deep dives: inventory as a count per (hotel, room type, night), so a three-night stay decrements three rows in one transaction, each only if above zero; search from a stale copy, the real check at booking; a hold that expires if payment stalls. Compare ticket booking.

Check yourself: I booked a date range atomically. I split stale search from strict booking. I handled abandoned payments.

Your turn: 6. A comment system

Readers comment under articles on a news site. Most articles get a few comments; a breaking story gets thousands an hour.

Compare with a model answer

Ask: how deep can replies nest? Sort by newest or top? Moderation before or after publishing? Must new comments appear live?

Deep dives: storing threads (a parent id per comment, plus a thread path for sorting) and cursor pagination; caching the breaking story's first page for a few seconds; asynchronous moderation that can hide a comment later.

Check yourself: I chose a thread model and showed one page's query. I handled the hot article. I said what users see during moderation.

Your turn: 7. A link analytics service

Show owners of short links clicks per hour, top countries and unique visitors. Peak traffic is 20,000 clicks a second.

Compare with a model answer

Ask: how fresh, seconds or an hour? Exact unique visitors, or within 1%? How long are raw clicks kept? Should bots count?

Deep dives: stream aggregation into per-link, per-hour counters, with raw events kept for reprocessing; approximate uniques with a sketch such as HyperLogLog, under 1% error in about 12 KB per counter; duplicate and late events. It extends the counting in the URL shortener.

Check yourself: I kept analytics off the redirect path. I justified exact or approximate uniques. I fixed a counting bug by replaying raw events.

Your turn: 8. An online auction

Timed auctions. Most get a few bids, but the last minute of a popular one gets hundreds of bids a second.

Compare with a model answer

Ask: minimum increments? Does a late bid extend the end? What do watchers see live? When is the winner charged?

Deep dives: ordering bids on one auction, with a conditional write "only if higher than the current top" or one owner per auction; pushing new top bids to watchers through persistent connections; closing on the server's clock, never the client's.

Check yourself: I serialised bids per auction, not globally. I sized the live fan-out. I handled a bid at the closing second.

#In the interview

Use the same opening on every unseen prompt, so something automatic comes out while you think:

  • "Let me make sure I have the scope: [restate it]. A few questions on features, then scale."
  • "I'll get one path working end to end, then go deep on [the hard part]."

Practise the closing follow-ups: "What would you build first?", "What is weakest?", "How would you know it works?"

#Common mistakes

Practising silently

Thinking a design through silently skips the hard part: speaking it clearly under time. Talk out loud and record.

Quick check

A code judge gets 30 submissions a second at contest peak, and each run takes about 10 seconds. How many runs are in progress at once?

  1. Not quite. That divides the rate by the run time; multiply instead.
  2. Right. Work in progress = arrival rate × time each job takes: 30 × 10 = 300.
  3. Not quite. That is the arrival rate. Each run lasts 10 seconds, so runs overlap.

You have run three mocks. Two reviews say you never raise failure until asked. What should the next session be?

  1. Right. Fix the named gap on fresh material.
  2. Not quite. Reading builds recognition, not the habit of saying it under time.
  3. Not quite. That trains one memorised answer, not the habit.

After your attempt, tick what you really did. Be strict: "I thought about it" is not "I said it out loud".
