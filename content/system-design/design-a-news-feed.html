You open a social app and see posts from the people you follow; scroll and older ones load. Post something and your followers see it within seconds. The hard question: when someone posts, do you copy it into every follower's feed then, or gather posts later, when each follower opens the app?

The two obvious answers fail at scale in opposite directions, and the fix is a hybrid you justify with numbers. It follows A Framework for Any Design.

Clarify the problem

Pin down what the feed contains, its order, and the shape of the follow graph, which decides the design.

Design the news feed for a social app. Only posts from accounts I follow, or also recommendations and ads? Chronological or ranked? Only accounts you follow. Ranked, but start chronological. How many daily users, how often do they open the feed, and how often do they post? 100 million daily users, each opening it about 10 times a day. About 10 million posts a day. How are follower counts spread? Can one account have tens of millions? The average is about 200 followers, but some accounts have 50 million. Then the feed read is the hot path. A new post can take a few seconds to appear, and a feed briefly missing a post is fine. Huge accounts need special handling. I'll leave out media, search and notifications. Asking about the distribution, not just the average, sets up the hybrid. Most candidates miss it until the interviewer mentions celebrities.
  • Functional: publish a post; see posts from followed accounts, newest first at launch, ranked later; scroll back through older ones.
  • Non-functional: the feed loads in under 200 ms at p99 on our side; stays available when parts fail; a new post reaches feeds within about 5 seconds; feeds may be eventually consistent.
  • Out of scope: media upload, search, notifications, ads, recommendations.

Estimate the scale

  • Feed reads: 100 million × 10 = 109 a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 12,000 a second; 36,000 at a 3x peak.
  • Posts: 10 million a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 116 a second; about 350 at peak. Reads outnumber posts 100 to 1.
  • Post storage: about 1 KB per post (media lives in a blob store): 107 × 1 KB = 10 GB a day, about 3.7 TB a year.

Write the endpoints for posting and reading the feed. How does the client ask for the next page?

POST /v1/posts and GET /v1/feed with a limit and a cursor. Page numbers return duplicates when new posts keep arriving at the top.

Define the API

POST /v1/posts
  body: { "text": "...", "media_ids": ["m_91"]? }
  201 Created   { "post_id": "7311045529", "created_at": "..." }
  400 Bad Request          empty or too long
  429 Too Many Requests    posting too fast

GET /v1/feed?limit=20&cursor=opaque-string
  200 OK  { "posts": [ { "post_id", "author", "text", "media_urls", "created_at", "like_count" } ],
            "next_cursor": "opaque-string" or null }
  400 Bad Request          cursor expired or invalid: start again from the top

POST   /v1/users/{id}/follow
DELETE /v1/users/{id}/follow

The decision worth arguing is cursor pagination: "continue after this exact item", so new posts at the top cannot shift the next page the way ?page=2 does. It is opaque, so its contents can change when ranking arrives. The deep dive shows both.

For posts, follows and the feed: what is the main lookup, and what store fits?

Posts by id. Follows both ways: "who follows X" for fan-out, "whom does Y follow" for reads. The feed: a short in-memory list of post ids per user.

Model the data

posts           post_id    BIGINT PRIMARY KEY   -- time-sortable id
                author_id  BIGINT
                text       VARCHAR(2000)
                media_ids  LIST
                created_at TIMESTAMP
                deleted    BOOLEAN

followers       (author_id, follower_id)        -- "who follows X": fan-out
following       (follower_id, author_id)        -- "whom does Y follow": reads

author_posts    author_id -> newest post ids    -- for pull and rebuilds

feed            user_id -> set of (post_id, author_id), sorted by post_id, max 500
  • Posts are written once, read by id, and partitioned by post_id: a wide-column or key-value store with a cache for hot posts. A time-sortable id (see Unique IDs) lets feeds sort by id alone.
  • The follow graph is stored once per direction, each partitioned by its first column, so both lookups are single-partition reads. The cost is keeping two copies in step: write both on follow, repair with a background job.
  • Feed lists live in a replicated in-memory store such as Redis. They hold ids, so an edit or delete never touches 50 million feeds, and they are derived: a lost list is rebuilt from following and author_posts.

Sketch the write path (a post reaching followers' feeds) and the read path (a feed page reaching the app). Which parts are synchronous?

The post is stored synchronously; a queue and fan-out workers copy it into feeds asynchronously. A read takes one feed list from cache and fills in the posts.

High-level design

The post service stores the post and publishes an event; fan-out workers do the slow copying into feeds. The feed service reads one list, adds posts from the few huge accounts the user follows, and fills in post bodies from the post cache.

Deep dives

Fan-out on write, on read, or both

This is the question interviewers care about most. Fan-out on write (push) copies each new post's id into every follower's feed. Fan-out on read (pull) stores nothing per reader: opening the app fetches and merges the newest posts of everyone you follow. Put numbers on both:

  • Pull for everyone: each open reads about 200 author lists, so 36,000 × 200 = 7.2 million list reads a second at peak.
  • Push for everyone: 69,000 inserts a second at peak, fine until a 50-million-follower account posts. If the fan-out fleet does 200,000 inserts a second, that post takes 5 × 107 ÷ 2 × 105 = 250 seconds and delays every post behind it.

Pick a threshold, say 100,000 followers, and say why: below it, a post costs at most 100,000 inserts, half a second of the fleet. Above it, posts are pulled at read time. A typical user follows a handful of such accounts, so a read merges one pushed list with a few pulled ones, all from cache.

Also skip inactive users when pushing. Someone away 30 days gets no inserts; when they return, build their feed by pull once, then push again.

Feed storage and pagination

Each feed holds up to 500 ids in a set sorted by post id, not a list you prepend to. Fan-out can finish out of order, and a queue redelivers events after a crash; a sorted set puts late posts in place and ignores repeats (see Idempotency and Retries). Ranking, in outline

The interview needs ranking's shape, not its model: a stage with about 50 ms of the 200 ms budget.

  1. Candidates: the ~500 pushed ids plus the pulled lists.
  2. Filter: deleted posts, blocked authors, posts already seen.
  3. Score: a model predicts engagement from features like how often the reader interacts with the author, the post's age and its early engagement.
  4. Re-rank for variety: at most 2 posts in a row from one author.

If ranking is slow or down, return the chronological feed: a worse feed beats no feed.

Cache strategy

  • Feed lists (800 GB), replicated. A lost node means rebuilding feeds by pull, so warm them gradually.
  • Post objects. The last two days' posts, about 2 × 10 GB = 20 GB, take most reads. Use cache-aside; delete the entry on edit or delete.
  • Recent posts of huge accounts, read by millions of feeds. Copy these hot keys into each feed server's memory for a few seconds so no single cache node takes every read.
  • Follow lists, both directions, to spare the graph store.

Keep like counts in a separate counter, cached briefly and updated in batches, or every like invalidates a hot post.

Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x (360,000 feed reads and about 690,000 fan-out inserts a second at peak), raw feed data reaches about 8 TB: a larger cluster partitioned by user id with consistent hashing. Partition the fan-out queue by author so workers scale out. First to hurt: huge accounts' hot keys (local in-memory copies) and the fan-out fleet (lower the threshold).

Follow-up questions

What happens when I unfollow someone?

Remove that author's ids from your feed list; at 500 entries the scan is cheap. A fan-out in flight may still add one, so the read path also drops ids whose author you no longer follow.

How do you handle a deleted post?

Mark it deleted and delete its cache entry. Feed lists still hold the id; hydration drops it. Nothing touches millions of feeds.

How does the app show "5 new posts"?

Every minute or so, the app asks how many ids are above its newest one: a cheap feed-list read. Pushing over an open connection costs a connection per active user.

How would you run this in two regions?

Serve feeds from each region's caches and stores, replicate posts and follows asynchronously, and run fan-out per region. A post may reach the other region a second or two later.

An account crosses the 100,000 threshold. What changes?

New posts switch to pull. Older posts already sit in followers' lists, so nothing moves; the read path merges both and drops duplicate ids.
