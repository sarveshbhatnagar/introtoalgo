A shop serves product images from one region. At peak, pages load 5,000 images a second at about 200 KB each: 5,000 × 200 KB = 1 GB/s, or 8 Gbit/s, all leaving one set of servers. Users 8,000 km away wait at least 80 ms per round trip for each image, though every shopper gets the same bytes.

The idea

A content delivery network (CDN) is a set of caching servers, called edges, in many cities. Users reach a nearby edge. If it has the file, it answers; if not, it fetches it once from your servers (the origin) and keeps a copy.

Latency: light in fibre covers about 200 km per ms (Networking Essentials), so an edge 500 km away costs 2 × 500 ÷ 200 = 5 ms per round trip, not 80. Load: if edges answer 95% of requests, the origin sends 1 GB/s × 0.05 = 50 MB/s.

The cost is that of any cache: copies in hundreds of places that you cannot update instantly.

Step 5 is why hit rates disappoint: every edge fills its own cache. An origin shield, one middle cache all edges ask first, means the origin sees one miss per file instead of one per edge.

Push or pull

Cache keys and invalidation

The cache key decides which requests share a copy. By default it is roughly host, path and query string. Keep only the parts that change the bytes:

  • Too much and the hit rate collapses: with ?utm_source=mail in the key, every campaign link caches its own copy.
  • Too little and users get the wrong content: if the key ignores ?size=small, everyone gets whichever size was cached first.

Three ways to change a cached file, best first:

  • Versioned names. Put a content hash in the name (app.3f9a1c.js) and cache it for a year. A new version is a new URL; nothing to purge. Give the HTML that links to it a short TTL.
  • TTL. Let copies expire. Simple, but old content can live for the whole TTL.
  • Purge. Ask the CDN to drop a path everywhere. Slow to spread, then every edge misses at once.

Signed URLs for private files

Paid videos and private files can use a CDN too. A signed URL carries an expiry time and a signature over path and expiry, often an HMAC with a secret your app and the CDN share. The edge checks both on its own, without calling your servers.

https://cdn.example.com/videos/v881/seg-0042.ts
    ?expires=1767225600
    &sig=HMAC(secret, "/videos/v881/seg-0042.ts" + "1767225600")

Keep expiry to minutes. For a video of hundreds of segments, one signed cookie for the folder saves signing each.

What goes on a CDN

  • Yes: images, scripts, styles, fonts, video, downloads, and public responses that are the same for everyone, even for a few seconds (a live score).
  • No: per-user pages, and anything every reader must see up to the second.

Uncached requests gain too: users handshake with a nearby edge that keeps warm connections to your origin.

Numbers worth remembering

  • Origin load = total requests × (1 − hit rate).
  • Video bandwidth = viewers × bitrate. 8 bits per byte.

In the interview

Bring in a CDN for static files, media, or users across regions. Say what goes on it and how it is invalidated: "Images and video are most of our bytes and the same for everyone, so they go on a CDN. Names carry a content hash, so I cache them for a year and never purge."

Videos are for paying subscribers only. Can they still go on a CDN? Yes. The app checks the subscription and hands out a signed URL or cookie that expires in minutes. Edges verify it themselves, so the origin only sees misses. What if someone shares the URL? It works for anyone until it expires, hence the short expiry. If sharing is a real threat, I'd sign in the viewer's IP too, so it fails on other networks. A weak answer says "the CDN checks the user's login", which sends every request back to the origin and undoes the CDN.

Follow-ups to expect:

  • "How does a user reach the nearest edge?" DNS that answers by the resolver's location, or anycast: one IP announced from many places, routed to the closest.
  • "What if the CDN goes down?" Fail over by DNS to a second CDN. The origin alone rarely survives 100% of the traffic.
  • "Why is the hit rate low?" Too many cache keys (tracking parameters, cookies), short TTLs, or a long tail of files over many edges. An origin shield helps the last.

Common mistakes

Cache a per-user response under a shared key and the next visitor sees someone else's page. Mark per-user responses Cache-Control: private or no-store.
