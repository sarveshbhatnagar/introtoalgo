A candidate hears "Design a photo-sharing app", says "OK", and starts drawing. Forty minutes later the whiteboard holds fourteen boxes, a queue, three caches and a sharded database. The feedback says "no hire": the candidate never asked how many users there were, could not say why the queue existed, and talked over two hints from the interviewer.

Nothing on that board was wrong. The candidate treated the interview as a drawing test. It is a design meeting with a colleague, scored on how you think out loud under time pressure.

The format

Most system design interviews last 45 or 60 minutes. A few minutes go to introductions and a few at the end to your questions, so a 45-minute slot gives you about 40 minutes of design. You get a short, vague prompt ("design a chat app") on purpose. You work on a whiteboard or a shared drawing tool, and the interviewer plays a teammate who knows the answers to your questions.

There is no single correct design. Two candidates can draw different systems and both pass. What the interviewer writes down is evidence about five things.

What is scoredStrong signalWeak signal
Problem explorationAsks about features, scale and priorities, then states what is in and out of scope.Starts drawing after the prompt, or asks twenty questions and never commits.
DesignA simple design that works end to end, where each box has a reason.Popular components added by reflex, or a path that does not connect.
Trade-offsNames at least two options, picks one, says what it costs and what would change the choice."We add a queue because it scales." No cost, no alternative.
DepthGoes several levels down on the hard part: data layout, failure, the numbers.Stays at box level everywhere, or goes deep on something easy.
CommunicationThinks out loud, checks in, takes hints and builds on them.Long silences, or defends a choice after the interviewer has shown it breaks.

Seniority changes how much depth and how much leading is expected, not the list itself. The lesson on what changes by level covers that.

How to spend the time

Time is the scarce resource. The usual failure is spending 20 minutes on requirements and estimates and never reaching the part where you show depth. Plan the hour before you walk in.

The middle blocks are the steps of a framework for any design, which gives minutes per step. In a 60-minute slot, keep the early steps about the same length and give most of the extra 15 minutes to deep dives and failure. That is where a longer interview is meant to dig.

Glance at the clock at two moments: when you finish clarifying (aim for about minute 11) and when the high-level design is drawn (about minute 27). If you are late at either point, say so and cut: "I'll skip the detailed API and come back to it if we have time."

When the interviewer interrupts

Interruptions are not a sign you are failing. The interviewer has a list of signals to collect in 40 minutes, and an interruption is usually them steering toward one. Work out which kind it is.

  • A steer. "Let's assume the database handles it. How do clients get new messages?" You are spending time on something they already believe you can do. Let it go and follow.
  • A probe. "What happens if this cache dies?" They are testing depth or failure thinking. Answer directly, with numbers if you have them, then return to where you were.
  • A hint. "Is a database write on every click a problem?" There is a flaw. Do not defend it. Say what breaks, fix it, and build on their point.
  • A curveball. "Now it has to work in three regions." A new requirement, to see if you adapt. Restate it, say which parts of your design change, and change only those.
  • A time check. "We have ten minutes left." Pick the single most interesting remaining topic and go there.

"Park" means saying it out loud: "Good question, the cache failing means reads fall through to the database at 10 times the normal load, so I'd add replicas. Going back to the write path..." One sentence of answer, one of return.

In the interview

The first two minutes set the tone. Do not draw yet. Say what you are going to do, so the interviewer can follow and correct you early.

  • Opening: "I'll start with a few questions on features and scale, do a quick estimate, then sketch the API and a high-level design, and leave time to go deep on the hardest part."
  • Checking in: "Does this level of detail work, or should I go deeper on storage?"
  • Committing: "I'll go with a relational database here because bookings need transactions. If writes grow 50 times, I'd revisit."
  • Admitting a gap: "I haven't run that system in production, but here is how I'd reason about it."
Quick question before you go on. Your feed service calls the database on every request. Is that OK? No. At 3,000 feed loads a second and about 10 reads each, that is 30,000 reads a second on one primary. I'd cache each user's feed for a minute and serve reads from replicas. The cost is that a new post can take up to a minute to appear, which I think is fine for a feed. It answers with a number, gives a fix, and names the cost. Then it returns to the plan. So back to the write path: when someone posts, ... Quick question before you go on. Your feed service calls the database on every request. Is that OK? Yes, it should be fine, databases are fast. We can always scale it later. The interviewer was pointing at a problem. "Scale it later" without saying how tells them you did not see it.

Follow-up questions you should expect near the end, with short answers:

  • "What would you build first?" The smallest version that serves the main path: one region, one database, no sharding. Say what metric would make you add the next piece.
  • "How would you know it is working?" Name two or three numbers you would watch (latency at the 99th percentile, error rate, queue depth) and an alert on each. See observability.
  • "What would you change with more time?" Pick the weakest part of your own design and say how you would fix it. Interviewers like candidates who can see their own gaps.

Common mistakes

The interviewer can only score what they hear. Five quiet minutes of drawing gives them nothing to write down. Narrate decisions as you make them, especially the ones you reject.

Spending 15 minutes on the perfect sharding scheme before the read path reaches a database leaves no time to show the rest. Get a simple design working end to end first, then go deep where the interviewer points.
