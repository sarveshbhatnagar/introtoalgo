A payment platform lets online shops charge their customers' cards through one API. The platform sends each charge to an external card processor, records the result, keeps track of how much money each shop is owed, and pays the shops out.

This question is asked because it reverses the usual trade-off: most designs give up some consistency to stay available, while a payment system must stay correct even if that means rejecting requests. The difficulty comes from not knowing what happened. A call to the processor can time out after the card was charged, a notification can arrive twice or late, and the books must always balance. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design.

Clarify the problem

Design a payment system. Are we a platform that shops call and that uses an external card processor, or are we building the processor itself? You are a platform. Shops call your API, and a third-party processor talks to the card networks. You keep a fee and pay the rest to the shops. How many payments a day do we handle, and do we need refunds? About 5 million a day, averaging $40. Yes, refunds are needed, and shops are paid out daily. Then my priorities are to never charge a customer twice, never lose an acknowledged payment, and keep books that show exactly what each shop is owed. If I have to choose, I will reject a payment before I risk a double charge. Saying early that you would reject a payment rather than double charge shows that you know this design favours consistency over availability, unlike a feed or a cache.

The functional requirements are to charge a card through the processor, report the status of a payment, refund all or part of a payment, keep a ledger of what each shop is owed, and reconcile with the processor every day. The non-functional requirements are that the card is charged at most once however often a shop retries, that no acknowledged payment is lost, that every movement of money can be audited for years, that a normal request is answered in about 2 seconds, and that correctness matters more than availability. The service stops waiting for the processor after 5 seconds.

Fraud detection, currency conversion, the bank transfer that pays shops and storing raw card numbers are out of scope. The processor gives the platform a token in place of the card number.

Estimate the scale

5 million payments a day is 5 million ÷ 86,400 ≈ 58 payments a second on average. Sales days can reach 10 times that, about 600 a second. One relational database handles that rate.

If 1 processor call in 1,000 times out, then 5,000 payments a day start with an unknown result, so the design must treat unknown outcomes as a normal daily case.

Write the endpoints for charging a customer and checking a payment. How can the shop retry safely after a timeout?

Creating a payment and creating a refund both carry an idempotency key chosen by the shop. A retry with the same key returns the original payment instead of creating a new charge.

The API decision to argue is the processing response. When the processor does not reply in time, the platform does not know whether the card was charged, so it says the outcome is not known yet and the shop learns it from a webhook or by polling. Answering "failed" would make the shop ask the customer to pay again.

Which records do you need for a payment, its history and the money it moves? Which may be updated, and which may only be appended to?

A payment row holds the status, which changes only through allowed transitions, and a uniquely constrained idempotency key. An append-only history records each status change. An append-only ledger holds entries that sum to zero within each transaction.

Model the data

payments
  payment_id      VARCHAR(32) PRIMARY KEY
  merchant_id     BIGINT
  amount_cents    BIGINT
  status          VARCHAR(16)   -- created | processing | succeeded | failed | refunded
  processor_ref   VARCHAR(64) NULL
  idem_key        VARCHAR(64)
  request_hash    CHAR(64)      -- detects a reused key with a new body
  UNIQUE (merchant_id, idem_key)

payment_events                  -- append-only history
  payment_id, seq, from_status, to_status, source, created_at

ledger_entries                  -- append-only, never updated
  entry_id        BIGINT PRIMARY KEY
  txn_id          BIGINT        -- entries of one txn sum to zero
  account         VARCHAR(64)   -- "processor_receivable", "merchant:42"
  amount_cents    BIGINT        -- debit positive, credit negative
  created_at      TIMESTAMP

Money is stored as integer cents, never as floating point, because floating point cannot represent most decimal fractions exactly.

The writes are small and transactional: insert a payment with its key, change the status only from the expected old status, and write all of a payment's ledger lines or none. That calls for a relational database with ACID transactions, run as a single leader with a synchronous replica. A horizontally scaled key-value store gives up these transactions, and 600 payments a second does not need one.

Deep dives

Idempotency keys from the shop to the processor

An idempotency key lets a client repeat a request any number of times with the same effect as sending it once. The idempotency lesson covers the pattern, and three details decide whether it works in a payment system.

The first is to write the key in the same database transaction as the payment. With the key in a cache and the payment in the database, a crash between the two writes would let a retry create a second payment.

The second is to scope the key to the shop and to store a hash of the request body next to it. If the same key arrives with a different amount, the shop has a bug, and the service returns 422 instead of silently charging the old amount.

The third is to pass a key to the processor as well, here the payment id pay_88. If the processor's reply is lost and the service retries, the processor recognises the key and returns the first charge's result. Every hop that can be retried needs its own key.

The payment state machine and unknown outcomes

A payment is in exactly one state, and only certain moves between states are allowed.

FromToTriggered by
createdprocessingthe service is about to call the processor
processingsucceededthe processor's reply, a webhook or a status query
processingfaileda definite decline, or the processor confirming that it has no such charge
succeededrefundeda refund for the full amount succeeds

Each move is a conditional update, for example SET status = 'succeeded' WHERE payment_id = 'pay_88' AND status = 'processing'. If the processor's reply and its webhook race each other, the second update changes zero rows.

Payments that stay in processing are expected, so a sweeper job handles them. It asks the processor about every payment that has been in processing for more than a minute, using the idempotency key. A payment may move to failed only when the processor says it has no charge with that key, and that answer can race with the original request still in flight. The sweeper therefore waits longer than the processor's own timeout before it believes "not found", or it resends the charge with the same key.

If a timed-out payment is marked failed, the shop asks the customer to pay again and both charges go through. A timeout means the outcome is unknown, so the payment stays in processing until the processor says otherwise.

Double-entry ledger or a balance column

A balance column per shop is easy to update but cannot be audited: when a balance is wrong, nothing records which payment made it wrong.

A double-entry ledger records every movement of money as entries whose amounts sum to zero. For the $40 payment, with a 3% platform fee of $1.20, the entries are:

AccountAmount (cents)Meaning
processor_receivable+4000the processor owes the platform $40
merchant:42−3880the platform owes the shop $38.80
platform_fees−120the platform earned $1.20

The sum is +4000 − 3880 − 120 = 0. Three rules make the ledger trustworthy.

  • Every transaction must sum to zero, which the service checks before it commits. Money then only moves between accounts and is never created or lost.
  • Entries are only appended. A mistake is corrected with a new reversing transaction and never by editing a row, so the history stays a complete audit trail.
  • Balances are derived data. A shop's balance is the sum of its entries. It can be cached in a row that is updated in the same transaction, but the entries are the source of truth.

Webhooks, retries and reconciliation

The processor delivers webhooks at least once and not always in order. The handler first verifies the signature. Then, in a single transaction, it stores the event id under a unique key and applies the event through the state machine, and only after the commit does it return 200. A repeated event hits the unique key and changes nothing. If the handler crashes before the commit, it never returns 200, so the processor sends the event again. Events that arrive out of order are harmless, because an illegal move changes zero rows.

Retries to the processor use exponential backoff with random jitter and the same idempotency key. Never retry on a second processor while the outcome on the first is unknown, because processors do not share idempotency keys and both could charge the card.

Reconciliation is the safety net behind everything else. Each day the processor publishes a settlement file listing what it actually charged and refunded. The reconciler matches each line to a payment by reference and amount.

Bottlenecks and failures

The first limit is the database leader. If traffic grew another 10 times beyond the sales-day peak of 600 payments a second, it would reach 6,000 payments and 18,000 ledger lines a second, which is too much for one leader. The fix is to partition by merchant_id, so that all transactions of one shop stay on one partition. Platform-wide accounts such as platform_fees become hot rows, so each partition keeps its own copy and reports add the copies together.

When a component dies, the rule is to stop accepting payments rather than guess, because lost sales cost less than a double charge.

Your call to the processor times out. What do you do? A timeout means I do not know the outcome, so the payment stays in processing. I retry with the same idempotency key, so the processor returns the first result instead of charging again. If that also fails, the shop is told the payment is processing, and a webhook or a status query settles it later. And if the webhook never arrives? A sweeper asks the processor about every payment stuck in processing for more than a minute, and the daily reconciliation catches anything still wrong. All of these go through the same state machine.

Follow-up questions

How do refunds work?

A refund is its own record with its own idempotency key and its own states, linked to the original payment. When the processor confirms it, the platform writes reversing ledger entries. The total of partial refunds may not exceed the original amount.

How would you support authorize now and capture later?

For goods that ship later, the platform authorizes the card first, which reserves the funds, and captures the money when the order ships. This adds the states authorized and captured to the state machine. Authorizations expire after some days, so a job must capture or release each one in time.

Could an eventually consistent database be used?

It should not be used for payments and the ledger. The insert of the key and the change of status must be atomic, and an acknowledged write that is lost is lost money. A shop's dashboard can still read from a replica that is a few seconds behind, because it only displays data.

What happens when a customer disputes a charge?

A dispute, also called a chargeback, arrives from the processor some weeks after the payment, and it takes the money back from the platform. The platform records it as a new event on the original payment with reversing ledger entries, and it either recovers the amount from the shop's balance or deducts it from the next payout. The shop can submit evidence through the platform, and if it wins, another set of entries reverses the loss.

How are shops paid out?

A daily payout job sums each shop's ledger balance for payments that the processor has already settled, and it creates one payout record for that amount. The record carries its own idempotency key, so a retried job cannot pay the same shop twice. The job runs only after reconciliation for that day has finished, so that a payout is never based on payments the processor disagrees with.
