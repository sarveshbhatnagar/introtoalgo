A customer presses "Pay $40" and a second later sees "Thank you". In between, the shop called a payment platform, which called a card processor, which called the card network and the bank. Any call can time out after the money moved. Your system must still know whether the customer was charged, exactly once, and who is owed what.

Most designs trade some correctness for availability; this one does the opposite. The hard parts are uncertainty: retries, lost responses, late notifications and books that must balance. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design.

Clarify the problem

Design a payment system. Are we a platform that shops call, using an external card processor, or are we building the processor itself? A platform. Shops call your API; a third-party processor talks to the card networks. You keep a fee and pay the rest to the shops. How many payments a day, and do we need refunds? About 5 million a day, averaging $40. Yes to refunds. Daily payouts. Then my priorities: never charge twice, never lose a payment, and keep books showing exactly what each shop is owed. If I must choose, I'll reject a payment rather than risk a double charge. "Reject rather than double charge" shows early that you know this design leans towards consistency, unlike a feed or a cache.
  • Functional: charge a card through the processor; report status; refund all or part; keep a ledger of what each shop is owed; reconcile with the processor daily.
  • Non-functional: any number of retries charge at most once; no acknowledged payment is lost; every money movement is auditable for years; answers in about 2 seconds, giving up on the processor after 5; correctness beats availability.
  • Out of scope: fraud detection, currency conversion, the payout bank transfer, storing raw card numbers (the processor gives us a token).

Estimate the scale

  • Payments: 5 million ÷ 86,400 ≈ 58 a second. Plan for 10x on sales days: about 600 a second.
  • Money: 5 million × $40 = $200 million a day. A bug that loses 0.1% costs $200,000 a day, so effort goes to correctness, not throughput.
  • Records: 5 million × 365 ≈ 1.8 billion payments a year; at 1 KB each, about 1.8 TB.
  • Uncertain outcomes: if 1 processor call in 1,000 times out, 5,000 payments a day start with an unknown result. That is a daily case, not an edge case.

600 payments a second is easy for one well-sized relational database. The hard part is making each write exactly right.

Write the endpoints to charge a customer and check a payment. How does the shop retry safely after a timeout?

Create and refund carry a shop-chosen idempotency key. A retry with the same key gets the same payment back, not a new charge.

The decision worth arguing is the processing answer. When the processor does not reply in time, the honest answer is "we don't know yet", and the shop learns the outcome from a webhook or by polling. "Failed" would invite a double charge.

What records do you need for a payment, its history, and the money it moves? Which may be updated, and which only appended?

A payment row with its status (changed only by allowed transitions) and a uniquely constrained idempotency key; an append-only status history; an append-only ledger whose entries sum to zero per transaction.

Model the data

payments
  payment_id      VARCHAR(32) PRIMARY KEY
  merchant_id     BIGINT
  amount_cents    BIGINT
  status          VARCHAR(16)   -- created | processing | succeeded | failed | refunded
  processor_ref   VARCHAR(64) NULL
  idem_key        VARCHAR(64)
  request_hash    CHAR(64)      -- detects a reused key with a new body
  UNIQUE (merchant_id, idem_key)

payment_events                  -- append-only history
  payment_id, seq, from_status, to_status, source, created_at

ledger_entries                  -- append-only, never updated
  entry_id        BIGINT PRIMARY KEY
  txn_id          BIGINT        -- entries of one txn sum to zero
  account         VARCHAR(64)   -- "processor_receivable", "merchant:42"
  amount_cents    BIGINT        -- debit positive, credit negative
  created_at      TIMESTAMP

Store money as integer cents, never floating point, which cannot represent most decimal fractions exactly. The writes are small and transactional: insert a payment with its key, change status only from the right old status, commit ledger lines all or none. That is a relational database with ACID transactions: a single leader with a synchronous replica. 600 payments a second needs transactions, not a key-value store's horizontal growth.

Deep dives

Idempotency keys, end to end

The idempotency lesson covers the pattern. Three details decide whether it works here.

  • Write the key in the same transaction as the payment. With the key in a cache and the payment in the database, a crash between the writes lets a retry slip through.
  • Scope and fingerprint the key. Unique per shop, stored with a hash of the request. The same key with a different amount is a client bug: return 422.
  • Pass a key downstream. Retries to the processor reuse pay_88, so a retry after a lost reply returns the first charge. Every retryable hop needs a key.

Say precisely what "exactly once" means: requests may arrive any number of times, and the charge takes effect once. No network delivers exactly once; the receiver makes repeats harmless.

The payment state machine

A payment is in one state, and only certain moves are allowed. Writing them down turns race conditions into a table you can check.

FromToTriggered by
createdprocessingwe are about to call the processor
processingsucceededprocessor reply, webhook, or a status query
processingfaileda definite decline, or the processor confirms it has no such charge
succeededrefundeda refund for the full amount succeeds

Each move is a conditional update: SET status = 'succeeded' WHERE payment_id = 'pay_88' AND status = 'processing'. If the reply and the webhook race, the second changes zero rows. No late event can drag a succeeded payment backwards; there is no such arrow.

Payments stuck in processing are expected. A sweeper asks the processor about ones older than a minute, by idempotency key. Only "no charge with that key" may move a payment to failed, and even that can race with the original request still in flight. So wait past the processor's own request timeout before trusting it, or resend the charge with the same key: the processor returns the first result or charges once.

Mark a timed-out payment failed and the shop asks the customer to pay again; both charges go through. A timeout means "unknown": stay in processing until the processor says otherwise.

A double-entry ledger

A balance column per shop is easy to update and impossible to audit: when it is wrong, nothing says why. A ledger records every movement as entries that sum to zero. For the $40 payment, with a 3% fee of $1.20:

AccountAmount (cents)Meaning
processor_receivable+4000the processor owes us $40
merchant:42−3880we owe the shop $38.80
platform_fees−120we earned $1.20

+4000 − 3880 − 120 = 0. (Real processors also take a fee; it is left out here.) Three rules make the ledger trustworthy:

  • Every transaction sums to zero. Check before commit. Money only moves between accounts.
  • Append only. Fix a mistake with a reversing transaction, never by editing a row. The history is the audit trail.
  • Balances are derived. A shop's balance is the sum of its entries. You may cache it in a row updated in the same transaction, but the entries are the truth.

Webhooks, retries and reconciliation

The processor sends webhooks at least once, not always in order. The handler verifies the signature, then in one transaction stores the event id under a unique key and applies the event through the state machine. Only then does it return 200. A repeat hits the unique key and changes nothing; a crash before commit sends no 200, so the event comes again. (Storing the id in a separate, earlier step would lose the event on a crash.) Out-of-order events are harmless: illegal moves change nothing.

Retries use exponential backoff, jitter, and the same key. A rule interviewers probe: never retry on a second processor while the first one's outcome is unknown. Keys are not shared between processors, so both could charge.

Reconciliation is the safety net. Each day the processor publishes a settlement file of what it actually charged and refunded; the reconciler matches each line to a payment and its ledger lines by reference and amount.

Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x, 6,000 payments and 18,000 ledger lines a second at peak, one leader strains. Partition by merchant_id, so each shop's transactions stay on one partition. Platform-wide accounts like platform_fees become hot rows every payment touches; give each partition its own copy and sum them for reports.

In every row below, when in doubt, stop taking payments rather than guess: two minutes of lost sales beats a double charge.

An external processor and your database cannot share one atomic transaction. Record intent first (processing), call out with a key, and settle the outcome from the reply, a webhook, a status query or reconciliation.

The first charge may have succeeded, and a retry without the same key charges again: double charges on a normal day. Your call to the processor times out. What do you do? Follow-up questions

How do refunds work?

A refund is its own record, with its own key and states, linked to the payment. On success it writes reverse ledger lines; partial refunds may total up to the original.

Authorize now, capture later?

For goods that ship later, authorize (reserve the funds) now and capture on shipping. Add authorized and captured states; authorizations expire after some days, so a job captures or releases them in time.

How do you pay shops?

Daily, sum each shop's ledger balance, create a payout record with its own idempotency key, and write ledger lines moving the amount to a payouts account. The bank transfer needs the same unknown-outcome care.

How do you store card numbers?

You don't. The browser sends the card to the processor, which returns a token. Keeping card numbers out of your systems shrinks the compliance burden enormously.

Could you use an eventually consistent database?

Not for payments and the ledger: the key insert and status change must be atomic, and a lost acknowledged write is lost money. A shop's dashboard can read a replica a few seconds behind.
