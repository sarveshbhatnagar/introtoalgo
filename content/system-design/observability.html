A checkout API's dashboard shows an average latency of 120 ms, comfortably green. Meanwhile support tickets say "checkout hangs". Ten real requests show why: nine finish in 20 to 43 ms, and one takes 950 ms. The average hides the request users complain about.

Fan-out makes it worse. If a page makes 20 backend calls, each with a 1-in-100 chance of being slow, at least one is slow 1 − 0.9920 ≈ 18% of the time. Nearly one page in five is slow while each backend's average looks fine.

The idea

Observability means answering two questions from the outside: is it broken for users right now? and where and why? Three kinds of data help:

  • Metrics: numbers over time: request rate, error rate, latency percentiles, CPU. They tell you that something changed.
  • Logs: one detailed record per event: "payment 9f2 timed out after 800 ms, attempt 2". They tell you why, for one request. Write structured fields (JSON) so you can search them.
  • Traces: one request's path across services. Each service records a span (name, start, duration) and passes a trace id to the next call in a header. They tell you where the time went.

For each service, start with the RED metrics: Rate, Errors and Duration (latency percentiles). For machines and queues, add saturation: how full the CPU, memory, connection pool or backlog is.

Percentiles, not averages

Sort the ten requests: 20, 22, 24, 25, 26, 28, 30, 32, 43, 950 ms. Their sum is 1,200, so the average is 120 ms, a time no request took. The median (p50) is 27 ms: what a typical user sees. With thousands of requests, the p99 is the latency 99% of requests beat: what your unluckiest regular users feel every day.

Track p50 for the typical case and p99 (sometimes p99.9) for the tail. Collect latency as histograms (counts per latency bucket) so you can combine servers correctly.

From alert to cause

An alert fires, a dashboard narrows it down, a trace finds the slow call, and a log explains it.

SLOs and alerts

A service level indicator (SLI) is a measured ratio of good events: "share of checkout requests that succeed in under 1 second". A service level objective (SLO) is its target: "99.9% over 30 days". The remaining 0.1% is the error budget, the failure you are allowed. While budget remains, ship features; when it runs low, fix reliability.

Alert on symptoms users feel (the SLO burning), not causes that may not matter (CPU at 80%). A useful rule: page a human if the budget burns 14.4 times faster than sustainable over the last hour. At that rate a 30-day budget is gone in 30 ÷ 14.4 ≈ 2 days, and one hour uses 14.4 ÷ 720 = 2% of it. Slower burns open a ticket for working hours.

In the interview

Expect "How would you know it is broken?" near the end of any design. A weak answer lists tools. A strong one starts from the user: name the one or two actions that matter, define an SLI and SLO for each, alert on the SLO, and say how you would find the cause.

Your notification system is live. How do you know it is working? The promise is "a notification arrives within 10 seconds", so my SLI is the share delivered within 10 seconds, with a 99.9% SLO over 30 days. I page when the budget burns fast. Each service also has RED metrics, and the queue has consumer lag, which usually moves first. The SLO alert fires. What then? Dashboards show which stage's lag or latency moved, a few slow traces find the span, then that service's logs for those trace ids explain it. A synthetic check also sends a test notification every minute, so silence is caught even with no traffic. It measures what users experience, ties alerts to it, and has a path from alert to cause.

Follow-ups:

  • "Logs are too expensive. What do you cut?" Sample successful requests, keep all errors, drop debug level in production, and lean on metrics.
  • "How do you catch a total outage with no traffic?" Synthetic checks from outside, and an alert when traffic drops near zero.

Common mistakes

Pages for CPU, disk and every error type teach people to ignore pages, including the real one. Page on user-facing SLO burn; send the rest to dashboards and tickets.

Averaging each server's p99 does not give the fleet's p99. Add the histograms, then compute the percentile.
