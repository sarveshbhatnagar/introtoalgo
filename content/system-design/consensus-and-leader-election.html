A billing scheduler runs on two machines so one can take over if the other dies. Each checks the other with a heartbeat every second and takes over after 5 missed beats. One night the network between them drops for 10 seconds. Each decides the other is dead, both become leader, and both run the nightly billing job. 40,000 customers are charged twice.

Neither machine was wrong given what it could see. Consensus solves this: several machines agree on one answer (who leads, what was written) even when some are slow, dead or cut off.

Why agreeing is hard

A machine that stops answering might be dead, overloaded, paused by garbage collection, or behind a broken link. From outside these look the same; no timeout tells them apart. Clocks drift, and messages arrive late, twice, or never.

With two machines, any rule fails: if each waits for the other's vote, a single failure stops everything; if each acts alone, a partition gives you two leaders (split brain).

The idea: majorities

Use an odd number of nodes, usually 3 or 5, and require a majority for every decision. Any two majorities of the same group share at least one node, so two conflicting decisions can never both get a majority. A cut-off minority simply cannot act.

Raft-style consensus (a simplified picture) builds on that:

  • One leader per term. Terms are numbered 1, 2, 3… A node becomes leader for a term only with votes from a majority, and each node votes once per term.
  • A replicated log. Every change is appended to the leader's log and copied to followers. An entry is committed once a majority has stored it; only then is it applied and acknowledged.
  • Elections on silence. If followers hear nothing from the leader for a randomised timeout (a few hundred ms), one becomes a candidate for the next term and asks for votes. Nodes vote only for a candidate whose log is at least as complete as theirs, so a new leader always has every committed entry.

The cost: every write waits for a majority, and with 2f + 1 nodes you survive f failures. 3 nodes survive 1, 5 survive 2. 4 nodes still survive only 1 (a majority of 4 is 3), so even sizes buy nothing.

One subtlety interviewers like: until it hears of term 4, the old leader may still answer reads from a stale copy. Systems promising fresh reads confirm leadership with a majority first, or use a time-based lease.

Leases and fencing tokens

Most systems do not run consensus themselves. They ask a small consensus-backed coordination service (such as ZooKeeper or etcd) for a lease: "you are leader for the next 10 seconds; renew before then." If the holder dies, the lease expires and someone else gets it.

A lease alone is not enough. A process paused for 15 seconds wakes believing its lease is valid and writes anyway. The fix is a fencing token: a number that grows with every grant, and storage rejects any write carrying a smaller number than one it has seen.

Where it shows up

  • Choosing the single leader of a replicated database, and promoting a new one.
  • Metadata stores: which node owns which shard, cluster membership, configuration.
  • Locks and "exactly one worker does this job" schedulers.

Keep consensus for small, critical metadata, not every user request: a majority round trip on every request costs too much.

In the interview

Mention consensus when something needs exactly one owner (a leader, a lock, a shard map) or when you promise no lost writes on failover. Keep it to a sentence or two unless pushed: "The shard map lives in a consensus-backed store such as etcd, three nodes, so it survives one failure. Workers hold leases and every write carries a fencing token." Interviewers almost never want Raft on the whiteboard.

How do you make sure only one scheduler runs the billing job? Schedulers take a lease from a coordination service with three nodes, so it survives losing one. The lease is 10 seconds and renewed every 3. The holder runs the job. What if the holder freezes and wakes up after the lease has moved? It would still try to write. So each lease carries a fencing token, and the billing store rejects any write with a token lower than the highest it has seen. I also make each charge idempotent per customer and month, so even a duplicate run cannot bill twice. A weak answer stops at "use a distributed lock". The strong one names the paused-process case and two independent defences.

Follow-ups you should expect:

  • "Why three nodes and not two?" Two cannot form a majority after one fails. Three can.
  • "What happens on the minority side of a partition?" It cannot elect a leader or commit, so it refuses writes: consistency over availability.
  • "Isn't lease expiry enough on its own?" No. Clocks drift and processes pause, so a holder can act after its lease ended. Anything that must not happen twice needs fencing.

Common mistakes

Two nodes that each decide alone will both lead during a partition. Use an odd-sized majority, or a coordination service that has one.

A lock tells a process it may act; it cannot stop a process that has paused and lost the lock without knowing. Only the resource checking a token can.
