A photo app saves each upload in a database column. Users add 2 million photos a day at about 3 MB each: 2 × 106 × 3 MB = 6 TB a day. Every replica copies every byte, the nightly backup no longer finishes overnight, and each upload ties up an app server while it streams through.

Databases are good at small rows that change and get queried. Photos are large, written once and read whole. They belong elsewhere.

The idea

An object store (such as Amazon S3 or its look-alikes) keeps blobs of bytes under keys like photos/7f3a9c/original.jpg. You PUT, GET or DELETE objects over HTTP. There are no folders, only key prefixes, and an object is replaced whole, never edited.

It is cheap per gigabyte, scales to petabytes without you managing disks, and is extremely durable. The costs: tens of milliseconds to the first byte, no partial updates, and slow listing of millions of keys. The rule: metadata in the database, bytes in the object store. The row holds owner, size, status and object key.

Objects, files and blocks

The metadata row is ordinary:

id          BIGINT PRIMARY KEY
owner_id    BIGINT
object_key  TEXT        -- "photos/7f3a9c/original.jpg", generated, never the user's filename
size_bytes  BIGINT
status      TEXT        -- pending | ready | deleted
created_at  TIMESTAMP

Uploading without touching the bytes

Relaying uploads through app servers is a common mistake: 6 TB a day is about 6 × 1012 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 70 MB/s of wasted work, and slow mobile uploads hold connections for minutes. Instead, hand the client a pre-signed URL: a link to one object key, signed with the server's credentials, allowing one action for a short time. The client sends bytes straight to the store.

Multipart upload for big files

A 2 GB video over 20 Mbps takes 2 × 109 × 8 bits ÷ 2 × 107 bits/s = 800 seconds, about 13 minutes. Failing at 90% wastes 12 minutes. Multipart upload splits it into parts, here 2 GB ÷ 100 MB = 20. Each part is its own request, can go in parallel, and retries alone: a failure costs one part, 100 MB at 20 Mbps = 40 seconds. A final "complete" call stitches the parts into one object. Add a lifecycle rule to clean up unfinished uploads, or their parts keep costing money.

Durability: copies or erasure coding

Object stores survive disk and rack failures in two ways:

  • Replication: 3 full copies on different machines. 3x the raw space; simple, fast to read and rebuild.
  • Erasure coding: split an object into, say, 10 data fragments plus 4 parity fragments, spread across disks. Any 10 of the 14 rebuild it, so it survives 4 losses at 14 ÷ 10 = 1.4x the space. But rebuilding one fragment reads 10 others, and small objects pay overhead.

Stores often replicate hot or small objects and erasure-code large, colder ones.

Numbers worth remembering

  • First-byte latency: tens of milliseconds, far more than a row read. Put a CDN in front of anything popular.
  • Replication: 3x raw space. Erasure coding 10 + 4: 1.4x, survives 4 losses.
  • Upload time = size in bits ÷ link speed: 1 GB at 10 Mbps is 8 × 109 ÷ 107 = 800 seconds.

In the interview

Bring in blob storage for images, video, documents or any file over a few hundred kilobytes. Say where bytes and metadata go, and that uploads bypass your servers.

How do users upload photos? The API writes a pending row and returns a pre-signed URL for one key, valid 15 minutes. The client uploads straight to the object store, so 70 MB/s of photos never touch our servers. An upload event triggers thumbnails and marks the row ready. What if the upload succeeds but the event is lost? The row stays pending. A sweeper checks old pending rows: if the object exists, it finishes processing; if not, it deletes the row. The status column is how we notice the two stores disagreeing. It keeps bytes off the app path and plans for drift between the stores.

Follow-ups to expect:

  • "How do you serve private photos?" Keep the bucket private; after checking permissions, hand out short-lived pre-signed GET URLs or signed CDN URLs.
  • "How do you delete?" Mark the row deleted so it vanishes at once, then delete the object and purge the CDN asynchronously.

Common mistakes

Two users upload IMG_0001.jpg and one overwrites the other. Generate the key; keep the original filename in the metadata row.
