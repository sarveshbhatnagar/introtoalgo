Four candidates get the same prompt: "Design the like button for a social app, with a count under every post." All four draw a working system. One is hired as a new grad, one as mid-level, one as senior, and one is turned down for staff and offered senior. The difference was never whether the design worked. It was how much of the session they led, how deep they went, and how far past the boxes they looked.

Interviewers calibrate against a level. Knowing what the bar is for yours tells you where to spend your 40 minutes.

The idea

The scorecard does not change by level: problem exploration, design, trade-offs, depth, communication, as in how the interview works. What changes is how much you are expected to do without being asked. A simplification, since companies define levels differently, but a useful one:

  • Junior (new grad, early career): a correct, simple design that works end to end, with help. You ask sensible questions, know the basic blocks, and respond well to hints.
  • Mid-level: you drive the structure. You estimate, justify each component, and handle one deep dive well when it is pointed out.
  • Senior: you drive the whole session. You find the hard part yourself, go deep on two or three areas, argue trade-offs with costs and conditions, and bring up failure before you are asked.
  • Staff: everything senior, plus judgement about the problem itself: which requirements to challenge, what not to build, what it costs, how it evolves, and who else depends on it.

One question, four answers

The scenario numbers: 50 million likes a day, and a count shown on every post view, about 100 views for every like. Over a day of about 100,000 seconds that is 50 million ÷ 100,000 = 500 likes a second on average (the exact figure is nearer 580), and 500 × 100 = 50,000 count reads a second. Staff candidates also close the gaps. If the service crashes between the insert and the publish, a like is stored but never counted, so a nightly job recounts popular posts from the like rows and corrects drift. And they put a number on growth. A like row is about 50 bytes, so 50 million a day × 365 is about 18 billion rows, near 1 TB a year. Small today, but a reason to decide retention and partitioning now rather than in year three.

How to practise for your level

Whatever your level, practise one level up in one area. A mid-level candidate who raises a failure mode unprompted, once, stands out. Mock interview practice has prompts and a way to run sessions.

In the interview

Ask the level if you do not know it, and ask what they want to see: "Should I focus on the overall design or go deep in one area?" Then make the level-defining moves out loud, so they are scored:

  • Mid: "Reads are 100 times writes, so I'll cache counts and accept a few seconds of staleness."
  • Senior: "The part I'm worried about is one viral post. Let me go there."
  • Staff: "Before I optimise this, do we need exact counts? If we show 12K, approximate is fine and much cheaper."
The design looks complete. Anything else? I think that covers it. Is there a part you'd like me to go into? Correct but passive. At senior level the interviewer wanted you to pick the hard part yourself. The design looks complete. Anything else? Two things. A viral post puts 5,000 updates a second on one count row, so I'd move counting to an aggregator that writes once a second. And if the aggregator is down, likes are kept but counts freeze; I'd alert on queue backlog so we notice in minutes. Finds the hard part, fixes it with a number, and closes with failure and detection.

Follow-ups by level that you should expect:

  • Mid: "Why that database?" The five-part answer from talking through trade-offs.
  • Senior: "What breaks at 10 times the traffic?" Name the first bottleneck and its number, then the fix.
  • Staff: "How would you roll this out?" Behind a flag, dual-writing old and new counts, comparing them, then switching reads; say how you would roll back.

Common mistakes

Naming more technologies does not raise your level; it gives the interviewer more to probe. Seniority shows in what you choose to leave out and in the reasons you give.

Talking about team ownership and migration at minute 10, before the like path reaches a database, reads as avoiding the work. Earn the wide view with a working design first.
