Four candidates get the same prompt: "Design the like button for a social app, with a count under every post." All four draw a working system. One is hired as a new grad, one as mid-level, one as senior, and one is turned down for staff and offered senior. The difference was how much of the session they led, how deep they went, and how far past the boxes they looked. Know the bar for your level and you know where to spend your 40 minutes.

The idea

The scorecard is the same at every level: problem exploration, design, trade-offs, depth, communication. What changes is how much you do without being asked. Companies define levels differently, but roughly:

  • Junior (new grad, early career): a simple, correct design that works end to end, with help. Sensible questions, the basic blocks, good use of hints.
  • Mid-level: you drive the structure. You estimate, justify each component, and handle one deep dive well when pointed to it.
  • Senior: you drive the whole session. You find the hard part yourself, go deep on two or three areas, argue trade-offs with costs, and raise failure before you are asked.
  • Staff: all of senior, plus judgement about the problem itself: which requirements to challenge, what not to build, what it costs, how it evolves, and who depends on it.

One question, four answers

The scenario numbers: 50 million likes a day, and a count shown on every post view, about 100 views for every like. Over a day of about 100,000 seconds that is 50 million ÷ 100,000 = 500 likes a second on average (the exact figure is nearer 580), and 500 × 100 = 50,000 count reads a second.

Staff candidates also close gaps. If the service crashes between insert and publish, a like is stored but never counted, so a nightly job recounts popular posts from the like rows. And they put a number on growth: at about 50 bytes a row, 50 million a day × 365 is about 18 billion rows, near 1 TB a year. Small today, but a reason to decide retention and partitioning now, not in year three.

How to practise for your level

Whatever your level, practise one level up in one area. A mid-level candidate who raises a failure mode unprompted, once, stands out.

In the interview

Ask the level if you do not know it, and what they want: "Should I focus on the overall design or go deep in one area?" Then make the level-defining moves out loud so they are scored:

  • Mid: "Reads are 100 times writes, so I'll cache counts and accept a few seconds of staleness."
  • Staff: "Do we need exact counts? If we show 12K, approximate is much cheaper."
The design looks complete. Anything else? I think that covers it. Is there a part you'd like me to go into? Correct but passive. A senior picks the hard part themselves. The design looks complete. Anything else? Two things. A viral post puts 5,000 updates a second on one count row, so I'd move counting to an aggregator that writes once a second. And if the aggregator is down, likes are kept but counts freeze; I'd alert on queue backlog so we notice in minutes. Finds the hard part, fixes it with a number, and closes with failure and detection.

Expect these follow-ups:

  • Mid: "Why that database?" The five-part answer.
  • Senior: "What breaks at 10 times the traffic?" Name the first bottleneck and its number, then the fix.
  • Staff: "How would you roll this out?" Behind a flag: dual-write old and new counts, compare, then switch reads. Say how you would roll back.

Common mistakes

Naming more technologies does not raise your level; it gives the interviewer more to probe. Seniority shows in what you leave out and the reasons you give.

Team ownership and migration at minute 10, before the like path reaches a database, reads as avoiding the work. Earn the wide view with a working design.
