A shop's only database server loses its disk at 14:00. The last backup is from 02:00: 12 hours of orders are gone. Restoring the 2 TB backup at 200 MB/s takes 2 × 1012 ÷ 2 × 108 = 10,000 seconds, nearly 3 hours of downtime.

With a second server receiving every change live, the same failure costs under a second of writes and a minute of downtime.

The idea

Replication keeps copies of the data on several machines (replicas), shipping each change through a log. You get durability (a dead disk loses nothing), availability (another replica takes over), and read capacity (reads spread across copies).

The cost: for a while after each write, replicas disagree. Most replication decisions are about how long that gap is and who can see it.

Leader and followers

One leader accepts all writes and streams its log to followers, which apply it in order. Reads can go to any of them.

Synchronous or asynchronous

  • Synchronous: the leader waits for a follower's confirmation before replying "saved". Failover loses no acknowledged write, but each write pays a round trip and stalls if that follower is down.
  • Asynchronous: writes never wait, but failover loses whatever had not shipped, as in the tracer.

The usual middle ground: one synchronous follower, the rest asynchronous. If the synchronous one fails, another takes its role.

Three ways to arrange replicas

Quorums: why R + W > N

In a leaderless store each key lives on N replicas. A write succeeds once W confirm; a read asks R and keeps the highest version. If R + W > N, every read set overlaps every write set, so a read always reaches the latest successful write. That needs a strict quorum: if stand-in nodes accept writes while owners are down (a "sloppy" quorum), reads can miss the write until it is handed back.

R + W > N guarantees overlap, not the behaviour of one up-to-date copy (linearizability). A quorum does not stop:

  • Concurrent writes to one key: both succeed; the store must merge or pick one.
  • A write that reached fewer than W replicas reports failure but may still be read later.
  • Mid-write, one reader sees the new value and a later reader the old.

Numbers worth remembering

  • Lag within a region: well under a second when healthy; seconds to minutes for an overloaded follower.
  • A synchronous write adds one round trip: about 0.5 ms in a data center, 50 to 150 ms between continents.
  • Async failover loses about write rate × lag. Detection takes seconds; shorter timeouts replace leaders that were only slow.

In the interview

Bring up replication for "must not lose data", "highly available", or reads that swamp one server. Say which topology, sync or async, and how reads after a write behave.

Your database is a single point of failure. Fix it. Leader-follower with two followers: one synchronous, so an acknowledged write is always on two machines, one asynchronous. Followers spread our 20,000 reads a second. A user edits their profile and sees the old one. Why? Their read hit a lagging follower. I'd send a user's reads to the leader for a few seconds after they write. Others seeing it 300 ms late is fine. It names the guarantee and who needs it.

Follow-ups to expect:

  • "How do you avoid two leaders after failover?" Elect leaders through a consensus service with term numbers; storage rejects writes with an old term (fencing). See consensus and leader election.
  • "Isn't replication a backup?" No: a bad DELETE replicates in milliseconds. Keep point-in-time backups too.

Common mistakes

R + W > N gives overlap, not conflict resolution. Name your rule for concurrent writes; last-writer-wins silently drops one.
