You type "pizza" into an app and see twenty places within a couple of kilometres, best first, each with stars and a distance. You tap one, read reviews, and later leave your own. Think Yelp.

"Near me" turns plain CRUD into a spatial index, a query mixing location with text, a read path for hundreds of reads per write, and a rating that stays right when two people review at once. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design. Try each "Your turn" box before reading on.

Clarify the problem

Design a service that finds businesses near a user, with reviews. Users search by location, category and keywords, open a page, and write a star review. Can one user review a business twice? One review per user per business. They can edit it. How big? How many businesses, users and reviews? 50 million businesses, 100 million daily users searching about 5 times a day, 2 million new reviews a day. Reads dwarf writes; search should return in about 200 ms. New data may take seconds to reach search, but the rating must never lose a review. I'll skip photos, ads and owner tools. Two freshness needs: search may lag, the rating total may not be wrong. That split drives the design.
  • Functional: search near a point by category and keywords, with filters (open now, price, minimum rating); view a business page with reviews; add or edit one 1-to-5-star review per business.
  • Non-functional: search under 200 ms at p99; reads highly available; new data searchable within a minute; the rating counts every review exactly once.
  • Out of scope: photos (see Design Photo Sharing), ads, owner dashboards, fake-review detection.

Estimate the scale

A day is 86,400 seconds; the evening peak is 3 times the average.

  • Searches: 100 million × 5 = 500 million a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 5,800 a second, about 17,000 at peak.
  • Business page views: say 2 per search, 1 billion a day ≈ 11,600 a second, about 35,000 at peak.
  • Review writes: 2 million a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 23 a second, about 70 at peak. Reads outnumber writes about (500 million + 1 billion) ÷ 2 million = 750 to 1.
  • Storage: 50 million businesses × 2 KB = 100 GB. Say 1 billion existing reviews × 1 KB = 1 TB, growing 2 million × 1 KB × 365 ≈ 730 GB a year.

Write the search, business page and review endpoints. How does the API make "one review per user" natural and safe to retry?

Search is a GET with a point, radius, keywords and filters, paged with a cursor. The review is a PUT keyed by business and user, so writing it twice equals writing it once.

Define the API

GET /search?lat=40.7128&lng=-74.0060&radius_m=2000
           &q=pizza&category=restaurants&open_now=true&min_rating=4&cursor=...
  200 { "results": [ { "id": "b_81", "name": "Luigi's", "rating": 4.6,
                       "review_count": 312, "distance_m": 450 }, ... ],
        "next_cursor": "..." }
  400 Bad Request   radius over 50 km, or no location

GET /businesses/{id}                   page data, cacheable for 60 s
GET /businesses/{id}/reviews?sort=recent&cursor=...

PUT /businesses/{id}/reviews/me        create or replace my review
  body: { "rating": 5, "text": "..." }
  200 { "rating": 5, "business_rating": 4.61, "review_count": 313 }
  400 Bad Request   rating not 1 to 5
  404 Not Found     no such business
DELETE /businesses/{id}/reviews/me

The decision worth arguing is PUT .../reviews/me over POST .../reviews. A retried POST tries to add a second review. A PUT names this user's one review, so a retry just replaces it. "One review per user" becomes the URL's shape, backed by a primary key.

Sketch businesses and reviews. Where does the rating live, and what answers "places near this point"?

A relational database: reviews keyed by (business, user), a running star sum and count on the business row. A search index, fed by the database's change stream, answers location and text queries.

Model the data

businesses
  business_id   BIGINT PRIMARY KEY
  name          VARCHAR(200)
  categories    TEXT[]            -- ["restaurants", "pizza"]
  lat, lng      DOUBLE
  hours         JSON              -- weekly opening intervals
  price_level   SMALLINT
  rating_sum    BIGINT            -- total stars of all reviews
  rating_count  INT               -- number of reviews
  -- about 2 KB per row

reviews
  business_id   BIGINT
  user_id       BIGINT
  rating        SMALLINT          -- 1 to 5
  text          TEXT
  updated_at    TIMESTAMP
  PRIMARY KEY (business_id, user_id)   -- one review per user, enforced

Access patterns: search near a point (17,000 a second at peak); read a business and its reviews (35,000 a second); write a review and update the rating (70 a second).

A review write updates two rows in one transaction, so the source of truth is a relational database, partitioned by business_id when it outgrows one machine; a business and its reviews share a partition. Search, geometry plus text plus ranking, goes to a search index with geo support (see search systems), built from the database.

Sketch a search, a page view and a review write. How does a new review reach search?

Three services behind a gateway. Search reads only the index; page views read through a cache; reviews write to the database in one transaction. A change stream updates the index seconds later.

High-level design

Each path gets its own service and store. The database sees only page misses and 70 writes a second.

Deep dives

The geospatial index

An index on latitude finds a thin band around the whole planet, and the database checks every business in it. You need an index that groups points by area. Design Ride Sharing introduces geohash cells and neighbour cells; here the points are businesses, which almost never move.

Interviewers still ask how hand-built versions work. With geohash, pick the precision from the radius. A 3 × 3 block covers any circle whose radius is no bigger than one cell's shorter side. A precision-6 cell is about 1.2 km × 0.6 km, too small for 2 km. A precision-5 cell is about 4.9 km square at the equator, so 9 cover 2 km. Width shrinks with latitude: at 50° north it is about 3.1 km, still enough, but compute from the real size.

A quadtree with at most 100 places per leaf needs at least 500,000 leaves; with 1.2 GB of coordinates it fits in one server's memory. Build it offline, ship it to every search server, and overlay new places until the next rebuild. The strong answer: "I'd use the engine's geo field, and here is how it works inside."

A pizza place 50 metres away can sit in the next cell, with a different prefix. Searching one cell misses it. Search the neighbours too, then filter by exact distance, since 9 cells cover far more than the circle.

Serving 750 reads for every write

The 25 GB index fits on every search machine. Do not shard it; add replicas. If one handles about 1,000 queries a second (measure yours), the 17,000 peak needs 17, so run about 20. Each query touches one machine, with no scatter-gather.

Business pages go through a cache: the top 10% of 100 GB is about 10 GB. Each review commit deletes the page's key; a 60-second expiry covers a lost delete. Cache search results for 60 seconds keyed by precision-6 cell, words and filters, so one cell's "pizza" searches share an entry; recompute distances for the exact point.

Keeping the average rating correct

Luigi's has 10 reviews totalling 40 stars, average 4.0. Ana gives 5 stars and Ben gives 1 at the same moment. The right result is 46 stars over 12 reviews, about 3.83. If each request reads the row, computes in the app and writes back, both read (40, 10). Ana writes (45, 11); Ben writes (41, 11). The row ends at 11 reviews averaging 3.73: Ana's review is lost. This lost-update race is the classic mistake.

BEGIN;
-- 1. lock the business row: reviews of this business now take turns
SELECT 1 FROM businesses WHERE business_id = 81 FOR UPDATE;
-- 2. my old review, if any
SELECT rating FROM reviews WHERE business_id = 81 AND user_id = 7;  -- 3, or none
INSERT INTO reviews (business_id, user_id, rating, text, updated_at)
     VALUES (81, 7, 5, '...', now())
ON CONFLICT (business_id, user_id)
     DO UPDATE SET rating = 5, text = '...', updated_at = now();
-- 3. new review: +5 stars, +1 review.  edit from 3 to 5: +2 stars, +0.
UPDATE businesses SET rating_sum = rating_sum + 2, rating_count = rating_count + 0
 WHERE business_id = 81;
COMMIT;

Why it is safe: every review of business 81 first locks its row, so they take turns, and step 2 sees the previous commit (at read committed, as in PostgreSQL, each statement reads fresh data). The primary key blocks a second review by one user even under a race. Store the sum and count, never the average: an average cannot take a delta.

Mixing words, filters and location

"Wood-fired pizza, open now, 4+ stars, within 2 km" has three parts. Separate steps fail: location first fetches thousands of downtown places to keep 20; text first finds every pizza place on Earth.

Use one index query: filters cut candidates, words match the inverted index, and the engine scores what is left. A simple score multiplies text relevance by a distance decay and a rating factor, so a 4.8-star place at 1.5 km can beat a 4.1-star place at 300 m. "Open now" compares one number: opening intervals are stored as minutes of the week in local time, and the server converts "now" to the area's local time.

Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x, 170,000 searches a second need about 200 index replicas: split them into regional clusters, each holding its region's index. 700 writes a second still fit a partitioned relational database. Repeated dinner-time queries from one cell hit the result cache.

What a strong answer sounds like

LevelWhat the answer does
JuniorFilters a lat/lng table with a distance formula; accepts a spatial index when asked about 50 million rows.
MidUses geohash or a quadtree with neighbour cells, separates the search index from the database, caches pages.
SeniorReplicates the in-memory index instead of sharding, combines geo, filters and text in one query, keeps ratings exact with sum and count, and says which data may lag.
How would you shard the geo index? At first I wouldn't. It's about 25 GB, so every node holds a full copy and a query touches one machine; about 20 replicas cover 17,000 searches a second. If it outgrew memory, I'd shard by region, since a search almost never crosses a continent, and give big-city shards more replicas. And if you had to build the spatial part yourself? A quadtree with about 100 places per leaf, built offline and shipped to every node, since places barely move. For 2 km I'd collect leaves touching the circle, then filter by exact distance. Data size first, then a shard key and its cost.

Follow-up questions

Fewer than 20 results within 2 km. What then?

Widen the radius in steps, 2 km, 5, 10, until you have enough or hit a limit. A quadtree walks to neighbouring leaves.

The user pans the map. How do you search a rectangle?

Send the map's corners instead of a point and radius; geo fields filter by box. Cap the box size and show clusters when zoomed out.

Should a place with one 5-star review rank above one with 300 reviews at 4.7?

No. Rank by an adjusted rating that pulls small samples toward the site average, such as (sum + 10 × site average) ÷ (count + 10). Show the real average on the page.

Search shows Luigi's at 4.60 but the page shows 4.61. Is that a bug?

No. Search reads a copy that lags by seconds; the page's cache is cleared on every review. Both totals are exact.

A review is removed as fake. What changes?

Hide it in the same kind of transaction: subtract its stars and 1 from the count. A nightly job recomputes sums to catch drift.
