A guest types "Lisbon, 12 to 15 June, 2 guests", scrolls a few hundred places, opens ten, and books one. Hotels list rooms, people rent out homes, and the site must say which are free on those exact nights.

It is ticket booking with rooms for seats, and the same rule: never sell one thing twice. But a stay covers several nights, so one booking claims several rows. And there is no on-sale spike; people search hundreds of times per booking, filtering by availability that keeps changing. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design; try each "Your turn" box before reading on.

Clarify the problem

Design a site where people book hotels and holiday homes. Both hotels with many rooms of one type and homes that are a single unit? Does the guest book instantly, or does the host approve? Both. Instant booking only. How many properties and bookings a day? How much do people browse before booking? About a million properties and 500,000 bookings a day. Call it 100 searches and 200 listing views per booking. So reads outnumber bookings 300 to 1. Priorities: never confirm two guests for the last room, never charge without a booking, keep search fast even if seconds behind. Search is a hint; booking is the truth. The candidate sees the write rate is tiny and says where staleness is allowed (search) and where not (booking). That split drives the design.
  • Functional: search by place, dates and guests; view a listing with photos, prices and a calendar; book one or more rooms of a type for a date range; pay; cancel.
  • Non-functional: no night sold beyond its limit (strong consistency on inventory); search under 300 ms, may lag bookings by seconds; listing pages under 200 ms; holds last 15 minutes; a confirmed booking is never lost.
  • Out of scope: reviews, host payouts, guest-host messaging, price setting, loyalty programmes.

Estimate the scale

A day has 86,400 seconds; assume the peak hour runs at 5 times the average.

  • Bookings: 500,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 6 a second, about 30 at peak. One database primary is enough.
  • Searches: 500,000 × 100 = 50 million a day, ≈ 600 a second, about 3,000 at peak.
  • Listing views: 500,000 × 200 = 100 million a day, 1,200 a second, 6,000 at peak.
  • Bookings stored: 500,000 × 365 ≈ 180 million a year, 180 GB at 1 KB each.
  • Photos: 1 million properties × 20 photos × 500 KB = 10 TB, in object storage behind a CDN.

Reads need the machines; bookings need the care.

Write endpoints for search, a listing page and booking. How do you write a stay's dates, and what does booking return when a night just sold out?

Search and listings are GETs; booking POSTs a held reservation, and a pay call confirms it. A stay is check-in and exclusive check-out, in local dates. A sold-out night returns 409.

Write a stay as two plain dates in the property's calendar, no time zone, covering the nights from check-in up to but not including check-out: 12 to 15 June is the nights of the 12th, 13th and 14th. UTC timestamps invite off-by-one bugs; midnight in Lisbon is not midnight on the server.

The listing page is two calls: the static part caches for minutes; availability and price change with every booking.

Sketch room types, inventory and reservations. How does a three-night booking claim all three nights at once?

A row per room type per night with a total and a reserved count. One transaction adds to every night where it fits the limit, and commits only if every night changed.

Model the data

room_types
  property_id    BIGINT
  room_type_id   BIGINT
  name           VARCHAR(100)   -- "Double room, sea view"
  max_guests     SMALLINT
  total_units    SMALLINT       -- 1 for a home
  PRIMARY KEY (property_id, room_type_id)

inventory                        -- one row per room type per night
  property_id    BIGINT
  room_type_id   BIGINT
  night          DATE           -- local date at the property
  total_units    SMALLINT
  overbook_limit SMALLINT       -- extra units the hotel allows; 0 for homes
  reserved       SMALLINT       -- held + confirmed
  price_cents    INT
  PRIMARY KEY (property_id, room_type_id, night)

reservations
  reservation_id BIGINT
  property_id    BIGINT
  room_type_id   BIGINT
  user_id        BIGINT
  check_in       DATE
  check_out      DATE           -- exclusive
  rooms          SMALLINT
  status         VARCHAR(10)    -- held | paying | confirmed | expired | cancelled
  held_until     TIMESTAMP
  total_cents    INT
  idem_key       VARCHAR(64) UNIQUE
  PRIMARY KEY (property_id, reservation_id)

A hotel sells a room type, not room 214; the front desk picks the room at check-in. So inventory is a count per type per night; a home has total_units = 1.

A booking changes several rows of one property all-or-nothing: a relational database with transactions. Every key leads with property_id, so a later partition by property never splits a booking. A guest's trip list is a separate table by user_id, written after commit.

Sketch search, listings and booking. Where does search get availability, and how does it learn of new bookings?

A search index holds listings and their sold-out nights, fed by the inventory change stream. Listing pages come mostly from a CDN. A booking service is the only inventory writer and calls the payment processor.

High-level design

Deep dives

Reserving several nights at once

A three-night stay is three rows that must all have room. Claim them in one atomic step, or a guest gets a booking with a hole in it.

BEGIN;
UPDATE inventory
   SET reserved = reserved + 1
 WHERE property_id = 501 AND room_type_id = 42
   AND night >= '2027-06-12' AND night < '2027-06-15'
   AND reserved + 1 <= total_units + overbook_limit;
-- rows changed must be 3 (the nights 12, 13, 14). Fewer: ROLLBACK, return 409.
INSERT INTO reservations (..., status, held_until)
     VALUES (..., 'held', now() + interval '15 minutes');
COMMIT;

Why it is safe: an updated row stays locked until commit; a second transaction wanting it waits. In PostgreSQL at read committed, the waiting update then re-checks its WHERE, sees the night is full and skips it; the row count is short and you roll back. At stricter isolation it fails with a serialization error, also a 409 (see transactions and isolation). Overlapping stays can deadlock on shared nights; the database aborts one, and you retry once or lock nights in date order.

A stay from the 12th to the 15th uses three nights, not four. Writing night <= check_out blocks a night the guest never sleeps, so a guest arriving on the 15th sees the room as taken. Use a half-open range everywhere: claim, release, search filter and price total.

Searching by availability

Each room type's document in the search index lists its sold-out nights. A room type fits if none of the stay's nights is listed: one "must not match" filter beside a geo filter (see Design Proximity Search). The change stream updates the list only when a night's free count reaches or leaves zero, in log order per room type, so a late "free" never overwrites a newer "sold out".

The list only knows "at least one free", so a family wanting three rooms may see a hotel with one left. Search rechecks the 25 results it will show in one batched replica read (25 room types × 3 nights = 75 rows) and drops failures.

Listing pages stay on a CDN for five minutes, purged when the host edits; the calendar reads a replica. None of the 6,000 views a second reach the primary.

Search results and calendars are copies, and copies lag. They decide what to show; only the booking transaction decides what to sell.

Holds, payment and expiry

Reserve the nights, then charge; charging first leaves a paid guest with no room when the claim fails. The flow is ticket booking's: a hold, a paying state the sweeper never touches, and an idempotent charge (Design a Payment System handles unknown results). Two things differ:

  • Expiry needs a writer. A seat row carries its own held_until, so an expired hold reads as free. A count cannot say which unit is stale, so the sweeper releases it: UPDATE reservations SET status = 'expired' WHERE id = 88002 AND status = 'held' AND held_until < now(), and only if that changed one row, subtract from the nights in the same transaction. The status check makes reruns safe.
  • Authorize now, capture later. Many stays are free to cancel until a deadline, so booking authorizes the card (holding the funds) and captures later. Authorizations lapse after days, so for a stay months ahead, save a card token and authorize nearer the date.

Overbooking on purpose and by accident

Some guests never arrive. A 100-room hotel with about 5 no-shows per 100 bookings may sell 104. overbook_limit lets each hotel set this per room type (0 for homes), and the platform enforces it exactly. The hotel decides, because it pays when a guest must go elsewhere.

Accidental overbooking is the bigger risk. Hotels often sell the same rooms on several sites, synced with lag, so two sites can each sell the last room in the same minute, and no transaction of yours can stop it. Push bookings out within seconds, and plan for the rest: relocate the guest to a comparable room and pay the difference.

Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x, 30,000 searches a second, the index needs work first: shard it by region (every query names a place) and add replicas. For 30,000 rechecks a second, add inventory replicas or an availability cache fed by the change stream. Bookings at 300 a second still fit one primary; partition by property_id for headroom.

How does search know which hotels are free? It joins the listings table with the bookings table and filters out anything with an overlapping booking. Fine for 100 listings. At 3,000 searches a second it loads the booking database, and it ignores rooms with several units. How does search know which hotels are free? The index keeps each room type's sold-out nights, updated from the change stream within seconds, and excludes anything sold out during the stay. I recheck the 25 results I show on a replica. Booking checks again in a transaction, so a stale result costs a retry, never a double booking. Why not keep the free count per night in the index? Follow-up questions

Prices change per night. How do you show the total?

Sum the stay's nightly prices and copy the total onto the reservation at hold time, so a mid-checkout price change does not alter what the guest agreed to.

How would you add request-to-book, where the host approves?

The same hold with a longer expiry, say 24 hours, plus a card authorization. Accept confirms; a decline or timeout releases both.

A hotel takes 5 rooms out for repairs. What happens?

Lower total_units on those nights in one transaction. If reserved now exceeds the limit, cancel nobody automatically; the extra bookings go to relocation.
