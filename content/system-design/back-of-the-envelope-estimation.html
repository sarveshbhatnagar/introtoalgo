A candidate designing a photo-sharing app is told "10 million daily users, 30 requests each". They spend four minutes dividing 300 million by 86,400 to get 3,472.2 requests a second, then draw the design they would have drawn for 30 or for 300,000. The interviewer learned nothing.

Estimation has one job: find the numbers big enough to change the design. Get the order of magnitude in under four minutes, out loud, then say "so...": so it fits on one machine, so it needs a CDN, so we must partition.

The handful of facts you need

Almost every estimate uses these conversions. Memorize them.

210 = 1,024103thousandKB

The powers drift apart (240 is about 10% more than 1012), which never matters here. Use 1 KB = 1,000 bytes.

  • A day is about 100,000 seconds (really 86,400), so your rates come out about 14% low. Say so once and move on.
  • A month is about 2.5 million seconds (really 2.6 million); a year about 30 million (really 31.5 million).
  • 1 million a day is about 10 a second. 1 billion a month is about 400 a second.
  • Sizes: an ASCII character is 1 byte, an integer or timestamp 8, a UUID 16, a small database row a few hundred. Photo sizes and server capacity come from the interviewer or your stated assumptions.

Latency figures live in numbers to know.

From daily users to servers

Daily users × actions per user gives requests a day. Divide by 100,000 for the average rate, multiply by a peak factor, and divide by what one server handles.

Storage, bandwidth and memory

  • Storage = items a day × bytes each × days kept × copies.
  • Bandwidth = requests a second × bytes per response.
  • Cache memory = hot items × bytes each.

Links are quoted in bits a second, storage in bytes: multiply bytes by 8.

How precise to be

Be careful near a real limit. If peak is 900 a second and one server handles 1,000, rounding matters: "That is close to one server's limit, so I'll plan for two or more."

In the interview

Estimate after clarifying, before the API (see the framework). Ask for inputs, state assumptions, and compute only what can change the design: usually peak rate, storage, and bandwidth or cache size.

  • "I'll round a day to 100,000 seconds, so my rates are a bit low."
  • "That's 1 GB a second of images, so images go through a CDN, never the API servers."
Assume 10 million daily users. How much traffic is that? At about 30 requests each, 300 million a day: 3,000 a second, and I'll assume 9,000 at peak. About 13 API servers at 70% load. So the API tier is not the hard part. Then what is? The photos. 5,000 views a second at 200 KB is 1 GB a second out, and a petabyte a year in. The design is mostly blob storage and the CDN. Every number leads to a decision, and shows where to spend the rest of the hour.

Expected follow-ups:

  • "Where does a peak factor of 3 come from?" An assumption about daily patterns. One time zone, a live event or a launch raises it.
  • "How sure are you?" "Within a factor of 2 or 3, enough to choose the architecture."

Common mistakes

Computing storage, bandwidth, memory and servers, then saying nothing about them, wastes four minutes. End each estimate with the decision it drives, or skip it.

Systems fall over at peak, not at average. Storage without replication is a third of the disk you will buy. Apply both, out loud.
