You want a pair of headphones, but not at $79.99. You paste the link into a price tracker, set "tell me under $65", and forget it. Three weeks later your phone buzzes: $64.99. The tracker shows every price that product has had for two years.

The hard part is a budget: the site has 500 million product pages and you may fetch only a few hundred a second. You must choose what to look at, borrow observations from users, and never send a false alert. Think Keepa, tracking one large store. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design; try each "Your turn" box before reading on.

Clarify the problem

Design a price tracker for a large e-commerce site. Where do prices come from: an official API, or product pages? How fast may we fetch? No price API. You fetch pages, and you must stay under 500 requests a second. How many products exist, and how many do our users track? About 500 million products. 10 million users have 50 million alerts on about 20 million distinct products. A browser extension with 2 million installs can report the prices users see. Then I can't watch everything often. I'll spend fetches where alerts are close to firing and use the extension to fill gaps. Priorities: never alert on a price that wasn't real, send each drop once, keep full history. How fast must an alert follow a drop? Within a couple of hours for popular products is fine. The candidate found the real constraint, the fetch budget, before drawing. "A false alert is worse than a late one" drives the crowdsourcing design.
  • Functional: track a product by link; alert at a target price or percentage drop; show a price-history chart; notify once when the price reaches the target; accept extension reports.
  • Non-functional: under 500 fetches a second; popular products checked every 2 hours, all tracked ones daily; no alert on an unverified price; one alert per drop; chart under 200 ms.
  • Out of scope: other stores, price predictions, product search, user accounts.

Estimate the scale

  • Fetch budget: 500 × 86,400 seconds = 43.2 million fetches a day.
  • Observations: 43.2 million fetches plus about 30 million extension reports ≈ 73 million a day.
  • Price changes: if 1 observation in 10 shows a new price, about 7.3 million a day, 85 a second. A small alert pipeline.
  • Alerts: 50 million × about 100 B = 5 GB. One database.

Sketch the price history and alert records. How does the chart tell "the price did not change for a month" from "nobody looked for a month"?

Store history as segments: a price, when first seen, when last confirmed. A gap between one segment's last confirmation and the next one's start is time nobody looked. Alerts are rows indexed by product and target price.

Model the data

price_segments                       -- one row per run of the same price
  product_id     VARCHAR(16)
  first_seen     TIMESTAMP
  last_seen      TIMESTAMP           -- moved forward by each matching observation
  cents          INT
  source         VARCHAR(8)          -- crawl | crowd (verified)
  PRIMARY KEY (product_id, first_seen)

alerts
  alert_id       VARCHAR(16) PRIMARY KEY
  user_id        BIGINT
  product_id     VARCHAR(16)
  target_cents   INT
  state          VARCHAR(8)          -- armed | fired
  fired_change   VARCHAR(16) NULL
  INDEX (product_id, state, target_cents)

crawl_state
  product_id     VARCHAR(16) PRIMARY KEY
  next_due       TIMESTAMP
  priority       REAL
  last_verified_cents INT

History is written in time order and read as "one product, one time range": a wide-column or time-series store keyed by (product_id, first_seen), where a chart is one range scan. Alerts need "armed alerts on P-77 with a target at or above $64.99", an index range scan in a relational database. Crawl state is a priority queue in the crawler.

Sketch how prices arrive, where they are checked and stored, and how a change becomes a notification.

Fetches and crowd reports feed one price service, which writes history and emits an event only when a verified price changes. An alert matcher turns events into notifications.

High-level design

Not drawn: the API servers write alerts to the alerts database, and the crawler reads alert counts from it to set priorities. Both arrows into the price service pass through a log partitioned by product_id, so one instance handles each product's observations in order, and two copies of one drop cannot both emit an event.

Deep dives

Spending the fetch budget

The crawler is the one from Design a Web Crawler, except its frontier is a priority queue ordered by next_due, with each product's interval set by how much users care. A token bucket holds the fleet to 500 fetches a second. One plan for 43.2 million a day:

TierProductsIntervalFetches a day
Hot: most trackers, or an alert within 5% of the price1 million2 hours1M × 12 = 12 million
Tracked19 million1 day19 million
Untracked, for history and new products480 millionabout 40 daysthe remaining 12.2 million

Tiers simplify a score: more trackers, an alert near the price, a volatile price and a long time since the last look all raise priority. An alert 40% below the price needs no fetch every 2 hours; one 2% away does. Each verified extension report moves next_due forward and saves a fetch, mostly in the hot tier, since people view the pages they care about.

Trusting crowd reports

Extension reports are free but come from strangers. Some are wrong by accident (a member price, a coupon, a used offer, a misread page); some on purpose. The rule: a crowd report can extend a price or ask for a check; it cannot alone make a big change.

  • The extension parses the crawler's fields (seller, condition, currency); anything but the public new price is dropped.
  • A change of up to 10% from a trusted install is accepted.
  • Anything bigger, or from a new install, waits for a second install on a different network to report the same price within an hour, or for a priority fetch.
  • Trust rises when an install's reports later agree with fetches and falls when they do not. Each install is rate limited.

Firing alerts on any report is fastest, but one bad report then reaches every tracker of that product, and you cannot unsend a notification. Only verified changes produce events.

Storing and charting history

Prices are mostly flat, so store segments: a new row only when the price changes; a matching observation moves last_seen. That is the estimate's 10x saving, and it keeps the chart honest: if last_seen is 10 days before the next segment, the chart shows a gap, not a flat line nobody checked.

A one-year chart for a daily-changing product is 365 segments, one range scan. For "all time" on an hourly-changing product, read precomputed daily rollups (low, high, last), so no chart reads more than about a thousand rows. Keep the all-time low in a per-product summary row.

A price is $79.99 until the moment it is $64.99. Joining the two points with a slope suggests prices like $72 that never existed. Draw steps.

Alerting once per drop

Duplicates come from three places:

  • Two sources see the same drop. Only a change from the last verified price emits an event, so the second observation emits nothing.
  • The pipeline redelivers. The log delivers at least once. Each notification carries the key alert_id + change_id, and the notification system drops keys it has sent. The alert's fired_change column lets the matcher skip work already done.
  • The price flaps. $64.99, $65.01, $64.99 is two drops below $65 by the letter of the rule. A fired alert re-arms only when the price climbs 5% above the target, or when the user asks.

A popular product can match 200,000 alerts at once. The matcher pages through the (product_id, state, target_cents) index in batches. At 2,000 sends a second, the last user hears 200,000 ÷ 2,000 = 100 seconds after the first.

Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x users the fetch budget stays fixed; the site sets it. Intervals stretch, and the extension, which grows with users, matters more. History grows by less than 10x, because prices do not change more often. Even the worst case, about 850 GB a year, shards easily by product_id.

Follow-up questions

How would you support many stores?

Each store gets its own budget, token bucket, parser and frontier; the rest does not care where a price came from. Matching one product across stores is a separate problem.

How do you detect fake drops, where the price is raised before a "sale"?

The history shows it. Compare the "sale" price with the 90-day median, not the day before, and label the drop honestly in the alert.

Why not just fetch tracked products more often from many IP addresses?

The budget is the site's limit. Evading it breaks the site's terms and gets you blocked. Coverage comes from the extension and better priorities.

How do you know the system is healthy?

Track the age of the last verified price per tier, parse failures, the share of crowd reports that fetches contradict, and change-to-alert time. Rising contradictions often mean a layout change, not cheaters.
