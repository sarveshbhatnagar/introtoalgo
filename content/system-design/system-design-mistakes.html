A candidate is asked to design a meeting-room booking tool for one office: 400 employees, maybe 50 bookings an hour. Forty minutes later the board holds a sharded database, a stream processor, three caches and a multi-region failover plan. The feedback says "no hire". None of it was needed, and they never asked how many people would use it.

Most candidates fail on habits, not knowledge, and each habit has a fix you can practise.

Before you draw

1. Skipping requirements

Looks like: the first box goes up right after the prompt. The interviewer sees no scoping, and you have no facts to justify anything later. Fix: spend the first few minutes on features, scale and priorities, written where the interviewer can see. Requirements and scope has the questions.

2. Designing for scale you did not establish

Looks like the meeting-room story: sharding for about one booking a minute. Fix: let the numbers decide: "This fits on one database with a replica; here is the number at which I'd shard." Knowing when not to scale is a strong signal.

3. No numbers

Looks like: "it's a lot of traffic, so we'll need caching." Fix: a 3-minute estimate of peak requests a second, storage and one more key figure, each ending in a decision. See estimation and numbers to know.

4. Reciting a memorised design

Looks like: asked for a chat app for hospital staff, you give a consumer chat design from a video, with global fan-out and no word on message retention. Fix: reuse patterns, not whole answers, and say which requirement makes this prompt different.

While you design

5. Drawing in silence

The interviewer scores only what they hear. Fix: narrate each decision, rejected ones too: "I considered a queue here, but the user needs the result, so it's a direct call."

6. Buzzword stacking

Looks like: "We'll put Kafka in front, Redis beside it, Cassandra behind, all on Kubernetes." Each name is a claim you must defend. Fix: name the category and the reason ("a durable log, because three consumers read the same events"), and add components only when a requirement asks. See talking through trade-offs.

7. Going deep too early

Looks like: 15 minutes on the perfect ID scheme while the read path still does not reach a database. Fix: have a simple design working end to end by about minute 27 of 45, then go deep where the interviewer points.

8. Not driving

Looks like: finishing each part and asking "what should I do next?" From mid level up, interviewers score whether you lead. Fix: state the plan up front, and at each pause propose the next step: "The hard part is the hot-key problem, so I'd go there unless you'd rather see storage."

9. Ignoring failure

Looks like: a design that works only when every box is up. Fix: walk the boxes and say what happens when each one dies, with a number.

Under pressure

10. Defending a choice after a hint

"Is a database write on every click a problem?" is rarely neutral. Looks like: "No, databases are fast." Fix: assume a hint points at a flaw, say what breaks with a number, and fix it.

11. Promising guarantees you cannot keep

Looks like: "the queue gives us exactly-once delivery." When an acknowledgement is lost, a message gets delivered twice. Real systems offer at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent consumer, so a repeat has no extra effect: exactly-once processing. Fix: name the real guarantee and the mechanism. See idempotency and retries.

12. Hand-waving the hard part

Looks like: "the database handles the concurrency" for a seat booking, the one place the interviewer wanted depth. Fix: at the part that makes this problem hard, slow down: name the race, show the rows, say which lock or condition prevents it.

You spot a flaw at minute 30 and freeze or rush past it. Noticing is a good signal: name it, patch it, move on.

In the interview

Practise these sentences until they are automatic:

  • "Before I draw, a few questions on features and scale."
  • "Next I'd go into the hard part, which here is double bookings."
  • "Let me walk each box and say what happens when it dies."
Design a meeting-room booking tool for our office. How many people and rooms, and is it one office? And is the main risk two people booking the same room at the same time? One office, 400 people, 30 rooms. Yes, double bookings are the complaint today. Then scale is small, about a booking a minute: one service and one relational database with a replica. The interesting part is preventing overlapping bookings for a room, so I'll spend most of our time there. Scope, a number, a right-sized design and a plan for depth, in under two minutes.

Expect these follow-ups:

  • "What if it grows to 100 offices?" 100 × 50 an hour is about 80 bookings a minute, still small. Say what you would watch.
  • "What are you least sure about?" Name your weakest part and how you would test it. Never say "nothing".
