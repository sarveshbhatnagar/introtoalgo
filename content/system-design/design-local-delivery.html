It is 9 pm and you are out of milk. An app shows only what can reach you within the hour; you add milk and snacks and pay. A driver brings them from one of hundreds of small company warehouses a few kilometres away. Think Gopuff.

It mixes three skills. "Near me" means travel time, not distance. The catalogue must read stock fast across several warehouses. And an order must take the last carton of milk exactly once, across a whole basket. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design. Try each "Your turn" box before reading on.

Clarify the problem

Design a service that delivers groceries within an hour from local warehouses. Customers see what is available near them and order; we own the warehouses and stock. Does an order ship from one warehouse? Prefer one. Splitting costs a second driver. How many warehouses, items and customers? 500 fulfilment centres, each with about 5,000 of our 20,000 products. 5 million daily users, 1 million orders a day. Then: never confirm an order we cannot fill, show only what really arrives within the hour, keep browsing fast. The catalogue may lag stock by a second or two; the order must be exact. I'll skip dispatch, picking and restocking. The candidate names the truth (the order transaction) and the copy (the catalogue) before drawing. That is the shape of the design.
  • Functional: for an address, show products deliverable within the hour; order a basket; reject or adjust an order if an item just sold out.
  • Non-functional: catalogue pages under 200 ms; availability at most a few seconds stale; never oversell (a confirmed order has its stock reserved); one region's failure does not stop others.
  • Out of scope: driver dispatch and tracking (see Design Ride Sharing), picking and packing, restocking, pricing.

Estimate the scale

  • Catalogue pages: say 20 per daily user: 5 million × 20 = 100 million a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 1,200 a second; evenings are about 4 times busier, about 4,600 at peak.
  • Orders: 1 million a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 12 a second, about 46 at peak: one order every 10 seconds or so per centre.
  • Inventory: 500 centres × 5,000 products = 2.5 million rows × 100 bytes = 250 MB. It fits in any server's memory.

Write the catalogue and order endpoints. Who chooses the centre, app or server? What does an order return when an item just sold out?

The app sends the address; the server chooses the centre. A sold-out item returns 409 with what is missing and the alternatives, so the customer fixes the basket in one tap.

The decision worth arguing is that the server picks the centre. An app sending a centre id could order from one that has closed or is now 45 minutes away in traffic; the address lets the server apply today's travel times and status. An idempotency key stops a retry creating a second order.

Sketch centres, stock and orders. How would you partition stock so a 15-item order is one local transaction?

Stock rows keyed by (centre, product) with on-hand and reserved counts, in a relational database partitioned by region. An order ships from one centre in one region, so its rows sit on one partition.

Model the data

centres
  centre_id PK, region_id, lat, lng, status    -- open | paused | closed

cell_centres                         -- precomputed, key-value
  cell        CHAR(6)                -- geohash of a ~0.75 km² area
  time_bucket SMALLINT               -- morning | midday | evening rush | night
  centres     JSON                   -- [["C1", 12], ["C2", 24]]: minutes away
  PRIMARY KEY (cell, time_bucket)

inventory                            -- partitioned by region_id
  region_id, centre_id, product_id
  on_hand     INT                    -- physically on the shelves
  reserved    INT                    -- promised to confirmed orders
  PRIMARY KEY (centre_id, product_id)
  CHECK (reserved <= on_hand)

orders                               -- same regional partition as inventory
  order_id PK, idempotency_key UNIQUE, region_id, customer_id, centre_id,
  status,     -- reserved | paid | picking | out | delivered | cancelled
  items JSON, total_cents, created_at

Available stock is on_hand - reserved. An order reserves at checkout; the shelf count drops only when a picker scans the item, so it always matches the shelf.

An order changes several rows that must succeed together, so inventory goes in a relational database with transactions. Partition by region, a metro area with a few dozen centres: an order touches one centre, so no transaction crosses partitions, and the order row commits with its reservations. Partitioning by product would need a distributed transaction per basket. Product names, photos and prices rarely change and go through a cache and a CDN.

Sketch the read path for a catalogue page and the write path for an order. Where does each get stock from?

An availability service answers pages from an in-memory stock copy fed by the change stream. An order service runs the reservation on the region's partition. Both use a precomputed cell-to-centres table.

High-level design

Reads serve 460,000 lookups a second from a copy; orders take 46 a second against the truth. Neither slows the other.

Deep dives

Mapping a customer to centres by travel time

A centre 3 km away across a river may be 30 minutes out; one 6 km away along a highway may be 10. The promise is about time, so "near" means travel time. With about 15 minutes for picking and handover, a 30-minute driving limit leaves margin inside the hour.

The table is small. A precision-6 geohash cell is about 1.2 km × 0.6 km ≈ 0.75 km² near the equator (smaller further from it; see Design Proximity Search). Say each centre serves about 50 km², roughly a 4 km radius in city traffic. 500 centres cover at most 25,000 km² ÷ 0.75 ≈ 33,000 cells; at 100 bytes each, 3.3 MB. With 3 centres per cell and 4 time buckets, a nightly rebuild is 33,000 × 3 × 4 ≈ 400,000 route calculations, a modest batch job.

Live conditions are flags read per request, not rebuilds: a centre paused because its pickers are swamped, or closed for the night, is skipped. That gives the team a lever during a rush.

A fast availability read across centres

For each of 50 products, a page needs the best of the customer's 2 or 3 centres. The truth is in the inventory database, but 460,000 lookups a second should not land on the database that takes orders.

Each server loads a snapshot at start-up, notes its stream position, and applies deltas from there: a database replica built from the change stream. For products whose counts drift with breakage and mis-scans, show them available only above a small safety buffer. Show a count ("only 2 left") only if you accept it is sometimes wrong for a second.

Ordering without overselling

A 15-item basket must reserve all 15 or none. Otherwise the customer pays for a bag with a hole in it, or a half-finished order locks 14 items away from others.

BEGIN;
UPDATE inventory AS i
   SET reserved = i.reserved + b.qty
  FROM (VALUES ('p_17', 1), ('p_42', 2), ...) AS b(product_id, qty)  -- 15 rows
 WHERE i.centre_id = 'C1' AND i.product_id = b.product_id
   AND i.on_hand - i.reserved >= b.qty;
-- rows changed = 15: INSERT the order, COMMIT.
-- fewer than 15:    ROLLBACK, and report which products fell short (409).
COMMIT;

Why it is safe: each changed row stays locked until commit. A second order for the same product waits, then, at read committed in databases such as PostgreSQL, re-checks its WHERE against the new values and skips the row; the count comes up short and it rolls back. At stricter isolation it gets a serialization error, handled the same way (see transactions and isolation). Two baskets locking shared products in different orders can deadlock; the database aborts one and the service retries it once. The CHECK (reserved <= on_hand) is a last defence against bugs.

The copy said oat milk was in stock for both Ana and Ben. Trusting it and decrementing would promise one carton twice. The copy decides what to show; only the conditional update decides what to sell.

The rest follows ticket booking: reserve, authorize the card, confirm. An unpaid reservation expires after a few minutes: a sweeper moves the order from reserved to cancelled only if still reserved, releasing its stock in the same transaction. It first asks the payment processor by idempotency key, in case the card was authorized just before a crash. If a picker finds a shelf empty despite the count, offer a substitute or refund and correct the count; no transaction fixes a miscounted shelf.

Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x, 46,000 pages and 460 orders a second, availability servers scale by adding copies. Orders are 460 ÷ 500 ≈ 1 a second per centre, still easy. The first strain is a hot product: a promotion queues many orders on one row's lock. Cap quantity per order, and if needed split the item's stock into several rows picked at random, as in ticket booking's general-admission answer.

What a strong answer sounds like

LevelWhat the answer does
JuniorUses straight-line distance, reads the main database per page, decrements items one at a time.
MidUses travel time, caches availability, and reserves the basket in one transaction with a conditional update.
SeniorPrecomputes travel time per cell, sees all stock fits in memory and copies it to read servers from a change stream, partitions by region so orders are local transactions, and separates the copy that shows from the truth that sells.
How would you shard the inventory database? By region. An order ships from one centre in one region, so every basket is a local transaction. Sharding by product would spread a 15-item basket over up to 15 shards and need a distributed commit per order. What's the cost? Regions are uneven, so a big metro needs a bigger partition, and a nationwide query, like one product's total stock, fans out. I'd serve those from an analytics copy.

Follow-up questions

No single centre has the whole basket. What then?

Offer choices in the 409: move to a farther centre with a later time, drop the missing items, or split into two deliveries for a fee. A split is two transactions on two centres in one region, releasing the first if the second fails.

How do you show a delivery time on the page?

The cell table's minutes for the chosen centre, plus that centre's picking backlog, published every few seconds.

A customer is right at the edge of a centre's area. Do they see it?

Only if their cell's travel time is under the limit. Cells make the edge fuzzy by a few hundred metres; add a small margin rather than routing per address.

How do you handle restocking deliveries?

A transaction adds to on_hand; the change stream carries it to the copies within seconds.

Could you reserve stock when an item goes into the cart?

It avoids checkout surprises, but abandoned carts would hide stock from customers ready to pay. Reserve at checkout, and warn in the cart when the copy shows an item ran out.
