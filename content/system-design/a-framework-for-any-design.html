Two candidates know the same material. One hears "design a pastebin" and jumps between caching, sharding and the API. The other clarifies, estimates, then designs, with time left for depth. The second passes, on order alone: a fixed order means you never skip a step and the interviewer can follow you.

The seven steps

Every case study on this site uses these steps as headings. Each step feeds the next: estimates decide the data store, access patterns the keys. Minutes assume a 45-minute slot (see how the interview works).

StepMinutesWhat you produce
1. Clarify the problem5Functional and non-functional requirements; what is out of scope.
2. Estimate the scale4Requests a second (average, peak), storage, bandwidth.
3. Define the API3The main endpoints, with error cases.
4. Model the data3Records, their lookups, the kind of store.
5. High-level design10A diagram where reads and writes both work.
6. Deep dives10Two or three hard parts: options and a choice.
7. Bottlenecks and failures5What breaks at 10 times the load, or when a part dies.

5 + 4 + 3 + 3 + 10 + 10 + 5 = 40 minutes. In a 60-minute slot, give most of the extra time to steps 6 and 7.

Worked example: a pastebin

A pastebin stores text and gives it a link. Here is the framework at spoken length.

1. Clarify the problem

Design a service where people paste text and share a link to it. Can anyone with the link read a paste? Can pastes expire? Is there a size limit? Accounts or editing? Yes. Expiry is optional. Up to 1 MB. No accounts or editing. How many pastes a day, and how often is each read? About 5 million new pastes a day, averaging 10 KB. Ten reads per paste.
  • Functional: create a paste (up to 1 MB, optional expiry) and get a link; read it by the link.
  • Non-functional: a paste is never lost; reads are fast (under 100 ms) and highly available; links are hard to guess.
  • Out of scope: accounts, editing, search, syntax highlighting.

2. Estimate the scale

Round a day to 100,000 seconds (really 86,400; see estimation).

  • Writes: 5 million ÷ 100,000 = 50 a second; assume 3 times at peak: 150.
  • Reads: 10 × 50 = 500 a second, 1,500 at peak.
  • Bandwidth out: 500 × 10 KB = 5 MB/s, 15 MB/s at peak.

3. Define the API

POST /pastes
  body: { "content": "...", "expires_in_days": 7? }
  201 Created   { "id": "x7Kp2QaZ", "url": "https://paste.example/x7Kp2QaZ" }
  413 Content Too Large   over 1 MB

GET /pastes/{id}
  200 OK   { "content": "...", "created_at": "...", "expires_at": "..." }
  404 Not Found   no such paste, or it has expired

4. Model the data

Two records, both looked up only by id:

  • Metadata: about 200 bytes (id, size, timestamps, content pointer) in a key-value or relational store: 5 million × 200 B = 1 GB a day.
  • Content: up to 1 MB of unchanging text, in blob storage under pastes/{id}.

The id is 8 random characters from 62 letters and digits: 628 ≈ 2.2 × 1014 possibilities. After five years (9 billion pastes) a new id clashes about 1 time in 24,000, so insert-if-absent and retry. But then 1 random guess in 24,000 finds some paste; against scrapers, use 10 characters (about 1 in 90 million) and rate-limit 404s.

Much less total data would change the choice; the first quiz question tries one case.

7. Bottlenecks and failures

At 10 times the load (15,000 reads a second at peak), add stateless servers and cluster the cache. Metadata grows 10 GB a day, so partition it by id; random ids spread evenly. Failures: a dead cache node sends reads to the stores, the metadata database fails over to a replica, a blob outage stops creates.

In the interview

Say the plan once, then name each step as you reach it:

  • "Before drawing, I'll pin down features and scale."
  • "Storage grows 18 TB a year, so I'll keep content out of the database."
  • "The design works end to end. Shall I go deeper on content storage or id generation?"

Expected follow-ups:

  • "Can we skip the estimates?" Yes. Ask only "roughly how much data?"
  • "Skip to the hard part." Go. The framework is a default, not a script.

Common mistakes

Saying "now estimates" and then ignoring the numbers is ritual. Each step must change something later: here 18 TB a year moved content to blob storage.
