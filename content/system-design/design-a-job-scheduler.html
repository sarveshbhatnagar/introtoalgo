"Send this user a reminder in 24 hours." "Retry this upload in 5 minutes." "Build the sales report every day at 06:00 in Tokyo." A team that runs its own timer on one server eventually loses jobs when it restarts. A shared scheduler stores each job safely and makes sure some worker runs it close to the right time.

"Run it later" hides three hard problems: finding the few due jobs among millions, handing each to one worker when workers crash and pause, and building "exactly once" from retries and idempotent jobs. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design.

Clarify the problem

Design a distributed job scheduler. One-off jobs, recurring cron schedules, or both? And do we run the job's code, or hand it to the team's workers? Both kinds. Teams run their own workers; you store, schedule and dispatch. How many jobs, and how precise must the timing be? About 100 million runs a day and a million recurring schedules. Starting within a few seconds of due time is fine. Then I'll promise: an accepted job is never lost, starts within 10 seconds of its due time under normal load, and runs at least once. It can run twice, so handlers must be idempotent. Workflows with dependencies are out of scope. Naming "at least once" up front shows you know exactly-once delivery is not on the menu.
  • Functional: schedule one-off jobs at a time or after a delay; recurring schedules from a cron expression and time zone; cancel; see status and attempts; retry with backoff up to a limit, then park for a human.
  • Non-functional: no accepted job is lost; jobs start within 10 seconds of due time under normal load; at-least-once execution; no single machine can stop scheduling.
  • Out of scope: running job code ourselves, job dependencies and workflows, priorities beyond a few named queues.

Estimate the scale

  • Job runs: 100 million ÷ 86,400 ≈ 1,200 a second on average.
  • Writes: created, enqueued, claimed, finished: 4 writes a run, about 4,800 a second. Too many for one primary at peak, so shard.
  • Waiting jobs: if 10% are scheduled a week ahead and the rest within the hour, 100 million × 0.1 × 7 days = 70 million wait at once; at 1 KB each, 70 GB. A week of finished jobs adds 100 million × 7 × 1 KB = 700 GB.
  • The burst: if 20% of the million schedules say "every hour, on the hour", 200,000 jobs are due in the same second at every :00.

Where 1,800 comes from: 1,200 jobs a second × 2 seconds each = 2,400 running at once, or 240 workers at 10 jobs each. With 50% headroom, 360 workers do 360 × 10 ÷ 2 = 1,800 a second.

Write the calls to schedule, cancel and inspect a job, and for workers to get and finish work. Push or pull?

Create a job, create a schedule, cancel, get. Workers pull: they get a lease, heartbeat while running, and report the result with their attempt number.

The decision to argue is pull, not push. Pushing means tracking each worker's capacity, and slow workers back up into the scheduler. Pulling workers take only what they can run; a backlog waits in the queue. The attempt number in every worker call lets the scheduler reject a worker that lost its lease.

Design the job record and its key. How do you make "which jobs are due now?" small among 70 million waiting?

Two records: the job, found by id, and a small "due" index keyed by (shard, minute bucket of the run time) holding job ids. A dispatcher reads just the current minute's partition, a few thousand rows.

Model the data

jobs                            -- one row per job, found by id
  job_id         VARCHAR(64) PRIMARY KEY   -- random, or "s_311@2026-11-15T06:00" for a schedule run
  shard          SMALLINT       -- hash(job_id) % 64
  queue          VARCHAR(64)
  payload        BLOB           -- up to 64 KB; larger goes to blob storage
  run_at         TIMESTAMP
  status         VARCHAR(12)    -- scheduled | enqueued | running | succeeded | cancelled | dead
  attempt        INT
  lease_until    TIMESTAMP NULL -- indexed for running jobs, for the lease sweep
  schedule_id    VARCHAR(32) NULL

due                             -- the timer: which ids are due when
  shard          SMALLINT
  bucket         TIMESTAMP      -- run_at rounded down to the minute
  run_at         TIMESTAMP
  job_id         VARCHAR(64)
  PRIMARY KEY ((shard, bucket), run_at, job_id)

schedules
  schedule_id, cron, time_zone, queue, payload, on_overlap, on_missed, next_run_at

A job and its due rows hash to the same shard, so creating a job is one local transaction. A retry or new run time just adds a due row; the dispatcher checks the job's status before enqueuing, so stale rows from cancelled or rescheduled jobs are skipped. Each dispatcher records, per shard, the last due row it handled, so a restart or takeover resumes there.

Any store with partitions, sorted keys within them and per-row conditional writes ("only if attempt is still 2") fits: a sharded relational database with due partitioned by time, or a wide-column store such as Cassandra. I would pick the relational database, where claims and leases are cheap conditional updates; the wide-column store wins if writes grow far past this.

Sketch the boxes: where jobs live, what finds due jobs, how they reach workers, and how two scheduler machines avoid scanning the same shard.

An API writes to the sharded store. Dispatchers own shards via a coordination service, scan the current bucket every second and put due ids on per-type ready queues. Workers pull ids and claim jobs in the store.

High-level design

Deep dives

Finding due jobs

Of seventy million waiting jobs, about 1,200 become due each second. Find those cheaply, every second, forever.

With 64 shards, the 200,000-job burst is 200,000 ÷ 64 ≈ 3,125 rows per partition: one quick read each. A dispatcher that falls behind reads missed buckets first, so a slow minute delays jobs but never loses them.

Leases and at-least-once execution

A worker that claims a job gets a lease until lease_until, extended by heartbeats. If the worker dies, the lease runs out and the job is handed out again. So execution is at least once: no job is stuck forever, but a worker that only looked dead may still finish.

A short lease (30 seconds here) recovers fast but re-runs jobs on any worker that pauses longer; a long one means fewer duplicates but slower recovery. Heartbeats keep the lease short even for hour-long jobs.

Shard ownership is the same idea one level up: dispatchers lease shards from a consensus-based coordination service (see consensus and leader election). When a stalled dispatcher's lease expires, another takes over, and briefly two may scan one shard. That is safe because every step is conditional: only one can move a job to enqueued, and a duplicate id finds the job already running and is dropped.

Marking a job done before running it gives at-most-once: a crash after the mark silently loses the job. Run first, mark done after, and make the repeat harmless.

The exactly-once illusion

"Exactly once" here can only mean exactly-once effect. A scheduler that re-runs jobs from dead workers must sometimes re-run jobs from live ones, like W3. Say so plainly, then give the tools:

  • Idempotent handlers. Pass a key derived from the job (its id, or the caller's key) to every downstream call (see idempotency and retries).
  • Fencing tokens. Every write carries the attempt number, and the target rejects older attempts. The store does this for status; a job can do it for its outputs.
  • Natural idempotency. "Set the 2026-11-15 report to this file" can run twice; "append a row to the report" cannot.

Cron schedules versus one-off jobs

Store a schedule as a definition, not a million future jobs. When an occurrence is claimed, create the next, with an id built from the schedule id and its time, so insert-if-absent turns two dispatchers creating the 07:00 run into one row. Three policies need a deliberate answer:

  • Overlap: the 06:00 run is still going at 07:00. Skip, queue, or run both? Default to skip for reports.
  • Missed runs: the scheduler was down from 02:00 to 05:00. Run once, run all three, or skip? Default to once.
  • Time zones: compute the next time in the schedule's own zone. On a daylight-saving change, 02:30 can happen twice or not at all; decide which, and test it.

For the :00 burst, let schedules say "hourly, any minute" and pick a stable minute by hashing the schedule id. The 200,000 jobs spread to about 200,000 ÷ 3,600 ≈ 56 a second, and the existing fleet keeps the promise.

Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x, a billion runs a day (about 12,000 a second), grow the store from 64 to 512 shards, split dispatcher ownership finer, and expect the burst to dominate. Ready queues are per job type, so one team's flood delays only that team.

What a strong answer sounds like

LevelWhat the answer does

Can you guarantee each job runs exactly once? Yes. I'd take a distributed lock on the job id before running it, so only one worker can have it. A lock with an expiry is a lease with the same hole: a paused worker keeps running after it expires. This promises what no design can give. Can you guarantee each job runs exactly once? Not execution. I can't tell a dead worker from a paused one, so I re-run the job, and sometimes both finish. I guarantee at least once, plus tools for exactly-once effect: attempt numbers as fencing tokens, and a stable key per job to pass downstream. So who is responsible for duplicates? Shared: the scheduler keeps duplicates rare and visible; job owners make handlers idempotent. I'd document that on day one.

Follow-up questions

How do you cancel a job that is due in a minute?

A conditional update sets cancelled only while the job is scheduled or enqueued. Claims check status, so a cancelled id already queued is dropped. Once running, cancelling is a request the job may honour.

How do retries work?

A failed attempt gets a new run time (now plus exponential backoff with jitter) and a new due row. After max_attempts it goes to a dead-letter status for a human, so a broken job cannot retry forever.

One team schedules 10 million jobs for midnight. What protects everyone else?

Per-queue dispatch rate limits and separate ready queues per team. Their jobs start late; everyone else's stay on time.

How would you add priorities?

Give each priority its own ready queue; workers pull high before low. Keep priority out of the store's key, so finding due jobs stays one partition read.

How do you know the scheduler is healthy?

Track scheduling lag (start minus due time) per queue, the oldest due job not yet enqueued, lease expiries per minute and dead letters. Alert on lag; users feel it.
