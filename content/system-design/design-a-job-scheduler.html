A job scheduler is a shared service that lets other teams say "run this job at this time" or "run this job on this schedule", for example a reminder email in 24 hours, a retry in 5 minutes or a report every day at 06:00. The scheduler stores each job durably and makes sure a worker starts it close to the requested time. Without one, each team keeps timers on its own servers, and those jobs are lost when a server restarts.

The question is hard for three reasons: finding the few jobs that are due among millions that are waiting, giving each due job to one worker even though workers crash and pause, and getting the effect of "exactly once" from a system that can only promise to run a job at least once. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design.

Clarify the problem

Design a distributed job scheduler. Do you want one-off jobs, recurring cron schedules, or both? And does the scheduler run the job's code, or does it hand the job to workers that each team runs? Both kinds of jobs. Teams run their own workers, and you store, schedule and dispatch the jobs. How many jobs do we expect, and how precise does the start time have to be? About 100 million runs a day and a million recurring schedules. Starting within a few seconds of the due time is fine. Then I will promise three things. An accepted job is never lost, it starts within 10 seconds of its due time under normal load, and it runs at least once. Because a job can run twice, handlers have to be idempotent. Saying "at least once" at the start shows that you know exactly-once execution is not on offer.
  • Functional requirements: schedule one-off and recurring (cron) jobs, cancel a job, show its status and attempts, and retry a failed job with backoff up to a limit.
  • Non-functional requirements: no accepted job is lost, jobs start within 10 seconds of their due time under normal load, execution is at least once, and no single machine can stop the scheduling.
  • Out of scope: running the job code, job dependencies and workflows, and priorities.

Estimate the scale

  • Job runs: 100 million a day divided by 86,400 seconds is about 1,200 runs a second on average.
  • Writes: each run is written four times (created, enqueued, claimed and finished), which is about 4,800 writes a second. One primary cannot take that at peak, so the store is sharded.
  • Waiting jobs: assume 10% of jobs are scheduled a week ahead and the rest within the hour. Then 100 million × 0.1 × 7 days = 70 million jobs are waiting at any moment, which is 70 GB at 1 KB each.
  • The burst: if 20% of the million schedules say "every hour, on the hour", then 200,000 jobs become due in the same second at every :00.

For the worker fleet, assume each job takes 2 seconds and a worker runs 10 jobs at a time. The average load is 1,200 × 2 = 2,400 jobs running at once, which is 240 workers. With 50% headroom the fleet is 360 workers, which can start 360 × 10 ÷ 2 = 1,800 jobs a second.

The decision to argue is pull instead of push. With push, the scheduler has to track each worker's capacity, and a slow worker backs up into the scheduler. With pull, each worker takes only what it can run, and the backlog waits in the queue. The attempt number in every worker call lets the scheduler reject a worker that has already lost its lease.

Design the job record and its key. Then decide how to make the question "which jobs are due now?" cheap when 70 million jobs are waiting.

The job, found by its id, and a small "due" index keyed by shard and minute of the run time. A dispatcher reads only the current minute's partition.

Model the data

jobs                            -- one row per job, found by id
  job_id         VARCHAR(64) PRIMARY KEY   -- random, or "s_311@2026-11-15T06:00" for a schedule run
  shard          SMALLINT       -- hash(job_id) % 64
  queue          VARCHAR(64)
  payload        BLOB           -- up to 64 KB; larger payloads go to blob storage
  run_at         TIMESTAMP
  status         VARCHAR(12)    -- scheduled | enqueued | running | succeeded | cancelled | dead
  attempt        INT
  lease_until    TIMESTAMP NULL -- indexed for running jobs, used by the lease sweep
  schedule_id    VARCHAR(32) NULL

due                             -- the timer: which job ids are due when
  shard          SMALLINT
  bucket         TIMESTAMP      -- run_at rounded down to the minute
  run_at         TIMESTAMP
  job_id         VARCHAR(64)
  PRIMARY KEY ((shard, bucket), run_at, job_id)

schedules
  schedule_id, cron, time_zone, queue, payload, on_overlap, on_missed, next_run_at

A job and its due rows hash to the same shard, so creating a job is one local transaction. A retry or new run time only adds a due row, and the dispatcher checks the job's status before enqueuing, so stale rows from cancelled or rescheduled jobs are skipped. Each dispatcher records the last due row it handled in each shard, so a replacement resumes from there.

Any store with partitions, sorted keys and conditional single-row writes works, for example a sharded relational database. I would pick that, because claims and leases are simple conditional updates there.

High-level design

Deep dives

Finding the jobs that are due

About 70 million jobs are waiting and about 1,200 of them become due every second. The scheduler has to find those jobs cheaply, every second, without reading the rest. There are four ways to do it.

One table with an index on the run time. All jobs sit in one table, and several pollers run a query such as WHERE run_at <= now() ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, which lets each poller take different rows. This works at a few hundred jobs a second. At our scale finished rows bloat the index, and 4,800 writes a second land on one primary.

Time-bucketed partitions. The due table is partitioned by shard and by the minute of the run time, so each scan reads one small partition and old buckets are dropped whole. The costs are that shard ownership has to be managed so that two dispatchers do not scan one partition, and that precision is limited by the one-second scan interval.

An in-memory timer wheel. A dispatcher loads the next few minutes of due rows into memory and fires jobs from there, which gives millisecond precision. A crash loses the memory, so the next owner has to reload the window from the store. It is worth adding only for sub-second precision.

Delayed delivery in a message queue. Many queues can hold a message back until a given time. Delays are usually capped, and it is hard to list, change or cancel waiting messages, so this fits short delays such as retry backoff but not jobs scheduled a week ahead.

I would choose time-bucketed partitions. With 64 shards the 200,000-job burst is about 3,125 rows in each partition, one quick read per shard. A dispatcher that falls behind reads the buckets it missed first, so a slow minute delays jobs but never loses them.

Leases and at-least-once execution

When a worker claims a job, it receives a lease: the right to run the job until lease_until, which the worker extends with heartbeats. If the worker dies, its heartbeats stop, the lease expires, and a sweep puts the job back on the queue for another worker. Execution is therefore at least once. No job stays stuck on a dead worker, but a worker that only looked dead may still finish the job, as W3 did in the walkthrough.

The lease length is a trade-off. A short lease, 30 seconds here, recovers from a dead worker quickly but re-runs the job whenever a worker pauses longer than that. A long lease causes fewer duplicates but slower recovery.

Shard ownership works the same way one level up: dispatchers lease shards from a coordination service that uses consensus (see consensus and leader election). When a stalled dispatcher's lease expires another takes over, and for a short time two may scan the same shard. That is safe because every step is a conditional update: only one can move a job to enqueued, and a duplicate id finds the job already running and is dropped.

The order of operations matters: a worker that marks the job done before running it loses the job if it crashes in between. It must run the job first, report afterwards, and rely on idempotency to make a repeat harmless.

Why exactly-once execution is not possible

A scheduler cannot tell a dead worker from a paused one, so to avoid losing jobs it must re-run jobs from workers that only look dead. When an interviewer asks for exactly-once, the honest answer is that the scheduler guarantees at-least-once execution and the system gets an exactly-once effect from three techniques.

Idempotent handlers. The job passes a key derived from the job, either its id or a key chosen by the caller, to every downstream call. The downstream service remembers the keys it has seen and ignores a repeat (see idempotency and retries). In the walkthrough, this is what stops the second reminder email.

Fencing tokens. Every write carries the attempt number, and the receiving system rejects older attempts. The job store does this for the job status. A fencing token cannot take back an action such as a sent email.

Naturally idempotent operations. Setting the report for 2026-11-15 to a given file gives the same result however often it runs, while appending a row to the report does not.

Recurring schedules and time zones

A recurring schedule is stored as a definition with a next_run_at, not as a million future jobs. When a dispatcher claims an occurrence, it creates the next one. The job id is built from the schedule id and the run time, such as s_311@2026-11-15T06:00, so if two dispatchers both create the 07:00 run, insert-if-absent leaves one row. Three policies need an explicit answer.

Overlap is what happens when the 06:00 run is still going at 07:00. The scheduler can skip the new run, queue it or run both, and skipping is the safest default for reports because two concurrent runs often write to the same output.

Missed runs are the occurrences that were due while the scheduler was down. It can run once, run every missed occurrence or skip them, and running once is the usual default.

Time zones mean the next run time must be computed in the schedule's own zone and stored as a UTC instant. When daylight saving time changes, 02:30 local time may happen twice or not at all, so the design must decide which run to keep and test that case.

The hourly burst is handled by giving schedules that say "hourly, at any minute" a stable minute chosen by hashing the schedule id. The 200,000 jobs then spread out to about 200,000 ÷ 3,600 ≈ 56 starts a second, which the existing fleet handles.

Bottlenecks and failures

At 10 times the load, about 12,000 runs a second, grow the store from 64 to 512 shards, split dispatcher ownership into smaller ranges and spread the :00 burst across minutes. Separate ready queues for each job type keep one team's flood from delaying others.

What a strong answer sounds like

LevelWhat the answer does
JuniorThe answer stores jobs in a database and has workers poll for due jobs, and handles a crashed worker only when prompted.
Mid-levelThe answer uses leases and heartbeats, accepts at-least-once execution, partitions jobs by time and retries with backoff.
SeniorThe answer sizes the :00 burst and spreads it, leases shards from a consensus service, uses attempt numbers as fencing tokens, and explains that exactly-once is an effect achieved with idempotent jobs.
Can you guarantee that each job runs exactly once? I cannot guarantee it for execution. I cannot tell a dead worker from a paused one, so I re-run the job, and sometimes both runs finish. I guarantee at-least-once execution and give job owners the tools for an exactly-once effect, which are attempt numbers as fencing tokens and a stable key per job that they pass downstream. So who is responsible for duplicates? The responsibility is shared. The scheduler keeps duplicates rare and visible, and the job owners make their handlers idempotent. I would document that contract from the first day.

Follow-up questions

How do you cancel a job that is due in a minute?

A conditional update sets the status to cancelled only while the job is scheduled or enqueued. Workers check the status when they claim a job, so a cancelled job whose id is already on a queue is dropped at that point. A running job can only be asked to stop.

How do retries work?

A failed attempt gets a new run time, which is the current time plus an exponential backoff with some random jitter, and a new due row is written for it. After max_attempts the job moves to a dead status so that a human can look at it.

One team schedules 10 million jobs for midnight. What protects everyone else?

Each team has its own ready queue and a limit on how fast its jobs are dispatched. The team's jobs start late because of the limit, while other teams' jobs stay on time.
