A candidate designing a restaurant reservation system builds a fast, cached search for open tables, with a booking endpoint behind it. Thirty minutes in, the interviewer asks: "Two diners just booked table 7 for 19:00 on Friday. Who gets it?" The cache-first design has no answer, and no time is left to rebuild the write path.

One early question would have saved it: "Is it ever acceptable to double-book a table?" Requirements decide which parts must be strict, which can be loose, and which to skip.

Two kinds of requirement

  • Functional requirements are what the system does, the user's verbs: "a diner searches open tables by restaurant, date and party size", "a diner books", "a diner cancels".
  • Non-functional requirements are how well it must do them: latency, availability, consistency, durability, scale. This is where designs differ: one system must never lose data, another must answer in 50 ms.
  • Out of scope matters as much: "I'll leave out payments and reviews" buys time for what carries signal.

Set non-functional requirements per path. In one product, search can be fast and slightly stale while booking is slower and strictly correct.

The questions that change the design

Ask about features, then scale, then quality. A question earns its place only if a different answer would change what you build.

  • Who uses it, and for what? The two or three core actions. Do restaurants manage tables in our system too?
  • What is out? Propose the cut yourself.
  • How many? Daily actions and the read/write ratio. Decides caching, replicas, partitioning.
  • How fresh must reads be? Can search lag a few seconds? Decides whether that path may use replicas and caches.
  • What must never go wrong? Double bookings, lost writes, double charges. Decides where you need strong consistency or transactions.
  • How available, and where? One country or global? Is a minute of booking downtime acceptable?

From answers to priorities

Write the result where the interviewer can see it:

  • Functional: search open tables; book; cancel; restaurants manage tables and hours.
  • Non-functional: no double bookings; a confirmed booking is never lost; search under 200 ms, may be a few seconds stale; booking 99.9% available.
  • Out of scope: payments, reviews, waitlists.

Put numbers on vague words. "Highly available" means nothing; 99.9% means 0.001 × 30 × 24 × 60 ≈ 43 minutes of downtime a month.

Then rank, so you know what wins when two requirements conflict. Here, correct bookings beat booking latency, and search freshness goes first.

The 3,000: at a peak of 50 bookings a second (5 times the average of 1 million ÷ 100,000 = 10), a one-minute outage queues 50 × 60 = 3,000 emails.

When you cannot get an answer

When the interviewer says "you decide", they are testing judgment.

In the interview

Spend about 5 minutes here (see the framework). Phrases that show judgment:

  • "The core flows are X, Y and Z. I'll leave out A and B. Does that match?"
  • "Is it ever acceptable to [double-book / lose a message / show a stale price]?"
  • "Reads outnumber writes 20 to 1, so the read path is the one to optimize."

Expected follow-ups:

  • "Why not make search strongly consistent too?" Every search would hit the primary: 1,000 reads a second at peak (20 million ÷ 100,000 = 200 average, times 5) on the machine that must stay fast for bookings. Staleness costs a rare 409.
  • "The primary dies just after confirming a booking. Is it lost?" With async replication it can be. So the commit waits for one synchronous standby before returning 201: a few milliseconds more, which the ranking allows.
  • "Now it's global." Serve search from every region. Each restaurant's bookings are still decided in one place, near the restaurant.

Common mistakes

"Fast, scalable, highly available" fits every system and decides nothing. Say "search under 200 ms, booking 99.9% available, no double bookings".

Everything strongly consistent overloads the source of truth; everything eventually consistent double-books tables. Decide per path.
