A candidate is asked to design a restaurant reservation system. They build a fast, well-cached search for open tables and a booking endpoint behind it. Thirty minutes in, the interviewer asks: "Two diners just booked table 7 for 19:00 on Friday. Who gets it?" The cache-first design has no answer, and there is no time left to rebuild the write path.

One question in the first five minutes would have saved it: "Is it ever acceptable to double-book a table?" Requirements decide which parts of the design must be strict, which can be loose, and which you can skip.

Two kinds of requirement

Functional requirements are what the system does: the verbs a user performs. Write them as short sentences: "a diner searches for open tables by restaurant, date and party size", "a diner books a table", "a diner cancels".

Non-functional requirements are how well it must do them: latency, availability, consistency, durability and scale. They are where designs differ. Two systems with the same features can need completely different architectures because one must never lose data and the other must answer in 50 ms.

A third list matters as much: out of scope. Saying "I'll leave out payments and reviews" buys you time for the parts that carry signal, and the interviewer can still pull something back in.

Non-functional requirements are per path, not per system. Search can be fast and slightly stale while booking is slower and strictly correct, in the same product.

The questions that change the design

Ask in this order: features, then scale, then quality. Each question earns its place only if a different answer would change what you build.

  • Who uses it, and for what? The two or three core actions. Diners search and book; do restaurants also manage their tables in our system?
  • What is out? Payments, reviews, waitlists. Propose the cut yourself.
  • How many? Daily users or daily actions, and the read/write ratio. This decides caching, replicas and partitioning.
  • How fresh must reads be? Can search be a few seconds behind? This decides whether replicas and caches are allowed on that path.
  • What must never go wrong? Double bookings, lost writes, charging twice. This decides where you need strong consistency or transactions.
  • How available, and where? One country or global? Is a minute of downtime on booking acceptable?

From answers to priorities

Write the result where the interviewer can see it, with numbers:

  • Functional: search open tables; book a table; cancel; restaurants manage tables and opening hours.
  • Non-functional: no double bookings; a confirmed booking is never lost; search answers in under 200 ms and may be a few seconds stale; booking stays available at 99.9%.
  • Out of scope: payments, reviews, waitlists.

Put numbers on vague words. "Highly available" means nothing; 99.9% means about 0.001 × 30 × 24 × 60 ≈ 43 minutes of downtime a month, and 99.99% means about 4 minutes. Four minutes is too short for a person to react, so 99.99% means automatic failover to a standby that is already running.

When you cannot get an answer

Sometimes the interviewer says "you decide". It tests judgment. There are three ways to handle unknowns.

In the interview

Spend about 5 minutes here, as in the framework. Phrases that show judgment:

  • "The core flows I see are X, Y and Z. I'll leave out A and B. Does that match what you want?"
  • "Is it ever acceptable to [double-book / lose a message / show a stale price]?"
  • "Reads outnumber writes 20 to 1, so the read path is the one to optimize."
  • "I'll assume one country. If it were global, the booking path would change most."

Follow-ups you should expect:

  • "Why not make search strongly consistent too?" Every search would read the primary: 1,000 reads a second at peak (20 million ÷ 100,000 = 200 on average, times 5) on the one machine that must stay fast for bookings. Staleness costs a rare 409, which is cheap.
  • "The primary dies just after confirming a booking. Is it lost?" With async replication it can be, which breaks "never lost". So the commit waits for one synchronous standby before the API returns 201. That adds a few milliseconds to each booking, which the ranking allows.
  • "What if the restaurant overbooks on purpose?" That is a new functional requirement (a per-restaurant overbooking limit). It changes the condition on the write, not the architecture.
  • "Now it's global." Search can be served from every region. Booking for a given restaurant still needs one place that decides, normally in or near the restaurant's region.

Common mistakes

"Fast, scalable, highly available" fits every system and decides nothing. Say "search under 200 ms, booking 99.9% available, no double bookings". Numbers can be argued with; adjectives cannot.

Making everything strongly consistent overloads the source of truth; making everything eventually consistent double-books tables. Decide per path.
