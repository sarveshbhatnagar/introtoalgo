A checkout request calls five services one after another: cart, pricing, inventory, payment, orders. Each is up 99.9% of the time, which sounds excellent. But checkout needs all five, so it works only 0.9995 ≈ 99.5% of the time. Over a 30-day month that is about 216 minutes of failed checkouts instead of 43.

Every dependency you add in a chain multiplies in its failures. Reliability is the craft of making the whole stay up when parts do not.

The idea

Availability is the share of time (or of requests) that the system does its job. Two rules of arithmetic drive every design choice:

  • In series, multiply availabilities. Five parts at 99.9% each: 99.5% together. More hops, lower availability.
  • In parallel, multiply the failure chances. Two copies that each fail 1% of the time are both down 0.01 × 0.01 = 0.01% of the time, so 99.99% together. This assumes they fail independently, which is a simplification: a shared power feed, a bad deploy or the same bug takes both down at once.

So: put redundant copies of everything critical, keep the critical path short, and make sure a failure in one part cannot drag the rest down with it.

Each extra nine cuts downtime tenfold and usually costs much more effort than the last one. At four nines, a human cannot even be paged and log in before the month's budget is gone: recovery must be automatic.

Redundancy and failover

Stateless servers are easy: run several behind a load balancer, and health checks remove a dead one in seconds. State is harder. A database leader with a standby needs failover: notice the leader is gone, promote the standby, point traffic at it. Downtime is detection plus promotion: with a health check every 5 seconds and 3 misses required, detection alone takes 15 seconds. Detect too eagerly and a slow leader gets replaced while still alive, which is how split brain starts (see consensus and leader election).

Stopping one failure from spreading

The worst outages are cascades: one slow dependency ties up its callers, which then fail their own callers. A product page that calls a recommendations service shows how the patterns fit together.

Two more patterns protect a service from its own callers. Load shedding: when a server is past what it can handle, it rejects extra requests at once with 503, instead of accepting everything and timing out on all of it. Shed the least important work first: browsing before checkout, background refreshes before user clicks. Rate limits do the same per client (see rate limiting). And retries must be limited, or they multiply load on a service that is already struggling (see idempotency and retries).

Multi-region basics

Within one region, spread copies across several zones (separate buildings and power), which covers most hardware failures. Going multi-region guards against losing a whole region, and it is expensive. Two numbers frame the choice: RPO, how much recent data you can lose, and RTO, how long recovery may take.

I start with one region across three zones. What changes my mind: a stated requirement to survive a region outage, which moves me to active-passive, or users on several continents who need low latency, which moves me to active-active with each user's data homed in one region.

In the interview

Reliability questions come after the main design: "What happens if this dies?" Answer per component with what the user sees and what you do. Say the availability target with its arithmetic: "99.9% is 43 minutes a month, so failover must be automatic and under a minute."

Your feed calls an ads service. The ads service starts taking 10 seconds. What happens? Feed requests call ads with a 200 ms timeout from a separate small connection pool, so slow ads cannot use up the threads the feed needs. If more than half of ad calls fail over 10 seconds, a breaker opens and the feed is served without ads. Users lose ads for a while, not the feed. The answer ranks features: the feed is critical, ads are optional. Degrading the optional part is the judgement being tested. And if the feed itself is overloaded? It sheds load: past its concurrency limit it returns 503 at once for background refreshes first, so foreground requests keep getting answers.

Follow-ups you should expect:

  • "How do you test this?" Kill instances and inject latency in a test environment, then carefully in production. A failover never exercised will not work when needed.
  • "Why not five nines?" 26 seconds a month means no human in the loop, and every dependency must be better still. Most products do not need it.
  • "What is your RPO for payments?" Zero: replicate payment writes synchronously to another zone before confirming.

Common mistakes

Two replicas in the same rack, behind the same switch, running the same new deploy, are not independent. Spread copies across zones and roll out changes gradually.

No timeout on a call to a minor service lets it take down a major one. Every network call gets a timeout, and every optional feature gets a fallback.
