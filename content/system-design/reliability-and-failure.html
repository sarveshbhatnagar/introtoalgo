A checkout request calls five services in turn: cart, pricing, inventory, payment, orders. Each is up 99.9% of the time. But checkout needs all five, so it works only 0.9995 ≈ 99.5% of the time: about 216 minutes of failed checkouts a 30-day month instead of 43.

The idea

Availability is the share of time (or requests) the system does its job. Two rules drive every design choice:

  • In series, multiply availabilities. More hops, lower availability.
  • In parallel, multiply the failure chances. Two copies that each fail 1% of the time are both down 0.01 × 0.01 = 0.01% of the time: 99.99% together. This assumes independent failures, which shared power, a shared bad deploy or a shared bug break.

So: make everything critical redundant, keep the critical path short, and stop one part's failure from dragging down the rest.

Each extra nine cuts downtime tenfold and costs more than the last. At four nines, a human cannot be paged and log in before the month's budget is gone: recovery must be automatic.

Redundancy and failover

Stateless servers are easy: run several behind a load balancer; health checks remove a dead one in seconds. State is harder. A database leader with a standby needs failover: notice the leader is gone, promote the standby, redirect traffic. Downtime is detection plus promotion; checking every 5 seconds with 3 misses required, detection alone takes 15 seconds. Detect too eagerly and a slow but live leader gets replaced: split brain (see consensus and leader election).

Stopping one failure from spreading

The worst outages are cascades: one slow dependency ties up its callers, which then fail their own callers. Take a product page that calls a recommendations service.

Two patterns protect a service from its own callers. With load shedding, a server past capacity rejects extra requests at once with 503 instead of timing out on all of them; shed the least important work first (browsing before checkout). Rate limits do the same per client (see rate limiting). And limit retries, or they multiply load on a struggling service (see idempotency and retries).

Multi-region basics

Within one region, spreading copies across zones (separate buildings and power) covers most hardware failures. Multi-region guards against losing a whole region, at a high price. Two numbers frame the choice: RPO, how much recent data you can lose, and RTO, how long recovery may take.

Start with one region across three zones. Move only for a stated need: surviving a region outage, or low latency across continents.

In the interview

Reliability questions follow the main design: "What happens if this dies?" Answer per component: what the user sees, what you do. Give the target with its arithmetic: "99.9% is 43 minutes a month, so failover must be automatic and under a minute."

Your feed calls an ads service. The ads service starts taking 10 seconds. What happens? The feed calls ads with a 200 ms timeout from a separate small connection pool, so slow ads cannot take the feed's threads. If over half of ad calls fail within 10 seconds, a breaker opens and the feed is served without ads. Users lose ads for a while, not the feed. It ranks features: the feed is critical, ads are optional. Degrading the optional part is the judgement tested.

Follow-ups:

  • "How do you test this?" Kill instances and inject latency in a test environment, then carefully in production. An unexercised failover will not work.
  • "Why not five nines?" Every dependency must be better still, with no human in the loop. Most products do not need it.
  • "What is your RPO for payments?" Zero: replicate payment writes synchronously to another zone before confirming.

Common mistakes

Two replicas in one rack, behind one switch, or running the same new deploy, are not independent. Spread copies across zones and roll out changes gradually.

A minor service with no timeout can take down a major one. Every network call gets a timeout; every optional feature gets a fallback.
