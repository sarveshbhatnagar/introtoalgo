Someone at another company presses Send. Their mail server finds your domain and hands over a message. Seconds later it is on your phone and laptop, filed in the inbox, spam moved aside, and next year you can find it by typing one word.

Behind it: a receive path that never loses accepted mail, petabytes of storage, a search index per person, retries against servers you do not control, and devices that agree. The steps follow A Framework for Any Design; try each "Your turn" box before reading on.

Clarify the problem

Design an email service like a big web mail provider. I'll assume the core is sending and receiving, labels, search and attachments. Do we also serve desktop clients over IMAP? Yes. Our own apps only; IMAP is a stretch goal. How many users, and how much mail per user? 100 million users. Each receives about 40 emails a day after spam is rejected, and sends about 5. Then my priorities: once we tell a sender's server "accepted", that message is never lost; new mail reaches every device within seconds; a user's own actions show at once. Search can lag a few seconds. The candidate names the one promise that cannot break and where lag is fine. Most of the design follows.
  • Functional: receive over SMTP; filter spam and viruses; list by label; open messages and attachments; send, with retries and bounces; label, mark read, archive, delete; search your own mail; sync devices.
  • Non-functional: no accepted message lost; new mail visible within 5 seconds; inbox under 200 ms; search under 500 ms; receiving stays up when reading is degraded.
  • Out of scope: calendar, contacts, chat, ads, end-to-end encryption, IMAP (discussed as a follow-up).

Estimate the scale

Assume an email stores 30 KB: 10 KB of headers and body, plus attachments (one email in 20, averaging 400 KB) adding 400 ÷ 20 = 20 KB. Peak is twice the average, since mail spreads over time zones.

  • Received: 100 million × 40 = 4 billion emails a day, about 90,000 a second at peak.
  • Sent: 100 million × 5 = 500 million a day, about 5,800 a second.
  • Reads: 20 opens per user a day is 2 billion reads, about 23,000 a second.
  • Inbound bandwidth: 120 TB a day ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 1.4 GB/s.
  • Per user: 40 × 365 × 30 KB ≈ 440 MB a year.

Write the app endpoints: list a label, open a message, send, change labels, search, and sync. What does "send" return, when delivery can take days?

Reads are GETs by label with a cursor; label changes are small operations. Send returns 202: queued, not delivered. Sync is "changes since N".

The decision worth arguing is sync. Re-downloading the inbox on every wake fetches 50 rows when one changed, and misses label changes on older mail. Instead a device asks a per-user change log for "everything after 81,237"; offline for a week, it just gets more. Label changes are operations ("add Receipts, remove INBOX"), not a new label set, so two devices editing different labels both win.

A message can carry many labels. A folder is a label limited to one per message; Inbox, Sent, Spam and Trash are system labels. Archiving removes INBOX; nothing moves.

Almost every query starts "for this user", so partition everything by user_id, in a sharded relational database (the deep dives say why). Bodies and attachments never change once written, which suits object storage.

Write the raw message to object storage and a reference to a durable queue, then reply "accepted". Workers filter and file it; apps read through a mail API.

High-level design

Deep dives

The receive path: when to say 250

SMTP's contract: 4xx means "try again later", and the sender will. 5xx means "permanently refused", and the sender's server tells its user. 250 after the body means you now own the message. So decide where each check runs.

The pipeline is at-least-once on purpose: a sender may retry after a lost 250, and the queue may hand a message to two workers. So make the mailbox write idempotent with a per-recipient dedupe key (see idempotency and retries). A message to three of your users is stored once and queued as three deliveries.

Back-pressure is free: if the queue nears its limit, SMTP in answers 451, a temporary failure, and senders retry later. Mail is delayed, never dropped.

Answering 250 as soon as the bytes arrive feels faster. If the server dies before writing, the sender has already deleted its copy. Write and replicate first, then reply.

Mailbox storage, partitioned by user

Map users to shards with a directory, not user_id modulo the shard count, so you can move one user without rehashing everyone (see sharding and partitioning). Metadata grows 2 TB a day.

Attachments are stored by the SHA-256 hash of their bytes, so a 5 MB file sent to 200 of your users takes 5 MB, not 1,000 MB. Access goes through a message the user owns, never by hash. A sweep deletes objects unreferenced for a few days; the grace period protects a delivery reusing one now.

Search, one user at a time

Index headers, body and attachment names; a slower job adds text from documents. from:, has:attachment and label: are filters. Results are checked against the mailbox, so a message deleted a second ago never appears. Search systems covers the inverted index.

The send path: retries and bounces

Before returning 202, one transaction writes the message to Sent and an outbox row; a relay moves outbox rows to the queue, so a crash cannot leave mail in Sent that never leaves. The draft id is the idempotency key, so a double tap sends once. Recipients on your own service skip SMTP.

  • Delivery: group recipients by domain, look up its MX record, connect over TLS, and sign with DKIM so the receiver can verify you.
  • Temporary failure (4xx, timeout): retry with growing gaps, say 5 minutes, 15, then hourly, for at least four to five days, as the SMTP standard suggests.
  • Permanent failure (5xx, such as "no such user") or retries used up: put a bounce with the address and reason in the sender's inbox. Only this path bounces.
  • Reputation: receivers judge your sending IPs, so rate-limit accounts and send bulk and personal mail from separate pools.

Sync across devices

Each user has a counter, a plain column since a user lives on one shard. Every mailbox change, in its transaction, takes the next number and writes a row to changes. Taking the number locks the counter row until commit, so changes commit in order and a reader never skips a late one. A device remembers the last number it saw.

  • Telling devices: an open connection for web, a push for phones (see realtime communication). The push says only "something changed"; a lost push just delays sync.
  • Offline edits: the device queues operations such as "mark m_9f2 read" and replays them on reconnect. On conflicting flags, the last write wins.
  • Far behind: keep 30 days of change log. Older requests get 410 and resync the newest 500 messages per label.

Bottlenecks and failures

At 10x, about 460,000 messages a second and 1.2 PB a day, storage cost is the first wall: compress bodies, store attachments once, tier old mail, enforce quotas. SMTP in and workers are stateless and scale out behind several MX records; mailbox and search add shards.

A sender's server hands you a message. When do you say "accepted"? After the raw message is in object storage with three copies and a reference is on a replicated queue. Until then I can fail with a 4xx and the sender keeps its copy. After that, losing it is on us. And if you crash after writing, before replying? The sender retries. The mailbox insert is unique on a dedupe key from the Message-ID and a content hash, so the second copy is a no-op. The answer ties durability to a protocol event and handles the duplicate its own choice creates.

Follow-up questions

How does "undo send" work?

Queue the outbound message with a delay, say 10 seconds; undo deletes it before release. Once handed to another provider, it cannot be recalled.

A mailing list sends one message to 1 million of your users. What happens?

Store the body once and queue 1 million deliveries: about 22 seconds at 46,000 a second. A lower-priority queue for bulk keeps personal mail moving.

How would you support IMAP clients?

Add an IMAP front end mapping labels to folders and the change log to IMAP's modification sequences. The catch: a message with two labels shows in two folders, and deleting it from one should only remove that label.
