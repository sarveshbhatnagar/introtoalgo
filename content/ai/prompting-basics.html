You paste a long article into an AI assistant and type "Summarize this." You get three tidy, accurate paragraphs, and they are useless: you needed to know whether the article changes your team's plans, and the summary is mostly background.

The model did what you asked and guessed everything you did not say. Prompting is the skill of not making it guess.

A prompt is a brief for a capable stranger

Picture a smart contractor who started today. They write well and know a lot, but nothing about you: who the summary is for, what decision it feeds, how long it should be, what you already know.

A language model is in the same position. It is good at producing the typical answer to a request. A vague request gets the typical answer; a request that carries your situation gets an answer for your situation.

The model only knows what is in the prompt. Anything you leave out, it fills in with the most generic guess.

See the difference

The "Summarize this" request and a typical reply:

Summarize this article. [2,000-word article about moving a company's web app from one database to another] The article discusses a company's migration from its original database to a new system. It begins by describing the history of the original setup and the reasons it became difficult to maintain. The authors then outline the planning process, including stakeholder meetings and timelines. Finally, they reflect on lessons learned, noting that communication and testing were key to success.

Now a prompt that says who is asking, why, and what shape the answer should take:

I lead a team of four engineers. We are deciding next month whether to make the same database move described in the article below. Summarize the article for that decision: - 5 bullet points at most - Focus on cost, time taken, and what went wrong - Skip the company history - End with one line: the biggest risk for a small team
[2,000-word article]
- Took 7 months against a 3-month plan; the overrun came from rewriting reports that queried the old database directly. - Two engineers worked on it full time; the article does not give a total cost. - Data copying itself was quick. Most of the problems were in code that assumed the old database's date formats. - They ran both databases side by side for six weeks to compare results before switching. - One outage happened during the switch, caused by a connection limit nobody had tested. Biggest risk for a small team: running two databases in parallel for weeks, which is what made their switch safe.

Nothing clever: it answers the questions a good colleague would ask before starting.

The four parts of a good prompt

You should be able to point at each of these, headings or not:

  1. The task. A direct instruction near the top: "Summarize", "Rewrite", "List", "Find the bug in".
  2. The context. Who it is for, why, and what you already know. "For a decision my team makes next month" changes what counts as important.
  3. The shape of the answer. Length, format, tone, what to leave out. "5 bullets, no history" is easy to follow.
  4. The input. The material to work on, clearly separated from your instructions.

The fourth matters more than it looks. If a pasted email says "please reply by Friday", the model can mistake that for your instruction. Wrap the material in clear markers, like <article> … </article> tags or a line saying "Text to summarize:".

"Don't be too wordy" leaves the model to guess what "too" means. "Answer in 3 sentences" does not.

Treat the first answer as a draft

Even a good prompt rarely gets a perfect first answer. Read the reply, name exactly what is wrong, and change the prompt to fix that one thing.

  • Too long? Give a number: "under 120 words".
  • Wrong focus? Say what matters: "I care about the cost, not the timeline".
  • Wrong tone? Say who reads it: "this goes to a customer who is already annoyed".
  • Made something up? Ask it to use only the provided text and say "not stated" when something is missing.

Changing one thing at a time tells you what helped. Rewrite everything and you learn nothing.

Teams that use AI well keep their best prompts in a shared document, like code snippets, and improve them when they slip.

Try it yourself

This prompt got a stiff, generic letter that missed the important facts. Rewrite it using the four parts: task, context, shape, input.

 Write an email to my landlord about the heating.

One stronger version:

Write a short email to my landlord asking them to fix the heating. Context: the heating in my flat has not worked since Monday (3 days). It is around 12°C inside at night. I reported it by phone on Monday and was told someone would come "this week", but nobody has contacted me. I have a good relationship with the landlord and want to keep it. Shape: polite but firm, under 150 words. Ask for a specific repair date by Friday. Mention that I can give access any weekday after 4pm. Sign it: Priya, Flat 3B

Why it works:

  • Task: not "about the heating" but "asking them to fix it".
  • Context: the facts the email must contain (how long, how cold, what was promised). Without them, the model invents vague filler.
  • Shape: tone, length, and one concrete request, a repair date, so the email ends with an action.
  • The sign-off and access times save you an edit afterwards.

Common mistakes

It does not know your project, your audience, your chats in other tools, or what "the usual format" means to you. If it matters, write it down.

When a reply is off, reread your prompt as the stranger receiving it. Usually the reply is a reasonable answer to what was written.

An unmarked document invites the model to follow instructions inside it. Mark where your instructions end and the material begins. Prompt injection shows why this matters for security too.
