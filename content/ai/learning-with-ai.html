Two friends learn recursion with an AI assistant for a week. The first pastes in each exercise, reads the solution, and nods along. The second tries each exercise first, asks for a hint when stuck, and explains the answer back before moving on. It is slower.

At the end of the week each gets a recursion problem on paper, with no assistant. The second works through it. The first stares at the page. Reading a clear explanation felt like understanding, but it was not practice at producing one.

Who does the thinking

You learn what you work to recall and produce. Following someone else's reasoning feels productive. Building it yourself, getting it wrong and fixing it, is what makes it stick. An assistant can do all the thinking for you, or make your own thinking faster and better aimed, depending on how you ask.

None of these is always wrong. A worked answer helps after you have tried, or for a topic you only need to recognise. The mistake is staying at the top of the list for something you need to be able to do.

Do the first attempt and the final explanation yourself. Use the assistant for what happens in between: hints, checks, examples and questions.

Set it up as a tutor

By default, assistants give complete answers. If you want a tutor, say so, and say what kind, at the start of the session.

I'm learning recursion in Python. I know loops and lists well. Act as a tutor: when I'm stuck, give me one hint at a time, as a question if you can. Don't show me corrected code unless I ask for it. After I fix something, ask me to explain why the fix works. Here is my attempt at summing a list. It works on [4, 2, 5] but my test with an empty list crashes: def total(nums): if len(nums) == 1: return nums[0] return nums[0] + total(nums[1:]) Good start: the recursive step is right. Let's find the crash. Trace total([]) by hand. Which line runs first, and what is nums[0] when the list is empty? len is 0, not 1, so it skips the if and does nums[0]. There is no item 0, so that's the error. Exactly. Now: what should the total of an empty list be? And once you know that, do you still need the len(nums) == 1 case?

The tutor never wrote the fix. The learner found the cause by tracing, and the next question leads to a better base case (an empty list sums to 0) that makes the one-item case unnecessary. They will remember it because they worked it out.

Four habits that keep you in charge

  1. Bring an attempt. Even a wrong one. "Here is what I tried and where I got stuck" gets a far more useful reply, and you have already done the hard thinking.
  2. Explain it back. Write the idea in your own words and ask what is wrong or missing. Gaps you cannot put into words are gaps you have.
  3. Get quizzed later. Ask for three questions or small problems on yesterday's topic, with no answers until you ask. Recall after a gap is one of the most reliable ways to make learning last.
  4. Ask for a different angle. If an explanation does not land, ask for an example with small numbers or a comparison to something you know. Then try the exercise again.

A tutor that is sometimes wrong

An AI tutor sometimes invents a fact, in the same calm voice it uses for everything else (see Why Models Hallucinate). While learning, you do not know enough yet to notice. Three safeguards:

  • Prefer well-trodden topics. The model is far more reliable on loops than on a niche library released last month.
  • Keep a trusted source open, such as official docs or a course, and check anything surprising against it.
  • Test what you can. If it says a function behaves a certain way, run it. Verifying AI Output has the full routine.

Try it yourself

This request will produce a lecture you half-read and forget. Rewrite it so the assistant makes you think: include your level, how you want help, and how you will know you understood.

 Explain dynamic programming to me. I want to learn dynamic programming. I'm comfortable with recursion and Big-O, and I've never seen DP before. Teach me by starting with one small problem I can solve by hand, not with definitions. Let me try it first. When I'm stuck, give one hint at a time. Only explain the general idea after I've solved the example. At the end, give me two new problems to solve on my own, and check my answers without showing me yours first.

Why it works: your level sets where to start. "Problem first, idea after" lands the explanation on something you just struggled with. The final two problems are the real test: if you cannot do them alone, you have not learned it yet. The site's DP introduction is a trusted source to check against.

Common mistakes

If you could not write the assistant's fix again tomorrow, you have a working file and no new skill. Delete your solution and redo it from a blank page. Where you get stuck is what to ask about next.

Once you see a full solution, the chance to work it out is gone. Ask for the smallest help that gets you moving: a hint, a question, or "is my approach on the right track, yes or no?"
