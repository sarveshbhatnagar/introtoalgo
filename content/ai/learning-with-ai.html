Two friends learn recursion with an AI assistant over the same week. The first pastes each exercise in, reads the solution and the explanation, and nods along. Everything makes sense. The second tries each exercise first, asks for a hint when stuck, and explains the answer back in their own words before moving on. It is slower and more frustrating.

At the end of the week they each get a recursion problem on paper, with no assistant. The second friend works through it. The first stares at the page. Reading a clear explanation felt exactly like understanding, but it was not practice at producing one.

Who does the thinking

You learn what you work to recall and produce. Following someone else's correct reasoning is easy and pleasant, which is why it feels productive. Building the reasoning yourself, getting it wrong and fixing it, is what makes it stick.

An AI assistant can sit anywhere on this line. It can do all the thinking for you, or it can make your own thinking faster and better aimed. The same tool, asked differently.

None of these is always wrong. Reading a worked answer is useful after you have tried, or for a topic you only need to recognise. The mistake is living at the top of the list for something you need to be able to do.

Do the first attempt and the final explanation yourself. Use the assistant for what happens in between: hints, checks, examples and questions.

Set it up as a tutor

By default, assistants try to be helpful by giving complete answers. If you want a tutor, say so, and say what kind. A short setup at the start of the session does most of the work.

I'm learning recursion in Python. I know loops and lists well. Act as a tutor: when I'm stuck, give me one hint at a time, as a question if you can. Don't show me corrected code unless I ask for it. After I fix something, ask me to explain why the fix works. Here is my attempt at summing a list. It works on [4, 2, 5] but my test with an empty list crashes: def total(nums): if len(nums) == 1: return nums[0] return nums[0] + total(nums[1:]) Good start: the recursive step is right. Let's find the crash. Trace total([]) by hand. Which line runs first, and what is nums[0] when the list is empty? len is 0, not 1, so it skips the if and does nums[0]. There is no item 0, so that's the error. Exactly. Now: what should the total of an empty list be? And once you know that, do you still need the len(nums) == 1 case?

The tutor never wrote the fix. The learner found the cause by tracing, and the next question leads them to a better base case (an empty list sums to 0), which also makes the one-item case unnecessary. That is the lesson about base cases, and the learner will remember it because they worked it out.

Four habits that keep you in charge

  1. Bring an attempt. Even a wrong one. "Here is what I tried and where I got stuck" gets you a far more useful reply than the bare question, and it means you have already done the hard part of thinking.
  2. Explain it back. Write the idea in your own words and ask the assistant to point out anything wrong or missing. Gaps you cannot put into words are gaps you have.
  3. Get quizzed later. Ask for three questions on yesterday's topic and answer without looking anything up. Recalling something after a gap is one of the most reliable ways to make it last.
  4. Ask for a different angle. If an explanation does not land, ask for a concrete example with small numbers, a picture in words, or a comparison to something you already know. Then try the exercise again.

"Give me three small problems on this, getting harder, and don't show answers until I ask" turns a topic into practice. Check the problems make sense before you spend an hour on one.

A tutor that is sometimes wrong

A human tutor rarely invents a fact. An AI tutor sometimes does, in the same calm voice it uses for everything else (see Why Models Hallucinate). When you are learning, this is harder to catch, because you do not know enough yet to notice.

Three safeguards help. Prefer well-trodden topics, where the model has seen many correct explanations; it is far more reliable on loops than on a niche library released last month. Keep a trusted source next to you, such as official docs or a course, and check anything surprising against it. And test claims you can test: if it says a function behaves a certain way, run it. Try it yourself

This request will produce a long lecture you will half-read and forget. Rewrite it so the assistant teaches you by making you think. Include your level, how you want to be helped, and how you will know you understood.

 Explain dynamic programming to me. I want to learn dynamic programming. I'm comfortable with recursion and Big-O, and I've never seen DP before. Teach me by starting with one small problem I can solve by hand, not with definitions. Let me try it first. When I'm stuck, give one hint at a time. Only explain the general idea after I've solved the example. At the end, give me two new problems to solve on my own, and check my answers without showing me yours first.

Why it works: stating your level skips what you know and avoids what you are not ready for. "Problem first, idea after" means the explanation lands on something you have just struggled with. The final two problems are the real test: if you cannot do them alone, you have not learned it yet, however clear the explanation felt. The site's own DP introduction is a good trusted source to check against.

Common mistakes

If the assistant fixed your exercise and you could not write the fix again tomorrow, you have a working file and no new skill. Close the chat, delete your solution, and redo it from a blank page. If you get stuck, that is what to ask about next.

An hour of reading clear explanations feels great and can leave little behind. Judge a study session by what you can do at the end without help, not by how much you read.

Once you see a full solution, you cannot unsee it, and the chance to work it out is gone. Ask for the smallest help that gets you moving: a hint, a question, or "is my approach on the right track, yes or no?"
