You ask an AI assistant to summarize your monthly budget. It replies with a tidy breakdown and a total, which you paste into the spreadsheet you share with your partner. It is 10 too low: every amount was copied correctly, but the addition was wrong.

The wrong total sounded as confident as everything else. That is why you verify: the writing does not tell you which parts to trust.

Checking means comparing with something else

Rereading a reply is not a check. Neither is asking the model "are you sure?" (see Why Models Hallucinate). A real check compares the reply with something outside it: your original data, a calculation you run, an official document, a test that passes or fails.

  1. Split the reply into claims. A claim is anything that could be true or false: a number, a name, a date, "this function handles empty input".
  2. Check each claim by its type. Numbers get recomputed. Facts get looked up in a primary source. Code gets run. Citations get opened and read.

Confident wording says nothing about accuracy. Check claims against a source outside the model, and check hardest where a mistake costs most.

Watch a reply get checked

Read the reply once and see if anything jumps out.

Summarize my monthly budget. Rent 950, groceries 312, transport 86, phone 45. Take-home pay 2,600. Here's your month at a glance. Rent is your largest expense at 950, which is 36.5% of your take-home pay. Total spending comes to 1,383, leaving you 1,217. Groceries are about 25% of your spending. One easy win: if you got transport down to 70, you would save 192 a year.

Nothing does, and that is the point. Now check its six claims one at a time.

Numbers: recompute, never re-read

Models produce numbers the way they produce words, one token at a time, so even simple arithmetic is sometimes wrong. When a number matters, recompute it from the inputs. For more than a couple of values, a few lines of code beat a calculator because you can rerun them.

const spending = { rent: 950, groceries: 312, transport: 86, phone: 45 };
const pay = 2600;

const total = Object.values(spending).reduce((sum, x) => sum + x, 0);
console.log("total:", total); // 1393
for (const [name, amount] of Object.entries(spending)) {
  console.log(name, ((100 * amount) / total).toFixed(1) + "%");
}
// rent 68.2%, groceries 22.4%, transport 6.2%, phone 3.2%
console.log("left over:", pay - total); // 1207

spending = {"rent": 950, "groceries": 312, "transport": 86, "phone": 45}
pay = 2600

total = sum(spending.values())
print("total:", total)  # 1393
for name, amount in spending.items():
    print(name, f"{100 * amount / total:.1f}%")
# rent 68.2%, groceries 22.4%, transport 6.2%, phone 3.2%
print("left over:", pay - total)  # 1207

Facts, code and citations

Facts

Names, dates, versions, laws, prices and quotes need a primary source: the official docs, the original report, the law itself. A page that repeats the claim may copy its mistake. For software, check the docs for your version: a function from an old version or a different library is a common wrong answer.

Code

Run it, then try inputs the example skipped: empty, one item, duplicates, negatives, very large values. Coding With AI shows how to turn them into tests.

Citations

A citation is three claims: the source exists, says what the reply says, and is about the same thing.

  • Open it. A dead link, or a paper you cannot find by its title, is a red flag.
  • Find the sentence. Search the page for the number or phrase. An on-topic source without the claim does not support it.
  • Read around it. A real quote can mean the opposite in context, or describe a different year, country or product.

In the prompt, ask for exact quotes, page or section numbers, and the working (or code) behind any calculation. A quote takes seconds to check; a vague paraphrase or a bare number does not.

How much to check

Party-theme ideas need no checking. A figure for your manager, a dose, a legal deadline or production code does. Ask: if this is wrong, who notices, and what does it cost? Check first where the answer is "someone else, and a lot".

Try it yourself

An assistant wrote this for a team update. List each claim, how you would check it, and which you would check first.

 Our sign-ups rose from 1,240 in May to 1,550 in June, a 20% increase. Most of the growth came from the referral program, which launched on June 3. According to the analytics guide, a 20% month-on-month rise is "well above typical" for products like ours.

Six claims in three sentences:

  1. 1,240 in May and 1,550 in June: compare with your analytics dashboard.
  2. 20% increase: recompute. (1,550 − 1,240) ÷ 1,240 = 310 ÷ 1,240 = 25%, not 20%. Wrong.
  3. Most growth came from referrals: check sign-ups by source. A claim about cause, possibly guessed from the launch date.
  4. Launched on June 3: check the release notes.
  5. The guide's quote: find "well above typical" in the guide and check it covers the same kind of product and measure.

Check the percentage first: ten seconds, and the whole update leans on it. Then the cause claim, which will steer the team's next move.

Common mistakes

Errors can sit far down a long table where nobody looked. Sample the middle and end too, or recompute the whole thing in code.

In the tracer, one wrong total caused two wrong claims. Recheck everything that depended on an error you find.

The same answer from a second assistant, or the same one again, is not verification. Both can repeat the same wrong pattern.
