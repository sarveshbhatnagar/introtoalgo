Verifying AI output means checking what a model produced against something outside the model before you rely on it. It matters because a model writes wrong statements in the same confident tone as right ones, so the wording of a reply never tells you which parts to trust.

Without a check, small errors travel. A model that adds up a budget incorrectly will still present the total cleanly, and you may paste it into a document that other people then use.

How to check a reply

Rereading a reply is not a check, and neither is asking the model whether it is sure (Why Models Hallucinate explains why that does not help). A real check compares the reply with something that does not come from the model: your original data, a calculation you run yourself, an official document, or a test that passes or fails.

The routine has two steps. First, split the reply into claims, where a claim is anything that could be true or false, such as a number, a name, a date, or "this function handles empty input". Second, check each claim in the way that suits its type: recompute numbers, look facts up in a primary source, run code, and open citations and read them.

A worked example

The reply below summarizes a small budget. Read it once and see whether anything looks wrong.

Summarize my monthly budget. Rent 950, groceries 312, transport 86, phone 45. Take-home pay 2,600. Here's your month at a glance. Rent is your largest expense at 950, which is 36.5% of your take-home pay. Total spending comes to 1,383, leaving you 1,217. Groceries are about 25% of your spending. One easy win: if you got transport down to 70, you would save 192 a year.

Nothing looks wrong, which is the point of the example. The reply contains six claims, and the tracer checks them one at a time.

Checking each type of claim

Numbers

Models produce numbers the same way they produce words, one piece at a time, so even simple arithmetic is sometimes wrong. When a number matters, recompute it from the inputs instead of rereading the reply. For more than a few values, a short script is better than a calculator because you can rerun it when the inputs change.

const spending = { rent: 950, groceries: 312, transport: 86, phone: 45 };
const pay = 2600;

const total = Object.values(spending).reduce((sum, x) => sum + x, 0);
console.log("total:", total); // 1393
for (const [name, amount] of Object.entries(spending)) {
  console.log(name, ((100 * amount) / total).toFixed(1) + "%");
}
// rent 68.2%, groceries 22.4%, transport 6.2%, phone 3.2%
console.log("left over:", pay - total); // 1207

spending = {"rent": 950, "groceries": 312, "transport": 86, "phone": 45}
pay = 2600

total = sum(spending.values())
print("total:", total)  # 1393
for name, amount in spending.items():
    print(name, f"{100 * amount / total:.1f}%")
# rent 68.2%, groceries 22.4%, transport 6.2%, phone 3.2%
print("left over:", pay - total)  # 1207

Facts

Names, dates, versions, laws, prices and quotes need a primary source, such as the official documentation, the original report or the text of the law. A web page that repeats the claim may have copied the same mistake. For software, check the documentation for the version you use, because a function from an older version or from a different library is a common wrong answer.

Code

Run the code, then try inputs the example did not cover: an empty input, a single item, duplicates, negative values and very large values. Code that passes the one example in the reply can still fail on these. Coding With AI shows how to turn those cases into tests.

Citations

A citation makes three claims at once: that the source exists, that it says what the reply says it says, and that it is about the same thing as your question. You check them in that order. First open the source. A dead link, or a paper you cannot find by its title, is a strong sign the model invented it. Then search the page for the number or phrase. A source that is on topic but does not contain the claim does not support it. Finally read the text around the passage, because a real quote can mean the opposite in context or describe a different year, country or product.

Deciding how much to check

You cannot check everything with the same effort, and you do not need to. Ten name ideas for a newsletter need no checking, because a bad one costs you nothing. A figure you will send to your manager, a medication dose, a legal deadline or code that will run in production does need it. The question to ask is who notices if this is wrong and what it costs them. Check first where the answer is that someone else notices, late, and at a high cost.

Pitfalls

In a long table or list, errors can sit far down where nobody looked. Sample from the middle and the end as well, or recompute the whole thing in code.

In the example above, one wrong total produced two wrong claims. When you find an error, recheck every claim that depended on it.

Getting the same answer from a second assistant, or from the same one asked again, does not confirm it, because both can repeat the same wrong pattern. Only a source or a calculation outside the model counts.

A real link to a real article does not support a claim if the article does not contain it. Find the sentence itself, and if you cannot, treat the claim as unsupported.

What to do differently in practice

Make the reply easy to check before you read it. Ask for exact quotes, page or section numbers, and the working or code behind any calculation. A quote takes seconds to find in a source, while a paraphrase or a bare number gives you nothing to compare against. Then pick the claims that would hurt most if wrong and check those first, recomputing numbers yourself and opening every citation you intend to rely on.

An assistant wrote this for a team update. List each claim, say how you would check it, and decide which you would check first.

 Our sign-ups rose from 1,240 in May to 1,550 in June, a 20% increase. Most of the growth came from the referral program, which launched on June 3. According to the analytics guide, a 20% month-on-month rise is "well above typical" for products like ours.

The three sentences contain five claims:

  1. 1,240 in May and 1,550 in June. Compare both with your analytics dashboard.
  2. A 20% increase. Recompute it: (1,550 − 1,240) ÷ 1,240 = 310 ÷ 1,240 = 25%, so the figure is wrong.
  3. Most of the growth came from referrals. Check sign-ups by source. This is a claim about cause, and the assistant may have guessed it from the launch date.
  4. The program launched on June 3. Check the release notes.
  5. The guide's quote. Find "well above typical" in the guide and confirm it covers the same kind of product and the same measure.

Check the percentage first, because it takes ten seconds and the rest of the update builds on it. Check the cause claim next, since it will decide what the team does.

What to practise next
