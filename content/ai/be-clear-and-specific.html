You ask a colleague to make your slide deck "a bit shorter". They cut 30 slides to 26. You wanted 10. The number was in your head and never left it.

Prompts fail the same way. Short, simple, professional, a few and improve feel clear to the writer. To the reader they are open questions, and a model answers open questions with the most common guess.

Every vague word is a decision you handed over

Some gaps hide inside words you did write (Prompting Basics covers the rest). "Keep it short" means you decided how short but did not say, so the model must decide again, without your reasons.

Replace each vague word with something you could check. If you cannot tell whether the reply followed the instruction, the model could not either.

VagueSpecific
Keep it shortUnder 80 words
Make it simpleA 12-year-old could follow it; explain any technical word in brackets

Give me some ideasGive 5 ideas, one line each
Improve this paragraphFix grammar and spelling only; keep my wording and length

"Improve" could mean fix typos, restructure, cut by half, or sound fancier. "Fix grammar and spelling only" leaves one meaning.

Watch the guesses disappear

Task: a subject line telling staff the office closes early on Friday. Here are six plausible replies to the vague version. Each detail you add rules some out.

Real prompts have thousands of possible replies, but each checkable detail still shrinks the set.

Say why, not only what

The tracer's last step gave a reason: "so people don't think it's a holiday". Reasons matter because you can never list every case. Compare two versions of one support rule:

Reply to this customer. Never mention refunds. Customer message: My order arrived with a cracked screen. I'd like to return it. I'm sorry to hear your screen arrived cracked. Please reply with a photo of the damage and your order number, and we'll look into it.

The rule was followed, and the reply dodges the question: the model had no idea whether a return was allowed.

Reply to this customer. Don't promise refunds: only the billing team can approve them, and promises we can't keep make customers angrier. For damaged items, offer a free replacement, which you are allowed to arrange. Customer message: My order arrived with a cracked screen. I'd like to return it. I'm sorry your screen arrived cracked. I can send you a free replacement right away. Just reply with a photo of the damage and your order number. If you'd prefer your money back instead, I'll pass your request to our billing team, who handle refunds.

Knowing the goal, the model found a helpful answer and handled the refund request honestly, a case the bare rule never covered.

Replace every vague word with something checkable, and give the reason behind each rule. Details say what to do; reasons cover the cases you forgot.

Four habits that make prompts specific

  1. Name the reader. "For a new hire in their first week" sets vocabulary, depth and tone.
  2. Give numbers. Word counts, item counts, reading level. Numbers are the easiest instruction to follow and check.
  3. Describe done. "It should end with one action the reader can take today."
  4. Say what to do when stuck. "If the date is not in the text, write unknown; do not guess." Otherwise the model fills the gap with something plausible, which is how made-up details slip in.

Imagine forwarding your prompt to a capable colleague new to your project. If they would come back with questions, put the answers in the prompt.

Try it yourself

This prompt produces a generic, gushing paragraph. Rewrite it: make every vague word checkable, name the reader, and give at least one reason.

 Write a good, professional description of our new water bottle. Make it catchy but not too long.

One stronger version:

Write a product description for our online shop. Product: steel water bottle, 750 ml, keeps drinks cold for 24 hours, fits standard car cup holders, dishwasher safe, 3 colours. Reader: people who cycle or commute and are comparing several bottles on their phone. They skim, so put the most useful fact first. Length: 50 to 70 words, then 3 bullet points with the key specs. Tone: plain and confident. No exclamation marks and no words like "revolutionary" or "game-changing", because our customers have told us that kind of language makes them trust a listing less.

Why it works:

  • "Good" and "catchy" became a reader (skimming commuters) and an ordering rule (most useful fact first).
  • "Not too long" became 50 to 70 words plus 3 bullets.
  • The facts are given, so the model does not invent features.
  • The hype ban has a reason, so the model also avoids hype words you did not list.

Common mistakes

"VERY IMPORTANT: be concise!!" is still vague: capitals show you care, not what concise means. Write "under 60 words". Heavy emphasis can also make a model overcorrect.

"Be thorough" and "keep it brief" force the model to pick one, unpredictably. Say which wins: "Keep it under 100 words, even if that means skipping minor points."

A task in the last sentence of a long background paragraph is easy to underweight. State the task first, then context, then material. If the material is long, repeat the question after it.
