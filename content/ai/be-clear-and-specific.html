You ask a colleague to make your slide deck "a bit shorter". They come back proud: 30 slides down to 26. You wanted 10. Nobody was careless. "A bit shorter" had a number in your head, and that number never left it.

Prompts fail the same way. Words like short, simple, professional, a few and improve feel clear to the person typing them. To the reader they are open questions, and a model answers open questions with the most common guess.

Every vague word is a decision you handed over

Prompting Basics showed that a model fills gaps with generic guesses. This lesson is about the gaps hiding inside words you did write. When you say "keep it short", you made a decision (how short) and then did not write it down. The model has to make that decision again, without your reasons.

The fix is to replace each vague word with something you could check. If you cannot tell whether the reply followed the instruction, the model could not either.

VagueSpecific
Keep it shortUnder 80 words
Make it simpleA 12-year-old could follow it; explain any technical word in brackets
Sound professionalFormal, no jokes, no exclamation marks
Give me some ideasGive 5 ideas, one line each
Improve this paragraphFix grammar and spelling only; keep my wording and length

Look at the last row. "Improve" could mean fix typos, restructure, cut by half, or make it sound fancier. Four people would do four different things. "Fix grammar and spelling only" leaves one.

Say why, not only what

The last step in the tracer did something different from the others. It gave a reason: "so people don't think it's a holiday". Reasons are worth more than they look, because you can never list every case.

Compare two versions of one rule for a customer support reply:

Reply to this customer. Never mention refunds. Customer message: My order arrived with a cracked screen. I'd like to return it. I'm sorry to hear your screen arrived cracked. Please reply with a photo of the damage and your order number, and we'll look into it.

The rule was followed, and the reply dodges the obvious question. The customer asked to return a broken product, and the model had no idea whether that was allowed.

Reply to this customer. Don't promise refunds: only the billing team can approve them, and promises we can't keep make customers angrier. For damaged items, offer a free replacement, which you are allowed to arrange. Customer message: My order arrived with a cracked screen. I'd like to return it. I'm sorry your screen arrived cracked. I can send you a free replacement right away. Just reply with a photo of the damage and your order number. If you'd prefer your money back instead, I'll pass your request to our billing team, who handle refunds.

With the reason, the model understood the goal (don't make promises the team cannot keep) and found a helpful answer inside it. It also handled the refund request honestly, a case the bare rule never covered.

Replace every vague word with something checkable, and give the reason behind each rule. The details tell the model what to do; the reasons tell it what to do in the cases you forgot.

Four habits that make prompts specific

  1. Name the reader. "For a new hire in their first week" sets vocabulary, depth and tone in one line.
  2. Give numbers. Word counts, number of items, reading level, a deadline. Numbers are the easiest instruction to follow and to check.
  3. Describe done. What does a good answer contain? "It should end with one action the reader can take today."
  4. Say what to do when stuck. If the input might be missing something, say how to handle it: "If the date is not in the text, write unknown; do not guess." Without this, the model fills the gap with something plausible, which is how made-up details slip in.

Before sending a prompt, imagine forwarding it to a capable colleague who has never heard of your project. If they would come back with questions, put the answers in the prompt.

Try it yourself

This prompt produces a generic, gushing paragraph. Rewrite it. Replace every vague word with something checkable, name the reader, and give at least one reason.

 Write a good, professional description of our new water bottle. Make it catchy but not too long.

One stronger version:

Write a product description for our online shop. Product: steel water bottle, 750 ml, keeps drinks cold for 24 hours, fits standard car cup holders, dishwasher safe, 3 colours. Reader: people who cycle or commute and are comparing several bottles on their phone. They skim, so put the most useful fact first. Length: 50 to 70 words, then 3 bullet points with the key specs. Tone: plain and confident. No exclamation marks and no words like "revolutionary" or "game-changing", because our customers have told us that kind of language makes them trust a listing less.

Why it works:

  • "Good" and "catchy" became a reader (skimming commuters) and an ordering rule (most useful fact first).
  • "Not too long" became 50 to 70 words plus 3 bullets, which you can check at a glance.
  • The facts are given, so the model does not invent features.
  • The ban on hype words comes with a reason, so the model also avoids hype words you did not list.

Common mistakes

"VERY IMPORTANT: be concise!!" is still vague. Capital letters tell the model you care; they do not tell it what concise means. Write "under 60 words" and drop the shouting. Heavy emphasis can also make a model overcorrect, applying the rule even where it should not.

"Be thorough" and "keep it brief" in the same prompt force the model to pick one, and you cannot predict which. If two goals compete, say which wins: "Keep it under 100 words, even if that means skipping minor points."

A long paragraph of background with the actual task in the last sentence makes the main instruction easy to underweight. State the task near the top, then the context, then the material. If the material is long, such as a whole report, repeat the question once more after it.
