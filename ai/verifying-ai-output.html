Verifying AI Output

A routine for checking AI answers: split a reply into claims, then check facts, numbers, code and citations against something outside the model.

You give an AI assistant your monthly budget notes and ask for a summary. It replies with a tidy breakdown, a total, and a few suggestions. You paste the total into the spreadsheet you share with your partner. It is 10 too low. Every amount in the reply was copied correctly. The addition was wrong.

Nothing in the reply looked off. The sentence with the wrong total sounded exactly as confident as the sentences that were right. That is the reason to verify: you cannot tell from the writing which parts to trust.

#Checking means comparing with something else

Reading a reply again and nodding is not a check. Neither is asking the model "are you sure?", for the reasons in Why Models Hallucinate. A real check compares the reply with something outside it: your original data, a calculation you run, an official document, a test that passes or fails.

The routine has two steps.

  1. Split the reply into claims. A claim is any sentence that could be true or false: a number, a name, a date, "this function handles empty input", "the report says X".
  2. Check each claim the way that suits its type. Numbers get recomputed. Facts get looked up in a primary source. Code gets run. Citations get opened and read.
Key idea

Confidence in the wording tells you nothing about accuracy. Check claims against a source outside the model, and spend the most effort where a mistake would cost the most.

#Numbers: recompute, never re-read

Language models produce numbers the same way they produce words, one token at a time. Simple arithmetic often comes out right, and sometimes it does not. When a number matters, recompute it from the inputs. For more than a couple of values, a few lines of code beat a calculator because you can rerun them.

Recompute the budget claims
const spending = { rent: 950, groceries: 312, transport: 86, phone: 45 };
const pay = 2600;
 
const total = Object.values(spending).reduce((sum, x) => sum + x, 0);
console.log("total:", total); // 1393
for (const [name, amount] of Object.entries(spending)) {
  console.log(name, ((100 * amount) / total).toFixed(1) + "%");
}
// rent 68.2%, groceries 22.4%, transport 6.2%, phone 3.2%
console.log("left over:", pay - total); // 1207
spending = {"rent": 950, "groceries": 312, "transport": 86, "phone": 45}
pay = 2600
 
total = sum(spending.values())
print("total:", total)  # 1393
for name, amount in spending.items():
    print(name, f"{100 * amount / total:.1f}%")
# rent 68.2%, groceries 22.4%, transport 6.2%, phone 3.2%
print("left over:", pay - total)  # 1207

If you ask the assistant to do the maths, ask it to write code like this and run it in a tool, or to show each step so you can check the steps. A number with no working is the hardest kind to trust.

#Facts, code and citations

#Facts

Names, dates, version numbers, laws, prices and quotes need a primary source: the official documentation, the original report, the law itself. A page that repeats the claim is weaker: it may copy the same mistake. For software, check the docs for the version you actually use. A function that existed three versions ago, or only in a different library, is a common wrong answer.

#Code

Run it. Then try the inputs the example did not cover: empty, one item, duplicates, negative numbers, very large values. Coding With AI shows how to turn those cases into tests before you accept the code.

#Citations

A citation is three claims at once: the source exists, it says what the reply says it says, and it is about the same thing. Check all three.

  • Open it. A link that fails to load, or a paper you cannot find by its title, is a red flag.
  • Find the sentence. Search the page for the specific number or phrase. A source on the right topic that does not contain the claim does not support it.
  • Read around it. A quote can be real and still mean the opposite in context, or describe a different year, country or product.
Ask for things that are quick to check

When you write the prompt, ask for exact quotes, page or section numbers, and the working behind any calculation. Checking a quote takes seconds. Checking a vague paraphrase means rereading the whole source.

#How much to check

You do not need to verify a brainstorm of party themes. You do need to verify a figure going to your manager, a dose, a legal deadline or code going to production. Before checking, ask: if this is wrong, who notices, and what does it cost? Then check the claims where the answer is "someone else, and a lot" first.

#Try it yourself

Try it: Plan the checks

An assistant wrote this paragraph for a team update. List each claim, say how you would check it, and say which one you would check first.

Show a sample answer

Six claims hide in three sentences:

  1. 1,240 in May and 1,550 in June: compare with your analytics dashboard, the primary source.
  2. 20% increase: recompute. (1,550 − 1,240) ÷ 1,240 = 310 ÷ 1,240 = 25%, not 20%. This one is wrong.
  3. Most growth came from referrals: check sign-ups by source. This is a claim about cause, and the reply may have guessed it from the launch date.
  4. Launched on June 3: check the release notes or the team calendar.
  5. The guide's quote: open the guide, search for "well above typical", and check that it talks about the same kind of product and the same measure.

Check the percentage first: it takes ten seconds and the whole update leans on it. Then the cause claim, because it will shape what the team does next.

#Common mistakes

Checking the first few items only

Spot-checking the first three rows of a long table and trusting the rest is risky. Errors can sit anywhere, including far down where nobody looked. Check a sample from the middle and the end, or recompute the whole thing in code.

Fixing one error and moving on

In the tracer, one wrong total caused two wrong claims. When you find an error, ask what else depended on it, and recheck those too.

Treating agreement as proof

Asking a second assistant, or the same one again, and getting the same answer is not verification. Both can repeat the same wrong pattern from their training. Agreement makes a claim feel safer without checking it.

#Check yourself

Quick check

A reply says "your three invoices total 4,215". What is the right check?

  1. Not quite. The re-check comes from the same process as the original. It can confirm a wrong number.
  2. Right. Recompute from the inputs. That compares the claim with something outside the model.
  3. Not quite. A total that is off by 10 or 100 looks about right. That is exactly the mistake to catch.

A cited article exists and is about the right topic, but you cannot find the quoted sentence in it. What should you conclude?

  1. Not quite. A real source on the right topic only supports a claim if it actually contains it.
  2. Right. Right. The quote may be invented or paraphrased beyond what the source says.
  3. Not quite. Not necessarily. It may be true and simply not from this source. You just cannot rely on this citation for it.

Which output most needs careful checking before it is used?

  1. Not quite. If one is bad you just pick another. Little is lost.
  2. Right. A wrong date here can cost real money and nobody else may notice until it is too late. Check it against the contract.
  3. Not quite. Worth a read for tone, but you know the facts and can spot changes quickly.

#What to practise next

Take the last long AI reply you used for something real and split it into claims. Check the two that would hurt most if wrong. Then continue with Writing a Good Brief, which shows how to ask for work that is easier to check in the first place.
