Why Models Hallucinate

Why a language model can state false things fluently and confidently, what causes it, and the habits that make it far less likely to hurt you.

You ask an assistant for three studies to cite in a report. It gives you three neat references: authors, year, journal, a one-line finding each. Two check out. The third looks just as real, with a plausible author and a journal that exists, but the paper does not. Nobody wrote it.

This is called a hallucination: output that is fluent and confident but false or unsupported. It is not a rare glitch, and the model is not lying. It follows directly from how a model makes text.

#Plausible is not the same as true

A language model produces text one token at a time, each picked from chances that say what usually comes next (What Is an LLM). Pretraining made those chances match real text, and real text is mostly true, so the model is usually right about things that are written about often.

But nothing in the loop checks facts. In pretraining, the model was rewarded for being a good continuation, not for being true. When it knows a fact well, the true continuation and the plausible one are the same. When it does not, it still produces a plausible one. Every step of the loop must output some token, and there is no separate step that stops when the model is unsure. "I don't know" only comes out if those words are themselves a likely continuation.

Key idea

A model writes what is likely, not what is checked. When it knows a fact, likely and true coincide. When it does not, it still writes something likely, in the same confident tone.

#The main causes

  • Rare facts are stored weakly. A fact seen thousands of times in training gets sharp, confident chances. A fact seen once or twice, like a small company's founding year or a minor paper's author, leaves only a blurry trace. Specific details (names, numbers, dates, quotes, URLs) are the most at risk.
  • Every step commits. Once a wrong token is written, it becomes part of the text the model reads. Later tokens stay consistent with it, so one bad guess can grow into a detailed, coherent false story.
  • Guessing can be rewarded. If training or evaluation scores answers only as right or wrong, a confident guess sometimes scores and "I'm not sure" never does. Researchers point to this as one reason models guess rather than hold back.
  • The answer is not in the text it has. Questions about your private documents, events after its cut-off, or anything it was never told cannot be answered from memory, so a plausible guess is all it can produce.
  • False premises. "Why did the city ban bicycles in 2015?" assumes it happened. A good continuation of that question is an explanation, so the model may supply one.

To be accurate about what is known: research suggests models often carry some internal signal of how sure they are, and for simple facts that signal is partly reliable. Training can teach a model to say "I don't know" more often. But the signal does not reliably show in the wording, and no current method removes hallucinations completely. Treat any specific factual claim as unverified until you check it.

#What actually reduces it

The strongest fix is to stop asking the model to remember, and ask it to read instead. Put the source material in the prompt and ask for answers grounded in it.

The second prompt does three things: it supplies the facts, it asks for a quote you can check in seconds, and it gives the model an honest way out. Systems that look up documents automatically and add them to the prompt use the same idea; see RAG Basics. Letting the model run a search or a calculator through tools helps for the same reason.

The last line of defence is you. Check names, numbers, dates, quotes and links before you use them, as carefully as a mistake would cost. Verifying AI Output turns this into a routine.

#Common mistakes

Asking &quot;Are you sure?&quot; as a check

The reply to "are you sure?" is produced by the same process as the original answer. The model may confirm a wrong answer, or abandon a right one because the question hints that it is wrong. Check against a source instead.

Trusting details because they are specific

A page number, a DOI or a URL feels like evidence, but formats are exactly what the model is best at producing. Specific-looking details are where you should look first, not last.

Turning the temperature down to fix it

As Sampling and Temperature showed, low temperature makes the top choice more likely. If the model does not know the fact, its top choice is still a guess. You get the same wrong answer more consistently.
