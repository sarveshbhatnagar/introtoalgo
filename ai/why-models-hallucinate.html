Why Models Hallucinate

Why a language model can state false things fluently and confidently, what causes it, and the habits that make it far less likely to hurt you.

You ask an assistant for three studies to cite. It gives three neat references with authors, year, journal and finding. Two check out. The third looks just as real, but the paper does not exist.

This is a hallucination: fluent, confident output that is false or unsupported. It is not a rare glitch or a lie; it follows from how a model makes text.

#Plausible is not the same as true

A model writes one token at a time from chances for what usually comes next (What Is an LLM). Pretraining made those chances match real text, which is mostly true, so it is usually right about things written about often.

But nothing checks facts. Pretraining rewarded plausible continuations, not true ones; when the model does not know a fact, it still produces a plausible one. Every step must output some token, sure or not. "I don't know" only comes out if those words are themselves a likely continuation.

#Watch a fake reference being built

A model is asked for a study showing that houseplants improve exam scores, and no such study is well known. The chances are invented to show the pattern.

Key idea

A model writes what is likely, not what is checked. It knew what a reference looks like and filled in the facts like any other blank, in the same confident tone.

#The main causes

  • Rare facts are stored weakly. A fact seen thousands of times gets sharp chances. One seen once or twice, like a small company's founding year, leaves a blurry trace. Names, numbers, dates, quotes and URLs are most at risk.
  • Every step commits. Later tokens stay consistent with a wrong one, so one bad guess can grow into a detailed false story.
  • Guessing can be rewarded. If training or evaluation scores only right or wrong, a confident guess sometimes scores and "I'm not sure" never does.
  • The answer is not in its text. Your private documents, events after its cut-off, anything it was never told: a plausible guess is all it can produce.
  • False premises. "Why did the city ban bicycles in 2015?" assumes it happened. A good continuation is an explanation, so the model may supply one.

Models often carry an internal signal of how sure they are, and training can make "I don't know" more common. But the signal does not reliably show in the wording, and no current method removes hallucinations. Treat specific factual claims as unverified until checked.

#What actually reduces it

The strongest fix: ask the model to read, not remember. Put the source in the prompt and ask for answers grounded in it.

The second prompt supplies the facts, asks for a quote you can check in seconds, and gives an honest way out. RAG systems add documents to the prompt automatically on the same idea, and a search or calculator through tools helps for the same reason.

The last line of defence is you: check names, numbers, dates, quotes and links as carefully as a mistake would cost. Verifying AI Output makes this a routine.

#Common mistakes

Asking &quot;Are you sure?&quot; as a check

The reply comes from the same process as the answer. The model may confirm a wrong answer or drop a right one. Check a source instead.

Trusting details because they are specific

A page number, DOI or URL feels like evidence, but formats are what the model is best at producing. Check specific-looking details first, not last.

Turning the temperature down to fix it

Low temperature makes the top choice more likely. If the model does not know the fact, that choice is still a guess, now repeated consistently.

#Check yourself

Quick check

Which of these is a model most likely to get wrong without a source?

  1. Not quite. It appears in a huge amount of text, so the chances are sharp and correct.
  2. Right. Rare facts leave a weak trace, so the model fills in a plausible year.
  3. Not quite. Formats are common and consistent in training text: a strength.

Why does a hallucinated reference look as convincing as a real one?

  1. Not quite. No intent. One process writes real and invented details.
  2. Right. Format tokens are confident and correct; the facts inside may be guesses, and both look the same.
  3. Not quite. Without tools, a model does not look anything up. It generates text that fits.

  1. Not quite. That does not give it the facts it is missing.
  2. Not quite. That only makes the top guess more consistent, not more correct.
  3. Right. The model reads instead of recalling, and the quote is quick to check.
