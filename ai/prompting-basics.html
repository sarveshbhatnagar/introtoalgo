Prompting Basics

Why vague prompts get generic answers, and the four things every good prompt includes: the task, the context, the shape of the answer, and the input.

You paste a long article into an AI assistant and type "Summarize this." You get back three tidy paragraphs. They are accurate. They are also useless to you, because what you actually needed to know was whether the article changes your team's plans, and the summary spends most of its words on background.

The model did not fail. It did exactly what you asked, and it had to guess everything you did not say. Prompting is the skill of not making it guess.

#A prompt is a brief for a capable stranger

Picture handing the same task to a smart contractor who started today. They write well and know a lot about the world. They know nothing about you: who the summary is for, what decision it feeds, how long it should be, or what you already know.

A language model is in the same position. It has read a huge amount of text, so it is good at producing what a typical answer to your request looks like. If your request is vague, you get the typical answer. If your request carries your situation, you get an answer for your situation.

Key idea

The model only knows what is in the prompt. Anything you leave out, it fills in with the most generic guess.

#The four parts of a good prompt

Most strong prompts contain four things. You do not need headings for them, but you should be able to point at each one.

  1. The task. What to do, as a direct instruction: "Summarize", "Rewrite", "List", "Find the bug in". Put it near the top.
  2. The context. Who it is for, why you need it, and what you already know. "For a decision my team makes next month" changes what counts as important.
  3. The shape of the answer. Length, format, tone, and what to leave out. "5 bullets, no history" is something the model can actually follow.
  4. The input. The material to work on, clearly separated from your instructions.

The fourth part matters more than it looks. If you paste an email into a prompt and the email says "please reply by Friday", the model can confuse that with an instruction from you. Wrap the material in clear markers, like <article> … </article> tags or a line saying "Text to summarize:", so there is no doubt about which words are yours.

Say what to do, not only what to avoid

"Don't be too wordy" leaves the model to guess what "too" means. "Answer in 3 sentences" does not. Turn every "don't" into a "do" where you can.

#Treat the first answer as a draft

Even a good prompt rarely gets a perfect answer on the first try. That is normal. The useful habit is to read the reply, name exactly what is wrong with it, and change the prompt to fix that one thing.

  • Too long? Give a number: "under 120 words".
  • Wrong focus? Say what matters: "I care about the cost, not the timeline".
  • Wrong tone? Say who reads it: "this goes to a customer who is already annoyed".
  • Made something up? Ask it to use only the provided text and say "not stated" when something is missing.

Changing one thing at a time tells you what actually helped. If you rewrite the whole prompt every time, you learn nothing about why the new version worked.

In the real world

Teams that use AI well keep their best prompts in a shared document, the way they keep code snippets. A prompt that reliably produces good release notes or good bug summaries is worth reusing, and worth improving when it slips.

#Common mistakes

Assuming the model knows your situation

It does not know your project, your audience, your earlier conversations in other tools, or what "the usual format" means to you. If it matters, write it down.

Blaming the model before reading your prompt

When a reply is off, reread your prompt as if you were the stranger receiving it. Most of the time, the reply is a reasonable answer to what was actually written.

Mixing instructions into the material

Pasting a document with no separation invites the model to follow instructions that appear inside the document. Mark clearly where your instructions end and the material begins. The lesson on prompt injection shows why this matters for security, too.

#Check yourself

Quick check

You ask for "a summary of this report" and get a generic overview. What is the most useful first change?

  1. Not quite. You may get a different wording, but the model still has to guess what you need, so it will still be generic.
  2. Right. Context tells the model what counts as important. That is the information it was missing.
  3. Not quite. The first summary was probably accurate. The problem was focus, and this line does not say what to focus on.

Which instruction is the model most likely to follow exactly?

  1. Not quite. "Too long" means different things to different readers. The model has to guess.
  2. Not quite. Vague. Brief-ish could be 50 words or 300.
  3. Right. A concrete, positive limit is easy to follow and easy for you to check.

Why wrap pasted material in markers like <article> tags?

  1. Not quite. Models read everything in the prompt. The tags are for clarity, not because they are required.
  2. Right. Right. Without a clear boundary, sentences inside the material can be mistaken for instructions from you.
  3. Not quite. Tags do not affect length. Say the length you want directly.
