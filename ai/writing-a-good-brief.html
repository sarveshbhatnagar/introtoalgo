Writing a Good Brief

How to write a brief for a larger AI task: the goal, the context, the constraints and the definition of done, and what goes wrong when one is missing.

A brief is the written instruction you give an AI assistant for a larger task, such as research, a document or a change across a codebase, where the model makes many decisions without checking with you. A good brief has four parts: the goal, the context, the constraints and the definition of done. It matters because the model cannot ask what you meant, so every question you leave unanswered is settled by a guess.

With a one-line request, the model picks a purpose, an audience, a length and a stopping point for you. If the task takes many steps, each wrong guess shapes the steps after it, and you may only notice when the whole result is wrong. A brief is a prompt (see Prompting Basics) that is written for this situation.

#The four parts of a brief

A useful test for a brief is to ask which questions a capable contractor would ask before starting, and to answer those questions in writing. The four parts below cover most of them.

#Goal

The goal states the outcome you want and what you will use it for. "Decide by Friday whether to revert the new subject-line style" is a goal. "Look into the newsletter numbers" is only a topic. Knowing the purpose lets the model choose between a long report and a one-line verdict, and lets it notice when a detail does not matter for your decision. Put the goal first, so that everything after it is read in light of it.

#Context

The context is whatever the model cannot find out on its own: the data to use, who will read the result, what you have already tried, and what else changed recently. The last item is the one people forget most often. Models tend to blame the only cause they were told about, so a change you leave out of the context is a change they cannot consider. Include only the context the task needs; a long history with no stated goal makes the model guess which parts matter (see Context Engineering).

#Constraints

Constraints say what the model must and must not do: which sources to use, which files it may change, how long the answer may be. Give the reason with each constraint. A rule such as "only change src/billing" covers the case you thought of. A rule with its reason, "because another team is releasing from src/checkout this week", also tells the model what to do in a case you did not think of, which is to report a checkout bug and leave it alone.

#Definition of done

The definition of done says what to hand back, in what form and how long, and what to do if the task cannot be completed. Without it, the model stops after the first plausible result, or keeps going far beyond what you wanted. Always say what a valid failure looks like, such as "say that the data cannot decide". If you do not, the model is pushed to produce a confident answer even when the evidence does not support one.

#Pitfalls

Background with no goal

A long account of the history that ends with "thoughts?" leaves the model to invent the task. State the goal first, then add only the background that the goal needs.

No definition of done

If the brief does not say what to hand back, the model chooses when to stop and what form the result takes. The usual outcome is a long answer to a question you did not ask. Name the output, its size and its format, and say what to do when the task cannot be finished.

Describing every step

A brief that scripts each step stops the model from finding a better route, and it takes you as long to write as doing the work yourself. Fix the outcome and the limits, and spell out the method only where it really matters, such as "run the tests before reporting back".

Leaving out the fact that only you know

People often write the context as what they already told the model, and skip the awkward fact that complicates the story, such as a second change or a known bad data source. Before sending a brief, ask what you know that would change the answer, and write it down.

#What to do in practice

Write the four parts as four short labelled paragraphs, as in the example above. The labels make it obvious when a part is missing, and a model reads them as clearly as a person does. For work that can do damage, such as editing files or sending messages, ask for a plan first and approve it before the model acts (see Coding With AI). A plan costs a minute to read and shows you the model's guesses before they become changes.

When a result comes back wrong, read it as a list of the questions your brief left open and add the missing answers, rather than only asking the model to try again. If the same task will be repeated, keep the improved brief and reuse it.

#Check yourself

Quick check

Which line is a definition of done?

  1. Not quite. It does not say what to hand back, so the model still decides what thorough means.
  2. Right. It names the output, its size and its form, so you and the model both know when it is finished.
  3. Not quite. This is context. It describes the situation and not what the finished result should look like.

In the newsletter brief, which part mattered most for getting a useful answer?

  1. Not quite. A short answer can still blame the wrong change.
  2. Right. Only you knew it. Without it, the model would probably blame the one change it was told about, the subject lines.
  3. Not quite. The brief never said that. It made an honest answer easy by allowing "can't tell" as a valid result.

Why give the reason for a rule such as "do not touch src/checkout"?

  1. Not quite. They usually follow the literal rule. The reason matters for the cases the rule did not foresee.
  2. Right. Knowing that another team is mid-release tells the model to report a checkout bug and not fix it.
  3. Not quite. Length is not the goal. The reason helps because of what it says.

#What to practise next

Before your next long AI task, write the four parts as four short labelled paragraphs and send that instead of a single line. Then continue with Learning With AI, where the goal of the work is your own understanding.
