Writing a Good Brief

Hand a bigger task to an AI the way you would to a contractor: state the goal, the context, the constraints, and what done looks like.

You ask a new colleague to "look into why the newsletter numbers dropped". Three days later they hand you a careful twelve-page report on deliverability, spam filters and industry trends. You wanted one answer: was it the new subject-line style, yes or no?

Your colleague filled every gap with a guess. AI assistants do the same, and over many unattended steps the guesses compound.

#A brief is a prompt for a bigger job

A brief is a prompt (see Prompting Basics) for a bigger job, such as research, a document or a codebase change, where the model makes many decisions without you. It has four parts:

  1. Goal. The outcome you want and why: "decide by Friday whether to revert the subject-line change".
  2. Context. What it cannot find out alone: the data, who the result is for, what you tried, what else changed.
  3. Constraints. What it must and must not do, with the reason: sources, files it may touch, length limits.
  4. Done. What to hand back, in what form, and what to do if the answer is "can't tell".
Key idea

A good brief answers the questions a capable contractor would ask before starting. Every open question becomes a guess.

#Common mistakes

All background, no goal

Paragraphs of history ending in "thoughts?" leave the model to invent the task. Put the goal first, and include only the context the task needs (see Context Engineering).

No definition of done

The model decides when to stop: after the first plausible result, or far past what you wanted.

Dictating every step

Scripting every step can block a better route. Fix the outcome and the limits; spell out the method only where it matters.

#Check yourself

Quick check

Which line is a definition of done?

  1. Not quite. It does not say what to hand back.
  2. Right. It names the output, its size and its form, so you both know when it is finished.
  3. Not quite. Context, not what finished looks like.

In the newsletter brief, which part mattered most?

  1. Not quite. A short answer can still blame the wrong change.
  2. Right. Only you knew it. Without it, the model would likely blame the only change mentioned: the subject lines.
  3. Not quite. The brief never said that. It made honesty easy by offering "can't tell" as a valid answer.

Why give the reason for "do not touch src/checkout"?

  1. Not quite. They usually follow the literal rule. The reason covers cases the rule did not foresee.
  2. Right. Knowing another team is mid-release tells it to report a checkout bug, not fix it.
  3. Not quite. Length is not the goal.

#What to practise next

Before your next long AI task, write the four parts as four short lines. Then continue with Learning With AI, where the goal of the work is your own understanding.
